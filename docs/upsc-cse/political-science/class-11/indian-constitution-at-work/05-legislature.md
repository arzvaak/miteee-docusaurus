## Topic Notes

### Why do we need a Parliament?

The legislature is not just a law-making body; it is the center of democratic political process. It enables citizens to hold representatives accountable, which is the basis of representative democracy. Even strong executives must retain majority support and answer to Parliament. It is the most representative organ, vested with power to choose and dismiss the government.

### Why do we need two Houses of Parliament?

A bicameral legislature (two houses) ensures representation for diverse sections and regions, and provides a double check on every decision by reconsideration in the second house. India’s Parliament consists of Rajya Sabha (Council of States) and Lok Sabha (House of the People). At the state level, only six states have a bicameral legislature (Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh).

### Rajya Sabha: Composition and Powers

Rajya Sabha represents the states. Its members are indirectly elected by elected members of State Legislative Assemblies. Representation is proportional to population (e.g., UP 31 seats, Sikkim 1). It has 12 nominated members from fields of literature, science, art, and social service. Term is 6 years, with one-third retiring every two years; hence it is a permanent house. Special power: Under Article 249, it can pass a resolution to empower Parliament to legislate on a state subject in national interest. It co-equals with Lok Sabha in non-money bills, constitutional amendments, impeachment, and electoral functions, but cannot remove the government or initiate/reject money bills.

### Lok Sabha: Composition and Powers

Lok Sabha is directly elected by people from territorial constituencies (currently 543) through universal adult suffrage. Tenure is 5 years, but can be dissolved earlier. It has exclusive powers over money bills and financial matters, and the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to it. It controls the executive through questions, motions, and the no-confidence motion. It shares equal power with Rajya Sabha in other legislative and constitutional matters.

### Functions of Parliament

Legislative Function: Enacts laws; major bills are introduced by the government. Control of Executive: Ensures accountability through various instruments. Financial Function: Approves taxation, budget, and expenditure. Representation: Voices divergent regional, social, and economic interests. Debating Function: Highest forum of debate without fear. Constituent Function: Amends the Constitution by special majority. Electoral Function: Elects President and Vice President. Judicial Function: Considers removal of President, Vice President, and judges.

### How does Parliament make laws?

A bill is a draft law. Types: Government Bill (minister) and Private Member’s Bill; Money Bill and Ordinary Bill. Money Bills can be introduced only in Lok Sabha; Rajya Sabha can recommend changes within 14 days but cannot reject. Ordinary Bills pass through introduction, committee scrutiny (minipliamentary bodies), debate, and voting in each house. In case of disagreement, a joint sitting is called (Lok Sabha usually prevails). After both houses pass, it goes to the President for assent.

### How does Parliament control the Executive?

In a parliamentary democracy, the executive is drawn from the majority party/coalition. Effective control is exercised through: Deliberation and Discussion: Question Hour (ministers answer), Zero Hour, adjournment motions, etc. Approval or Refusal of Laws: Bills require parliamentary approval; government may need to make concessions. Financial Control: Budget approval; Parliament scrutinizes expenditure and taxation. No Confidence Motion: Lok Sabha can remove the government.

### Parliamentary Privileges

Members have freedom of speech in legislature; no action can be taken for anything said (Article 105 for Parliament, Article 194 for state legislatures). The presiding officer decides on breach of privilege. This enables fearless criticism and control over the executive. Privileges include immunity from civil/criminal proceedings for official acts.

## Mains Answer Practice

### Mains Prompt 1: Parliamentary Privileges

> Discuss the law relating to the powers of Parliament and State Legislatures to punish a person for breach of their privileges.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2017 Law Paper I Q3(b)  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** Introduction: Parliamentary privilege refers to special rights and immunities enjoyed by legislatures and their members to perform their functions freely. Sources: Articles 105 and 194 of the Constitution, conventions, and rules of procedure. Power to punish: Each House can punish individuals for contempt or breach of privilege. Contempt includes disrespect, obstruction, or assault on members. Procedure: A member raises a question of privilege; if admitted, it may be referred to the Committee of Privileges. The committee examines and reports; the House then decides the punishment. Punishments include admonition, reprimand, suspension, or imprisonment (rare and only during session). Limitations: Privileges cannot override fundamental rights completely; in case of conflict, the harmonious construction approach is adopted. In Keshav Singh case (1965), the Supreme Court held that privileges are subject to fundamental rights and judicial review. In Raja Ram Pal case (2007), the Court upheld the power of expulsion but ruled that it must not violate constitutional provisions. Conclusion: While legislatures have inherent powers to punish for breach, these are not absolute and must be exercised in conformity with constitutional principles.
