## Master Summary

Local government is essential for democracy, enabling participation and accountability at the grassroots. The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments (1992-93) gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj Institutions (rural) and Municipalities (urban), mandating regular elections, reservations for SCs/STs and women, and transfer of subjects. Despite progress, challenges remain in fiscal autonomy and effective devolution.

## Topic Notes

### Why Local Governments?

Local government brings governance closer to people, facilitating efficient, cost-effective problem-solving. It ensures active participation and accountability, which are key to democratic decision-making. Examples like Geeta Rathore (Sarpanch) and Vengaivasal Gram Panchayat illustrate how local bodies can effectively protect local interests and drive development.

### Growth of Local Government in India

Self-governing village communities (sabhas, panchayats) existed historically. Modern elected local bodies were initiated by Lord Rippon in 1882. Government of India Acts 1919 and 1935 furthered village panchayats. Gandhi advocated decentralized power through village republics. In the Constituent Assembly, local government was made a State subject under Directive Principles (non-justiciable), with concerns over factionalism and national unity leading to limited initial emphasis.

### 73rd and 74th Amendments: Constitutionalisation

In 1992, Parliament passed the 73rd (rural) and 74th (urban) Constitutional Amendments, effective from 1993. They mandated a three-tier structure, direct elections, reservations for women and SC/ST, transfer of subjects (XI and XII Schedules), and creation of State Election Commissions and State Finance Commissions. These amendments brought uniformity and constitutional backing to local governments across India.

### 73rd Amendment: Panchayati Raj

Highlights: (i) Uniform three-tier PRIs: Gram Panchayat (village), Mandal/Taluka Panchayat (block), Zilla Panchayat (district). (ii) Mandatory Gram Sabha comprising all adult voters. (iii) Direct elections to all tiers; five-year term; if dissolved, elections within six months. (iv) Reservation: one-third seats for women; proportionate reservation for SC/ST; chairperson post also reserved. (v) Transfer of 29 subjects listed in the Eleventh Schedule (agriculture, education, health, etc.) to PRIs, subject to state legislation.

### 74th Amendment: Nagarpalikas

Similar provisions for urban areas: (i) Three types of urban local bodies: Nagar Panchayat (transitional), Municipal Council (smaller town), Municipal Corporation (larger city). (ii) Constitution of wards and direct elections. (iii) Reservations for women, SC/ST, OBC. (iv) Transfer of 18 subjects listed in the Twelfth Schedule (urban planning, water supply, public health, etc.). (v) Independent State Election Commission and Finance Commission applicable to both rural and urban bodies.

### Implementation and Impact

All states have enacted laws; elections held regularly. Over 2.4 lakh Gram Panchayats, 6,000 intermediate panchayats, 600 Zilla Panchayats established. Significant representation: 13 lakh women members, many in leadership roles. Empowerment through reservations has altered social profile, though tensions with dominant groups persist. However, limited fiscal autonomy: local bodies raise only 0.24% of total revenues, heavily dependent on state and central grants. Many states have not fully transferred subjects, limiting effective functioning. Real decentralization and financial devolution remain challenges.

### State Election Commission and State Finance Commission

The State Election Commissioner, appointed by the state, conducts elections to local bodies autonomously (independent of the Election Commission of India). The State Finance Commission, appointed every five years, reviews financial position and recommends distribution of revenues between state and local bodies, and between rural and urban local bodies, ensuring non-political allocation of funds.
