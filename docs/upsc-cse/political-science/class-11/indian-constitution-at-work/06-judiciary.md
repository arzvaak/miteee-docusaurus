## Topic Notes

### Independence of Judiciary

Independence of judiciary means the executive and legislature must not restrain the functioning of the judiciary or interfere with its decisions. Judges must act without fear or favour. It is not arbitrariness; the judiciary is accountable to the Constitution, democratic traditions, and the people. The Indian Constitution ensures independence through: non-interference in appointments (though executive plays a role, the collegium system now exists); fixed tenure and difficult removal procedure; financial independence (salaries charged on Consolidated Fund, not voted by legislature); immunity from criticism (contempt of court power); and prohibition of parliamentary discussion of conduct except during removal proceedings.

### Appointment and Removal of Judges

Appointment of Supreme Court and High Court judges is by the President after consultation with the CJI. A convention of appointing the senior-most judge as CJI was broken in 1973 and 1975. The Supreme Court in the 1990s evolved the collegium system: CJI recommends names in consultation with four senior-most judges, giving the judiciary a decisive voice. Removal is difficult: only for proven misbehaviour or incapacity, requiring a special majority in both Houses (majority of total membership and 2/3 of present and voting). The first and only removal motion against Justice V. Ramaswami failed in 1993 despite an inquiry finding him guilty, because the required majority of total House strength was not met.

### Structure of Judiciary

India has a single integrated judicial system: Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts in states, and district/subordinate courts below. Lower courts are under the superintendence of higher courts. The Supreme Court's decisions are binding on all courts; it can transfer judges and cases, and has all-India jurisdiction.

### Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

Original Jurisdiction: Disputes between Union and states or between states; exclusive authority as a federal umpire. Writ Jurisdiction: Under Article 32, directly enforce fundamental rights through Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto. Appellate Jurisdiction: Hears appeals from High Courts in civil, criminal, and constitutional matters; also special leave petitions under Article 136. Advisory Jurisdiction: President may seek opinion on questions of law or fact of public importance; advice is not binding. The Supreme Court can review its own judgments (Article 137) and its orders are enforceable throughout India (Article 144).

### Judicial Activism and Public Interest Litigation

Judicial activism refers to a proactive role by the judiciary in addressing social issues and enforcing rights. Its main tool is Public Interest Litigation (PIL), which allows any public-spirited citizen to file a case on behalf of aggrieved persons. Started in 1979 with Hussainara Khatoon vs. Bihar (undersigned prisoners), it expanded to include letters and newspaper reports as petitions (Sunil Batra case). PIL has widened the scope of rights (clean air, water, dignified life), enabled access to justice for the poor, and enforced executive accountability (e.g., electoral reforms). Critics argue it overburdens courts, blurs separation of powers, and creates a delicate balance among organs of government.

### Judiciary and Rights

The judiciary protects rights through: (1) writs under Article 32 (SC) and 226 (HC) to remedy violations of fundamental rights; (2) judicial review under Article 13 – the power to strike down unconstitutional laws. Judicial review extends to reviewing legislations for violation of fundamental rights or federal distribution of powers. Though the term is not explicitly mentioned, it is implied by the written Constitution and the Supreme Court's role as its interpreter. PIL and judicial activism have made rights meaningful for disadvantaged sections.
