## Topic Notes

### The Importance of Rights and the Bill of Rights

A democracy must ensure individuals have certain rights and that the government recognizes them. A 'bill of rights' is a list of rights mentioned and protected by the constitution, prohibiting government from acting against individual rights. The Indian Constitution includes a Bill of Rights in Part III, known as Fundamental Rights. These rights protect individuals from state action and provide remedies for violations.

### Fundamental Rights in the Indian Constitution: An Overview

Part III of the Constitution lists Fundamental Rights, which are justiciable and can be enforced by courts. They differ from ordinary legal rights as they are constitutionally guaranteed and can only be amended through constitutional amendment. The state cannot make laws that violate these rights. However, they are not absolute; reasonable restrictions can be imposed.

### Right to Equality (Articles 14-18)

Article 14: Equality before law and equal protection of laws. Article 15: Prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Article 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment; reservation permitted for backward classes (Article 16(4)). Article 17: Abolition of untouchability. Article 18: Abolition of titles (except military and academic). These articles aim to eliminate social inequalities and ensure dignity for all.

### Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22)

Article 19: Six freedoms – speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession, subject to reasonable restrictions. Article 20: Protection in respect of conviction for offences (no ex-post facto laws, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination). Article 21: Protection of life and personal liberty – expanded by Supreme Court to include right to livelihood, shelter, health, etc. Article 21A: Right to free and compulsory education for children aged 6-14 years. Article 22: Protection against arrest and detention; preventive detention allowed with safeguards.

### Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24)

Article 23: Prohibits human trafficking and forced labour (begar); violation punishable. Article 24: Prohibits employment of children below 14 years in hazardous occupations. These rights aim to protect vulnerable sections from exploitation.

### Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28)

India is a secular state with no official religion. Article 25: Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation of religion, subject to public order, morality, and health. Article 26: Freedom to manage religious affairs. Article 27: No forced taxation for promotion of any religion. Article 28: Freedom from religious instruction in state-funded educational institutions. State can regulate secular activities associated with religion and ban social evils like sati.

### Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30)

Article 29: Protection of interests of minorities; right to conserve distinct language, script or culture. Article 30: Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. The state cannot discriminate in granting aid to institutions. These rights preserve the diversity and identity of minorities.

### Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called it the 'heart and soul' of the Constitution. It allows citizens to directly approach the Supreme Court or High Courts for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Courts can issue writs: Habeas Corpus (to produce detained person), Mandamus (to order public duty), Prohibition (to stop inferior court from exceeding jurisdiction), Certiorari (to quash an order), and Quo Warranto (to challenge right to hold office). Article 32 itself is a fundamental right.

### Directive Principles of State Policy: Comparison with Fundamental Rights

Directive Principles (Part IV) are non-justiciable but fundamental in governance. They aim at establishing social and economic democracy. While Fundamental Rights are justiciable and limit state action, DPSPs guide the state in policy-making. The judiciary has harmonized them through various judgments (e.g., Minerva Mills). Key DPSPs include right to work, education, public assistance, equal pay for equal work, and participation of workers in management (added by 42nd Amendment).

## Mains Answer Practice

### Mains Prompt 1: Directive Principles

> “The provisions of the Directive Principles of State Policy are not enforceable by any court, but they are fundamental in the governance of the country.” Critically examine the role of the Government to fulfil the desired objectives enshrined in Part IV of the Constitution.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2021 (Law Paper-I)  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Define DPSPs and their non-justiciable nature.
2. Arguments for DPSPs as fundamental in governance: they guide legislation, provide framework for welfare state, complement Fundamental Rights.
3. Government's role: enactment of laws (e.g., MGNREGA, Right to Education) to implement DPSPs, judicial interpretations harmonising DPSPs and FRs (Kesavananda Bharati, Minerva Mills).
4. Challenges: lack of enforceability leads to slow progress, resource constraints, political will.
5. Conclusion: DPSPs remain crucial as moral and political directives; government efforts are essential to realize the vision of a just society.

### Mains Prompt 2: Article 21

> What are the Rights within the ambit of Article 21 of the Indian Constitution?

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2011 (General Studies Paper 1)  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Article 21 – 'No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.'
2. Judicial expansion of 'life' and 'personal liberty': include right to livelihood (Olga Tellis), right to shelter (Gauri Shankar), right to health (Parmanand Katara), right to education (Unni Krishnan), right to clean environment (Subhash Kumar), right to privacy (Puttaswamy).
3. Procedure established by law – must be fair, just and reasonable (Maneka Gandhi).
4. Conclusion: Article 21 is the source of many unenumerated rights and promotes human dignity.

### Mains Prompt 3: Right to Freedom

> “Right of movement and residence throughout the territory of India are freely available to the Indian citizens, but these rights are not absolute.” Comment.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2022 (General Studies Paper 2)  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Article 19(1)(d) and (e) guarantee freedom of movement and residence.
2. Scope: citizens can move freely and reside in any part of India; essential for national unity and individual liberty.
3. Restrictions under Article 19(5): reasonable restrictions in the interests of general public or for protection of Scheduled Tribes.
4. Examples: restrictions on movement in tribal areas, curfews during emergencies, prohibition of entry into certain areas for security reasons.
5. Conclusion: Rights are not absolute; state can impose reasonable restrictions to balance individual freedom with public interest.
