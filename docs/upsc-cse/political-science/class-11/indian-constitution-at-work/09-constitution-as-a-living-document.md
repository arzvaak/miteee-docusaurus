## Master Summary

The Indian Constitution is a living document that balances rigidity and flexibility through a well-defined amendment process under Article 368. It has been amended 106 times as of 2024, reflecting technical adjustments, judicial-legislative disagreements, and political consensus. The basic structure doctrine, propounded in Kesavananda Bharati, ensures that no amendment can alter the Constitution's fundamental identity. The chapter explains three categories of amendments (simple majority, special majority, and ratification by states), analyses the frequency and types of amendments, and highlights landmark amendments like the 42nd, 44th, 73rd, 74th, and GST-related changes.

## Topic Notes

### Introduction: Constitution as a Living Document

A constitution is not static; it must adapt to changing socio-political needs. India’s Constitution has remained intact since 1950 while undergoing 106 amendments. This balance is achieved through a flexible yet rigid amending procedure, judicial interpretations, and political practices. The framers provided for amendments, ensuring the document evolves without losing its core principles.

### Why Constitutions Need Amendments

Societies change, and constitutions must respond to new challenges. Unlike some nations that rewrite constitutions frequently (e.g., France), India has sustained the same document due to: (a) in-built amendment provisions, (b) far-sighted drafting, and (c) interpretative flexibility by courts and politics. Constitutions are both sacred and adaptable instruments.

### Procedure for Amendment (Article 368)

Article 368 empowers Parliament to amend the Constitution by addition, variation, or repeal. Three categories exist:
1. By simple majority: Matters like admission of new states (Art. 2), alteration of state boundaries (Art. 3), etc. No special procedure needed.
2. By special majority: Most provisions require a majority of the total membership of each House and a two-thirds majority of members present and voting. Both Houses must pass separately; no joint session.
3. By special majority + ratification by states: Provisions affecting federal structure (e.g., distribution of powers, representation of states in Parliament, High Courts, Supreme Court, executive power of the Union/states) require ratification by at least half of the state legislatures by simple majority.
Only elected representatives handle amendments; no referendum or external body is involved, upholding parliamentary sovereignty. The President must give assent.

### Analysis of Amendments (Why So Many?)

Over 69 years (as per chapter), 106 amendments occurred. They can be classified:
- Technical/Administrative: Clarifications, extensions (e.g., SC/ST reservation every 10 years, age of High Court judges increased to 62).
- To override judicial interpretations: Parliament amended provisions when courts gave interpretations divergent from legislative intent, e.g., property rights (1st, 25th, 42nd amendments).
- Political consensus: Post-1984 amendments like anti-defection (52nd), lowering voting age (61st), Panchayati Raj (73rd/74th), reservations (77th, 81st, 82nd) had broad support.
- Controversial amendments: 38th, 39th, 42nd during Emergency (1975-77) were seen as subverting the Constitution. 42nd amended 53 articles, Preamble, and restricted judicial review. Later 43rd and 44th amendments restored the balance.
Amendments occur irrespective of single-party or coalition regimes; they reflect evolving societal and political needs.

### Basic Structure Doctrine

The judiciary, through the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), propounded that Parliament’s amending power under Article 368 does not extend to destroying or altering the ‘basic structure’ of the Constitution. Elements like supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic form of government, secular character, separation of powers, federalism, etc., are part of the basic structure. Any amendment infringing upon this can be struck down by the Supreme Court. This doctrine limits parliamentary sovereignty and ensures the Constitution’s core ideals remain inviolable. The chapter emphasizes how this has shaped the evolution of the Indian Constitution.

### Key Amendments and Their Significance

1st Amendment (1951): Added restrictions on free speech, validated land reforms, added Ninth Schedule.
42nd Amendment (1976): Added ‘socialist’, ‘secular’, ‘integrity’ to Preamble; inserted Fundamental Duties; curtailed judicial review; extended Lok Sabha term to 6 years; gave primacy to Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights.
44th Amendment (1978): Restored much of pre-42nd position; made right to property a legal right; ensured that emergency can be proclaimed only on grounds of armed rebellion.
52nd Amendment (1985): Anti-defection law.
61st Amendment (1988): Reduced voting age to 18 years.
73rd & 74th Amendments (1992): Provided constitutional status to Panchayats and Municipalities.
86th Amendment (2002): Right to education for children aged 6-14 (Article 21A).
101st Amendment (2016): Introduced Goods and Services Tax (GST), reflecting cooperative federalism.
