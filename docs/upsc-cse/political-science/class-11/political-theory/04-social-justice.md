## Master Summary

This chapter explains justice as a principle for arranging social life, distributing benefits and burdens, and treating persons with equal dignity. It moves from the older idea of giving each person their due to modern debates on equal treatment, proportionate rewards, and special needs. It also introduces John Rawls' veil of ignorance as a way to design fair rules without knowing one's own social position. For UPSC, the chapter is useful because it connects ethical reasoning with welfare policy, affirmative action, local government, public distribution, health, education, and the constitutional promise of social and economic justice.

## Answer Skeletons

### 150-word: "Justice requires more than equal treatment."

1. Define justice as fair ordering of social benefits, burdens, rights, and duties.
2. Equal treatment for equals prevents arbitrary discrimination.
3. But proportionate justice recognises relevant differences in work, responsibility, and contribution.
4. Special needs require support for those disadvantaged by disability, deprivation, or historical exclusion.
5. Constitutional link: rights ensure non-arbitrariness; DPSPs and welfare policies pursue social and economic justice.
6. Conclude that justice combines equality, merit, need, and dignity.

### 250-word: "Rawls' veil of ignorance is useful for public policy."

1. Opening: Rawls asks us to design rules without knowing our class, caste, gender, ability, or wealth.
2. This creates impartiality because any rule may later apply to oneself.
3. It supports equal basic liberties and fair opportunity.
4. It also justifies protecting the worst-off because rational persons would guard against ending up disadvantaged.
5. Apply to public policy: health, education, food security, disability access, and emergency relief.
6. Limitation: real policy also needs resources, administrative capacity, and democratic debate.
7. Conclusion: Rawls offers a disciplined method for turning compassion into fair institutional design.

## Topic Notes

### What is Justice?

Justice is concerned with the fair arrangement of social life. It asks who gets what, who bears which burdens, and whether rules respect equal dignity. A just order is not merely one where law exists; it is one where law, policy, and institutions distribute rights, opportunities, and duties by defensible principles.

### Principles of Justice

Equal treatment for equals is the first principle: people with the same relevant features should not be treated differently because of caste, class, gender, race, religion, or status. Proportionate justice adds that rewards and responsibilities may differ when the difference is relevant, such as skill, effort, risk, or public responsibility. Recognition of special needs adds that some people require additional support to enjoy real equality, for example because of disability, age, deprivation, or historical exclusion.

### Just Distribution

Social justice requires fair distribution of goods and services, not only fair words in law. Education, health, nutrition, security, livelihood, and political voice shape whether people can actually use their freedoms. Redistribution becomes relevant where wealth, status, or opportunity are so unequal that ordinary competition simply reproduces inherited advantage.

### Rawls and the Veil of Ignorance

Rawls' method asks us to imagine designing society without knowing our future social position. We do not know whether we will be rich or poor, able-bodied or disabled, dominant or marginalised. Under this veil of ignorance, rational persons would choose rules that protect basic liberties, fair opportunity, and the interests of the worst-off. For UPSC answers, this is useful because it turns impartiality into a decision method.

### Free Market and State Intervention

Markets can reward effort, skill, innovation, and choice, but they also allocate many goods by purchasing power. A person without income may be formally free yet unable to access health, education, or nutrition. State intervention is justified where basic conditions of a dignified life and fair opportunity are absent. A balanced answer should avoid both extremes: markets can create resources, while public institutions must prevent exclusion and secure minimum conditions.

### Mains Prompt 3: Social Justice in the Constitution

> Examine the concept of social justice as envisaged in the Constitution, more particularly in Chapter IV of the Constitution and under Article 14 of the Constitution.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2018, Law I, Q2(a)  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** Introduction: Social justice as a dynamic concept aiming at removal of inequalities; Constitution’s preamble and Part IV (DPSP) reflect it.
Body:
- Chapter IV (DPSP): Articles 38 (social order promoting welfare), 39 (equal pay, livelihood, distribution of resources), 41 (right to work, education), 46 (protection of weaker sections) – all embody social justice.
