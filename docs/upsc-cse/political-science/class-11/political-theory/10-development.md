## Topic Notes

### What is Development?

Development is a normative concept – it implies desirable change. Traditionally equated with economic growth (increase in GDP), it is now recognised as a multi‑dimensional process encompassing health, education, freedom, and equality. The chapter distinguishes between growth‑oriented and human‑development models, emphasising that development must improve the quality of life for all, especially the marginalised.

### Models of Development

1. Economic Growth Model: Focuses on industrialisation, infrastructure, and rising national income. Critics argue it neglects distribution, environment, and non‑material well‑being.
2. Human Development Model: Inspired by Amartya Sen and Mahbub ul Haq, it values capabilities, health, education, and political freedom (reflected in the Human Development Index).
3. Sustainable Development: Defined as ‘development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs’. It integrates environmental protection with social and economic progress.

### Critiques of the Development Model

Large‑scale development projects (dams, mines, industries) often displace local communities, particularly tribals and farmers, without adequate rehabilitation. They cause environmental degradation, loss of livelihoods, and cultural erosion. Movements like Narmada Bachao Andolan and Chipko have challenged such models. The chapter questions whether development that destroys nature and communities can genuinely be called ‘development’.

### Sustainable Development and Environmental Ethics

The chapter stresses that environmental degradation is not an inevitable cost of development. It advocates for eco‑friendly technologies, public participation, and a shift from mindless consumerism to ‘decent living’. International agreements like the Rio Summit (1992) and the Johannesburg Summit (2002) are mentioned as efforts to institutionalise sustainable development.

### Displacement, Rights, and Rehabilitation

Displacement caused by development projects violates basic rights. The chapter argues for a rights‑based approach where affected people are partners in development, not victims. Rehabilitation must ensure restoration of livelihoods, housing, and community networks. State policies should balance national development with the protection of vulnerable sections.

- Understanding the issue: Historical marginalisation, loss of land‑livelihood, alienation of tribals in Malkangiri and Naxalbari. 
- Strategies : 
  1. Inclusive development: Participatory planning, consent of Gram Sabhas under PESA, rehabilitation before displacement. 
  2. Land rights: Implement Forest Rights Act properly; halt land acquisition for non‑essential projects.

  7. Dialogue and trust building: Engagement with civil society, addressing grievances. 
Conclusion: A holistic, rights‑based approach that treats development as an instrument of inclusion, not exclusion, is essential to counter LWE.
