## Topic Notes

### 1. Understanding Peace: Negative vs. Positive Peace

Peace is traditionally understood as the absence of war or conflict (negative peace). However, modern peace research emphasizes **positive peace**, which includes the absence of structural violence, presence of social justice, equality, and harmony. Johan Galtung’s framework distinguishes between direct violence (personal harm) and structural violence (institutionalized inequality) that prevents human fulfillment.

### 2. Forms of Violence: Direct and Structural

Direct violence includes physical aggression, war, and terrorism. Structural violence is embedded in social, political, and economic systems, manifested as poverty, discrimination, and oppression. Cultural violence legitimizes direct and structural violence through norms and ideologies. Understanding these forms is crucial for comprehensive peace-building.

### 3. Causes of Conflict and Approaches to Peace

Conflict arises from resource scarcity, identity assertions, ideological clashes, and unjust systems. Approaches to peace include conflict resolution (addressing root causes), conflict management (containing violence), and peacebuilding (creating sustainable structures). Peace processes may involve negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and reconciliation.

### 4. Gandhian Perspective on Peace and Non-violence

Mahatma Gandhi advocated **Ahimsa** (non-violence) and **Satyagraha** (truth-force) as powerful means for social and political change. For Gandhi, peace was not passive but active resistance to injustice without hatred. His methods included civil disobedience, non-cooperation, and fasting as moral pressure. Gandhi’s vision linked peace with economic self-reliance and communal harmony.

### 5. Role of United Nations in Peacekeeping

The UN, through its Security Council, deploys peacekeeping forces to conflict zones under Chapter VI (pacific settlement) and Chapter VII (enforcement actions). Peacekeeping operations have evolved from monitoring ceasefires to multi-dimensional missions including disarmament, demobilization, reintegration (DDR), electoral support, and reconstruction. India is a major contributor to UN peacekeeping.

### 6. Contemporary Challenges to Peace

The 21st century faces new threats: global terrorism, nuclear proliferation, cyber warfare, climate change, and pandemics. Arms races, particularly in nuclear weapons, pose existential risks. Economic inequalities and resource conflicts exacerbate tensions. Gender-based violence is increasingly recognized as a peace and security issue (UNSCR 1325). Achieving peace requires collective action, disarmament, sustainable development, and promotion of human rights.

## Conceptual Deepening

Peace should be treated as a process, not a static condition. A society may have no open war and still be unpeaceful if large groups face hunger, fear, discrimination, displacement, or humiliation. This is why the positive peace idea matters for UPSC: it allows you to connect peace with justice, rights, inclusive development, rule of law, and trust in institutions.

Three layers help in answers:

1. **Negative peace:** stopping direct violence.
2. **Positive peace:** removing structural causes of violence.
3. **Just peace:** ensuring that peace does not merely silence victims but restores dignity, accountability, and reconciliation.

## Gandhian Peace

Gandhi's idea of peace was not passive avoidance of conflict. It was active resistance to injustice through truth, non-violence, self-discipline, and willingness to suffer without hatred. For UPSC, do not reduce Gandhi to "peace lover." His method was politically confrontational but ethically non-violent.

- **Ahimsa:** Avoiding physical and mental harm; rooted in respect for dignity.
- **Satyagraha:** Truth-force; resistance based on moral courage.
- **Means and ends:** Unjust means cannot produce a just peace.
- **Constructive programme:** Peace requires social reconstruction, not only protest.
- **Communal harmony:** Peace requires inter-community trust.
- **Swaraj:** Self-rule includes ethical self-restraint, not merely transfer of power.

## UN and International Peace

- **UN Charter logic:** Prohibits aggressive war and encourages peaceful settlement of disputes.
- **Security Council:** Primary responsibility for international peace and security.
- **Peacekeeping:** Not explicitly named in the Charter, but evolved as a practical instrument.
- **Peace enforcement:** Stronger coercive action may be authorised in severe threats.
- **Peacebuilding Commission:** Supports recovery from conflict and institution building.
- **India's role:** India has been a major contributor to UN peacekeeping and uses it as a marker of responsible global citizenship.

Use the UN with balance: it gives legitimacy and coordination, but veto politics, funding gaps, weak mandates, and consent problems limit effectiveness.

## Indian Constitutional and Policy Linkages

- **Article 51:** Directs the State to promote international peace and security, maintain just and honourable relations, respect international law, and encourage arbitration.
- **Preamble:** Justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity provide the domestic foundation of positive peace.
- **Fundamental Rights:** Protect dignity and reduce state violence when enforced properly.
- **Directive Principles:** Social and economic justice reduce structural violence.
- **Federalism and local self-government:** Democratic accommodation can reduce internal conflict.
- **Non-alignment and strategic autonomy:** Historically linked India's foreign policy to peace, decolonisation, and independent judgment.
- **Panchsheel:** Mutual respect, non-aggression, non-interference, equality, and peaceful coexistence.

## Examples and Case Studies

- **India's freedom movement:** Satyagraha shows non-violent mass politics against injustice.
- **Northern Ireland peace process:** Negotiation, power-sharing, and institutional guarantees helped reduce long conflict.
- **South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission:** Shows restorative justice after systemic violence.
- **UN peacekeeping in Congo:** Useful for India's role in multidimensional missions.
- **Climate-related resource stress:** Shows how environmental insecurity can become a peace issue.
- **Domestic violence and gender justice:** Shows that peace is not only an international concept; the home can be a site of violence.
- **Nuclear deterrence:** Can prevent direct war but creates ethical and existential risks.

## Answer Templates

**Template 1: "Peace as positive peace."**

Peace is not merely the absence of open violence. Negative peace may stop war, but positive peace requires justice, rights, dignity, social trust, and institutions that prevent future violence. For example, a ceasefire may reduce direct harm, but lasting peace requires rehabilitation, accountability, inclusive development, and reconciliation. Therefore, democratic governance must address both immediate security and structural causes such as poverty, discrimination, displacement, and hate.

**Template 2: "Ethics of non-violence."**

Non-violence is not passive submission. In the Gandhian sense, it is active resistance to injustice through truth, moral courage, self-restraint, and refusal to dehumanise the opponent. It protects the ethical link between means and ends: violent methods may defeat an enemy but can reproduce fear and domination. In public administration, this translates into dialogue, proportionate force, trust-building, protection of vulnerable groups, and commitment to constitutional values.

**Template 3: "Conflict resolution answer close."**

The way forward must combine negative peace and positive peace: immediate cessation of violence, impartial enforcement of law, humanitarian relief, dialogue with stakeholders, rehabilitation, institutional reform, and long-term justice. Peace becomes sustainable only when citizens see the state as fair, rights-respecting, and capable of resolving grievances without repression.

  1. Primacy of human rights and peace over profit: reject arms trade that fuels conflict.

### Mains Prompt 2: Ethical dilemmas in gender justice vs cultural resistance

> You are the Sub-Divisional Magistrate (SDM) posted in a culturally conservative town that has witnessed a rise in cases of domestic violence, harassment, and gender-based crimes. A dedicated all-women police station is being constructed, but local religious leaders oppose it, claiming it will disrupt family harmony. Women’s activists support it. Junior staff suggest postponing due to political pressure. (a) What are the ethical dilemmas and governance issues presented in this case? (b) As an SDM, how will you balance gender reforms with local cultural resistance, ensure inclusive decision-making, and uphold constitutional values while maintaining law and order?

**Source:** upsc-cse-mains-2015-general-studies-paper-4-0e7c5d7009  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** (a) Ethical dilemmas and governance issues:
- Dilemmas: Gender justice vs. cultural sensitivity; short-term peace (avoiding protests) vs. long-term constitutional mandate; professional integrity vs. political pressure.
- Governance issues: Law and order management, balancing stakeholder interests, maintaining public trust, inter-departmental coordination.

(b) Balancing approach:
- Hold inclusive dialogue with all stakeholders to address misconceptions and highlight legal provisions.
- Emphasize constitutional values (Articles 14, 15, 51A) and the police station’s role in protecting all genders.
- Implement confidence-building measures like community policing and involve women from the local community.
- Do not postpone; uphold rule of law while managing dissent through persuasion and visible security.
- Ensure the initiative is framed as a community welfare measure, not an adversarial move against tradition.
