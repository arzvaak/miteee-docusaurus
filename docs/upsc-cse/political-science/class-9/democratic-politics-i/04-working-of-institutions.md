## Topic Notes

### Introduction

In a democracy, governance is carried out through three key institutions: the Legislature, the Executive, and the Judiciary. This chapter examines their composition, functions, and mutual checks and balances, using the example of the passage of a law to illustrate institutional interplay.

### Parliament

Parliament is the supreme legislative body. It is bicameral, consisting of the Rajya Sabha (Council of States) and the Lok Sabha (House of the People). The Lok Sabha is directly elected, while Rajya Sabha members are indirectly elected. Rajya Sabha is a permanent house, whereas Lok Sabha has a term of five years unless dissolved earlier. Key functions: making laws, controlling the executive through questions and motions, financial oversight (budget), amending the Constitution, and representing the people.

### Political Executive

The political executive includes the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers. In the parliamentary system, the Prime Minister is the head of government. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Major appointments: Prime Minister appointed by President; other ministers on PM's advice. The PM chairs cabinet meetings, coordinates departments, and is the link between President and Cabinet.

### Permanent Executive

The permanent executive or bureaucracy consists of civil servants who implement policies. They remain in office regardless of government changes, providing continuity. They are selected through competitive exams and are meant to be politically neutral.

### The President

The President is the nominal head of state. Executive power is vested in the President but exercised on the advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74). The President has discretionary powers in certain situations, e.g., appointing a PM when no clear majority exists. The President can also return a bill for reconsideration, but if passed again, must give assent.

### Judiciary

The judiciary is independent and integrated. At the apex is the Supreme Court, followed by High Courts and subordinate courts. It adjudicates disputes, protects fundamental rights (writs under Article 32), and can declare laws unconstitutional (judicial review). Judges are appointed by the President and can be removed only through impeachment. Independence is ensured through security of tenure, fixed salaries, and difficult removal process.

### Inter-Institutional Balance

The three organs function with checks and balances: Parliament makes laws but the judiciary can review them; the executive implements laws but is answerable to Parliament; the judiciary upholds the Constitution. This system prevents concentration of power and ensures accountability.

### Mains Prompt 3: UPSC 2021 General Studies Paper II Q4

> To what extent, in your view, the Parliament is able to ensure accountability of the executive in India?

**Source:** upsc-cse-mains-2021-general-studies-paper-ii-e42fa31c7a  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Parliamentary democracy envisages executive accountability to the legislature through various mechanisms. 2. Instruments of accountability: Question Hour, debates, parliamentary committees (Public Accounts Committee, Departmentally Related Standing Committees), motions (adjournment, no-confidence), budget control. 3. Effectiveness: Committees scrutinize policies in detail; media coverage highlights issues; ministers can be censured. 4. Limitations: Frequent disruptions reduce time for scrutiny; anti-defection law weakens individual MP's role; lack of adequate research support; delegated legislation bypasses parliamentary oversight. 5. Comparison: Unlike presidential systems, the fusion of executive and legislature blurs accountability; coalition era further dilutes. 6. Conclusion: Parliament can ensure accountability but needs reforms like strengthening committees, ensuring no-confidence motions are not trivialized, and promoting meaningful debate.

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Article 53(1) vests the executive power of the Union in the President, but Article 74(1) mandates that the President shall act on the advice of the Council of Ministers. 2. Nominal vs. real executive: In parliamentary system, President is the constitutional head; real power lies with the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers. 3. Critique of the statement: While largely ceremonial, the President possesses certain discretionary powers (e.g., appointment of PM when no clear majority, assent to bills, dissolving Lok Sabha, and, in rare cases, returning a bill for reconsideration). Conclusion: The statement is partly true, but the vesting clause retains symbolic and reserve powers essential for constitutional balance.
