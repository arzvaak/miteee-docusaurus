## Topic Notes

### Introduction

The chapter explores the expansion of democracy over the last century, noting that more than half of independent countries are now democracies. It uses real-life stories to illustrate the fragility and resilience of democratic systems.

### Two Tales of Democracy: Chile

Chile under President Salvador Allende (1970–73) saw socialist policies favouring the poor. His government was overthrown by a military coup led by General Augusto Pinochet on 11 September 1973, with reported U.S. support. Pinochet's 17-year dictatorship involved widespread human rights abuses. Democracy was restored after a 1988 referendum rejected Pinochet's rule. Michelle Bachelet, a former political prisoner, became President in 2006, symbolizing Chile's democratic recovery.

### Two Tales of Democracy: Poland

In 1980, the communist government in Poland faced a strike at the Lenin Shipyard led by Lech Walesa. The strike gave rise to Solidarity, the first independent trade union in a communist state. Despite martial law in 1981, the movement persisted, leading to partially free elections in 1989 and Walesa's presidency in 1990. This marked Poland's transition from a non-democratic to democratic system.

### Common Features of Democracy

From these stories, democracy is identified as a form of government where: (1) only leaders elected by the people rule the country, and (2) people have freedoms of expression, association, and protest. Non-democratic regimes, whether military or one-party, lack these features.

### The Changing Map of Democracy (1900–2000)

Maps show democracy's expansion throughout the 20th century. In 1900, only a few countries (mostly in Europe and the Americas) were democratic. By 1950, more countries became democratic, but Africa and Asia remained largely non-democratic. By 2000, a majority of countries had democratic governments, though parts of the Middle East, China, and some Asian and African nations remained non-democratic. The expansion was uneven across regions.

### Phases in the Expansion of Democracy

1. **Early Phase (pre-20th century):** Democracy began with the French Revolution and US independence, but initially with limited suffrage. Universal adult franchise was achieved gradually (e.g., New Zealand 1893, Britain 1928, US 1965).
2. **End of Colonialism (post-WWII):** Many Asian and African colonies became democracies after independence, but few sustained it (e.g., Ghana's democracy fell to a coup).
3. **Recent Phase (post-1980):** A third wave of democratization saw transitions in Latin America, the collapse of the Soviet Union (1991), and democratization in Eastern Europe. However, setbacks occurred in Pakistan, Nepal, and Myanmar. By 2016, about 140 countries held multi-party elections.

### Democracy at the Global Level?

The chapter briefly raises the question of whether international institutions like the UN are democratic. It notes that the UN Security Council’s structure (permanent members with veto power) reflects power politics rather than democratic equality among nations, indicating that global governance still lacks democratic principles.
