## Master Summary

This chapter examines three social divisions—gender, religion, and caste—and their expression in Indian politics. Gender division is rooted in social stereotypes leading to patriarchy; women face discrimination in literacy, work, and safety, but feminist movements and political representation (e.g., reservations) have advanced their status. Religion-based communalism, when politics becomes exclusive and partisan, threatens democracy; India's secular state provides no official religion, ensures religious freedom, and allows state intervention against discrimination. Caste, a uniquely Indian hierarchy based on hereditary occupation and untouchability, is changing through socio-economic developments, yet persists in political mobilization, vote banks, and affirmative action. The chapter argues that while some expression of social differences is healthy, divisive communal or casteist politics must be combated.

## Topic Notes

### Gender and Politics

Gender division is a hierarchical social division based on social expectations and stereotypes, not biology. A sexual division of labour assigns women primarily to unpaid domestic work, leading to their invisibility in public life and politics. Feminist movements organized for equal rights, enhancing women's political and legal status. In India, despite constitutional guarantees, women face disadvantages: lower literacy (54% female vs. 76% male), declining child sex ratio (919 in 2011), unequal pay (Equal Remuneration Act, 1976), and violence. Political representation of women is low (14.36% in Lok Sabha in 2019, less than 5% in state assemblies). The Panchayati Raj system reserves one-third of seats for women, and the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women’s Reservation Act, 2023) provides 33% reservation in Parliament and Assemblies. Political expression of gender issues benefits disadvantaged groups.

### Religion, Communalism, and Politics

Religion can legitimately influence politics when it promotes ethical values (e.g., Gandhiji’s moral politics) or addresses discrimination (e.g., reforming family laws). The problem, communalism, arises when religion becomes the basis of national identity and is used in exclusive, partisan ways. Communalism takes forms: everyday prejudice, majoritarian dominance, political mobilization using sacred symbols, and communal violence (e.g., Partition riots). India’s secular state has no official religion; the Constitution guarantees freedom to profess, practice, and propagate any religion; prohibits religious discrimination; and allows state intervention to ensure equality (e.g., banning untouchability). Secularism is a foundational principle that must be defended through combating communal propaganda and mobilization.

### Caste and Politics

Caste division is an extreme form of hereditary occupational hierarchy sanctioned by rituals and based on exclusion and untouchability. Social reformers (Jotiba Phule, Ambedkar, Periyar) challenged it. Modern factors—urbanization, education, occupational mobility, and legal prohibition of discrimination—have weakened caste hierarchy, but its effects persist: caste remains linked to economic status, and upper castes dominate the urban middle class. SCs (16.6%), STs (8.6%), and OBCs (~41%) together constitute two-thirds of the population. Politics uses caste: parties nominate candidates based on caste arithmetic, appeal to caste sentiments, and rely on universal franchise to mobilize caste groups. Caste can be both a divisive force and a means for marginalized groups to demand representation and voice.

## Mains Answer Practice

### Mains Prompt 1: Mains 2018 PSIR Paper I

> Discuss Ambedkar's ideas on 'annihilation of caste'.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2018 Political Science and IR Paper I, Q2(b)  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** Introduction: Ambedkar’s critique of caste as a graded hierarchy that denies equality and social fraternity. Main Body: (1) Rejection of divine sanction—caste is a human-made system preserved through endogamy and social boycott. (2) Annihilation, not reform—mere removal of untouchability is insufficient; the entire hierarchical structure must be dismantled. (3) Means—inter-caste marriage, inter-dining, and destruction of religious scriptures that justify caste. (4) Political and legal remedies—reservation for depressed classes, representation in legislatures, and constitutional safeguards. (5) Relation to Hindu social order—caste cannot be eradicated without transforming the religious foundation of society. Conclusion: Ambedkar’s vision remains relevant; his ideas frame contemporary debates on social justice and caste annihilation.
