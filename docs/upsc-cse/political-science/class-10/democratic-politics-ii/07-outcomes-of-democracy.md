## Master Summary

Democracy is a form of government that creates conditions for citizens to achieve their goals. It is assessed on outcomes like accountability, responsiveness, legitimacy, economic growth, reduction of inequality, accommodation of social diversity, and promotion of dignity and freedom. While democracies outperform autocracies in political legitimacy and dignity, they face challenges in corruption, economic inequality, and conflict resolution. However, the ability of citizens to criticize democracy itself is a measure of its success.

## Key Concepts

- Democracy as a form of government, not a panacea for all problems.
- Accountability, responsiveness, and legitimacy as core political outcomes.
- No guarantee of higher economic growth; negligible difference with dictatorships.
- Persistent economic inequality despite political equality.
- Accommodation of social diversity through negotiation and majority-minority respect.
- Promotion of individual dignity and freedom, especially for marginalized groups.
- Public dissatisfaction as a sign of democratic maturity and citizen awareness.

## Topic Notes

### How to Assess Democracy’s Outcomes?

Democracy is a form of government, not a solution to all socio-economic problems. It creates conditions for achievement; citizens must use those conditions. Assessment should be based on expected outcomes like equality, dignity, improved decision-making, conflict resolution, and error correction. There is a dilemma: democracy is good in principle but often unsatisfying in practice. This demands careful evaluation of actual records against reasonable expectations.

### Accountable, Responsive and Legitimate Government

Democracy must produce a government accountable to citizens and responsive to their needs. It involves deliberation and negotiation, causing delays but ensuring decisions are more acceptable and effective. Transparency (right to information, open debate) is a hallmark. In practice, democracies have mixed records: free elections but not always fair; public debate exists but not for every decision; corruption remains a concern. However, democratic government is legitimate – people’s own government. This legitimacy generates overwhelming support globally, even in non-democracies.

### Economic Growth and Development

Evidence (1950–2000) shows dictatorships had slightly higher average economic growth than democracies, but in poor countries, the difference is negligible. Economic development depends on many factors (population, global situation, priorities). Democracy is not a guarantee of development, but it shouldn’t lag behind dictatorships. Other positive outcomes justify preferring democracy. Tables in the chapter show that internal inequalities can be high even in democracies (e.g., South Africa, Brazil) compared to egalitarian ones (Denmark).

### Reduction of Inequality and Poverty

Political equality coexists with growing economic inequality. The ultra-rich hold a disproportionate share of wealth, while the poor struggle for basics. Despite the poor being a majority of voters, democratically elected governments often fail to address poverty adequately. In Bangladesh, over half the population lives in poverty. Democracies have not been very successful in reducing economic disparities, but they provide a platform for the poor to voice concerns.

### Accommodation of Social Diversity

Democracy is best suited to handle social differences through competition and negotiation, reducing violence. Two conditions are necessary: (1) majority rule must not become permanent community rule; decisions and elections should allow changing majorities; (2) every citizen must have a chance to be in the majority at some point. Non-democracies often suppress differences, while democracies can fail if they exclude groups (e.g., Sri Lanka's ethnic conflict).

### Dignity and Freedom of the Citizens

Democracy promotes individual dignity and freedom, recognizing the equal worth of all. Long struggles by women and disadvantaged castes have gained moral and legal force under democratic principles. Though inequalities persist, they lack legitimacy. The right to criticize and complain is evidence of democratic success: it transforms subjects into citizens. Belief in the efficacy of vote is widespread, showing that people feel their voice matters.
