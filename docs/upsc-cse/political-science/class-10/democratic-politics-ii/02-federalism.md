## Master Summary

This chapter explains federalism as a system of government with divided powers between central and state governments. It outlines key features, contrasts 'coming together' and 'holding together' federations, and examines India's federal structure, including the three-fold legislative distribution, special provisions for certain states, and the role of the judiciary. The chapter discusses how federalism is practiced through linguistic states, language policy, and evolving centre-state relations, especially post-1990 coalition politics. Finally, it covers decentralisation to local governments through the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments, describing Panchayati Raj and urban local bodies.

## Topic Notes

### What is Federalism?

Federalism is a system of government where power is divided between a central authority and constituent units. It typically has two levels of government, each with its own independent jurisdiction. The dual objectives are to safeguard national unity while accommodating regional diversity. It requires mutual trust and agreed rules of power-sharing. In contrast, a unitary system either has a single level of government or subordinate sub-units.

### Key Features of Federalism

1. Two or more levels of government.
2. Each tier has its own jurisdiction in legislative, taxation, and administrative matters.
3. Jurisdictions are constitutionally guaranteed.
4. Fundamental provisions cannot be unilaterally changed; consent of both levels is needed.
5. Courts interpret the constitution and arbitrate disputes.
6. Revenue sources are clearly specified for financial autonomy.

### Types of Federations

‘Coming together’ federations (e.g., USA, Australia) are formed by independent states pooling sovereignty. States usually have equal power. ‘Holding together’ federations (e.g., India, Spain) involve a large country dividing power between national and state governments, where the centre tends to be more powerful and constituent units may have unequal powers.

### What Makes India a Federal Country?

The Constitution declares India a ‘Union of States’. It provides a two-tier system (Union and States), later adding a third tier (local governments). Legislative powers are divided into three lists:
- Union List: subjects of national importance (defence, foreign affairs, currency)
- State List: police, trade, agriculture
- Concurrent List: education, forest, marriage (both can legislate; Union law prevails in conflict)
Residuary powers (subjects not listed) belong to the Union.
Some states (Assam, Nagaland, etc.) have special provisions under Article 371 for cultural protection and land rights. Union Territories like Chandigarh and Delhi have limited power. Changes to the power-sharing arrangement require a two-thirds parliamentary majority and ratification by half the state legislatures. The judiciary ensures implementation, and both levels have fiscal autonomy.

### How is Federalism Practised?

Success of Indian federalism depends on democratic politics, not just constitutional provisions. 
- Linguistic States: States were reorganized on linguistic lines after independence. This improved unity and administration, contrary to early fears of disintegration.
- Language Policy: No national language; Hindi is official. 22 Scheduled Languages recognized. English continued for official use alongside Hindi after protests from non-Hindi states, especially Tamil Nadu.
- Centre-State Relations: Post-1990, rise of regional parties and coalition governments strengthened state autonomy. The Supreme Court’s judgment made arbitrary dismissal of state governments difficult.

### Decentralisation in India

Local government is the third tier. The 1992 Constitutional Amendments made it mandatory to hold regular elections, reserve seats for SCs/STs/OBCs and women (one-third), and establish State Election Commissions. State governments must share revenue and powers.
- Rural: Gram Panchayat (village level), Panchayat Samiti (block), Zilla Parishad (district). Gram Sabha (village assembly) approves budgets and reviews performance.
- Urban: Municipalities for towns, Municipal Corporations for big cities. Elected representatives and mayors govern.

## Prelims Traps

- Do not treat NCERT examples as exhaustive lists.
- Watch qualifiers such as only, always, never, directly, indirectly, constitutional, statutory, formal, and informal.
- "Federal" does not mean equal power for all units in India; unequal representation and special provisions are compatible with Indian federalism.
- "Union of States" does not mean states have a right to secede.
- "Hindi is the official language" is not the same as "Hindi is the national language."
- "Local government" is constitutionally recognised, but the extent of devolution still depends heavily on States.
- "Concurrent List" does not mean State law always prevails; Union law prevails in a conflict.
