## Master Summary

The chapter 'The Crisis of Democratic Order' examines the political and constitutional turmoil that led to the declaration of Emergency in India in 1975. It details the economic distress, mass agitations in Gujarat and Bihar, the Naxalite movement, and the executive-judiciary conflict, all of which contributed to a sense of crisis. The chapter explains how Prime Minister Indira Gandhi’s response—declaring Emergency under Article 352—suspended democratic rights, enabled press censorship, and facilitated extensive use of preventive detention. It also discusses the controversial 42nd Amendment and the Shah Commission’s findings of excesses. The chapter concludes with debates on whether the Emergency was necessary and its enduring lessons for Indian democracy.

## Topic Notes

### Background to the Emergency

After 1967, Indian politics became increasingly polarised. Indira Gandhi’s popularity peaked after the 1971 war, but economic problems—high inflation (23% in 1973, 30% in 1974), industrial stagnation, unemployment, and failed monsoons—created widespread discontent. The 'Garibi Hatao' slogan remained unfulfilled. The Congress faced allegations of personalisation of power and confrontation with the judiciary over fundamental rights and parliamentary supremacy.

### The Naxalite Movement

Originating in Naxalbari (West Bengal) in 1967, the peasant uprising led by CPI(M) cadres evolved into the Communist Party (Marxist-Leninist) under Charu Majumdar. It advocated armed guerrilla warfare against the state, targeting rich landowners. The movement spread to many states, often in Adivasi areas facing exploitation. While some factions later joined democratic politics, the movement continues in several districts, posing internal security challenges.

### Gujarat and Bihar Movements

In January 1974, students in Gujarat protested against corruption and price rise, eventually forcing President’s rule and fresh elections (June 1975) where Congress was defeated. In Bihar (March 1974), students invited Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) to lead a non‑violent agitation. JP demanded dismissal of the Congress government and gave a call for 'Sampoorna Kranti' (Total Revolution). The movement attracted national support from non‑Congress parties, projecting JP as an alternative to Indira Gandhi. A nationwide railway strike in 1974 added to the unrest.

### Conflict with Judiciary

Three constitutional issues strained executive‑judiciary relations: (1) Can Parliament abridge Fundamental Rights? (2) Can it curtail the right to property via amendment? (3) Could it override Fundamental Rights to implement Directive Principles? The Supreme Court ruled against the government in all three, culminating in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), which laid down the basic structure doctrine. The government’s supersession of three senior judges to appoint Justice A. N. Ray as Chief Justice of India further politicised the judiciary.

### Declaration of Emergency

On 12 June 1975, the Allahabad High Court invalidated Indira Gandhi’s election over corrupt practices. The opposition intensified protests; JP called for a nationwide satyagraha and asked the armed forces to disobey 'illegal orders'. On 25 June 1975, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed, on the Prime Minister’s advice, proclaimed an Emergency under Article 352 on grounds of 'internal disturbance'. Fundamental Rights stood suspended, press censorship was imposed, and opposition leaders were arrested under preventive detention laws.

### Consequences of the Emergency

The Emergency centralised power in the Union government, suspended democratic rights, and saw widespread arrests. The press was censored; many journalists were jailed. The 42nd Amendment (1976) brought sweeping constitutional changes: extended the Lok Sabha term from 5 to 6 years, insulated the Prime Minister’s election from judicial review, and weakened federalism and fundamental rights. The Shah Commission later exposed 'excesses' such as forced sterilisations and slum demolitions.

### Controversies and Lessons

Supporters argued the Emergency was needed to curb extra‑parliamentary agitations and restore order; the CPI initially backed it, citing an international conspiracy. Critics, including JP, held that protests were peaceful and the government had routine powers to handle them. The real threat was to Indira Gandhi’s personal power. The Emergency demonstrated how constitutional provisions could be misused, but also showed the resilience of democratic forces: the Janata Party’s victory in 1977 reversed many 42nd Amendment provisions and restored faith in democracy.
