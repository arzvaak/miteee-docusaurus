## Topic Notes

### Regional Aspirations and Nation-Building

The 1980s witnessed a resurgence of regional aspirations for autonomy, often involving armed assertions and leading to negotiated settlements between the Centre and regional groups. India’s approach, rooted in democratic politics, allows expression of regional identity without branding it anti-national, while ensuring policy accommodation. However, tensions arise when national unity overshadows regional needs, or regional demands overlook national interests.

### Areas of Tension

Post-independence challenges included Partition, integration of princely states, linguistic reorganization, and demands for separate states. Jammu and Kashmir, Nagaland, Mizoram, and the Dravidian movement initially questioned India’s unity. Linguistic state agitations led to the creation of Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, etc., and later, Punjab, Haryana, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, and Uttarakhand. Redrawing internal boundaries addressed diversity but did not resolve all conflicts permanently.

### Jammu and Kashmir: Roots and Autonomy

J&K comprises three distinct regions: Kashmir Valley (Muslim majority, Kashmiri speaking), Jammu (Hindu-Muslim-Sikh mix), and Ladakh (Buddhist-Muslim). The Kashmir issue has external (Pakistan’s claim) and internal (Kashmiri identity and autonomy) dimensions. After the 1947 tribal invasion, the Maharaja acceded to India, leading to special status under Article 370—granting greater autonomy, a separate constitution, and limited applicability of central laws. However, grievances persist: unfulfilled plebiscite promise, erosion of autonomy, and perceived democratic deficit.

### Jammu and Kashmir: Politics and Insurgency

Sheikh Abdullah’s National Conference championed autonomy but faced dismissal in 1953. After his 1974 accord with Indira Gandhi, he returned as Chief Minister, but his son Farooq’s dismissal in 1984 bred resentment. The 1987 election was widely seen as rigged, fueling militancy and separatist movements demanding either independent Kashmir, merger with Pakistan, or greater autonomy. Post-1990, insurgency and violence dominated, with elections only in 1996 and 2002. Separatist strands have since shifted toward negotiating autonomy within India.

### Punjab: Political Context and the Anandpur Sahib Resolution

Punjab’s reorganisation in 1966 created a Sikh-majority state, but the Akali Dal struggled with coalition politics, dismissals, and limited Hindu support. The 1973 Anandpur Sahib Resolution demanded regional autonomy and a redefined Centre-State relationship, with references to Sikh dominance, which some interpreted as separatist. The movement escalated into armed insurgency after the 1980 dismissal of the Akali government, with militants occupying the Golden Temple.

### Operation Blue Star and Aftermath

In June 1984, the Indian Army conducted Operation Blue Star to flush out militants from the Golden Temple. The operation damaged the shrine and deeply hurt Sikh sentiments, intensifying militancy. Prime Minister Indira Gandhi’s assassination by her Sikh bodyguards in October 1984 triggered anti-Sikh riots, especially in Delhi, with inadequate government response. Prime Minister Manmohan Singh later apologised for the violence. The cycle of violence eventually subsided, paving the way for return to electoral politics.

### Dravidian Movement: Regional Pride and Electoral Success

The Dravidian movement, led by E.V. Ramasami ‘Periyar’, opposed Brahminical dominance and north Indian cultural hegemony. It demanded a separate Dravida Nadu initially but later used democratic means. The DMK, formed after a split, agitated for Tamil cultural revival, anti-Hindi imposition, and regional economic interests, winning power in 1967. Dravidian parties (DMK, AIADMK) have dominated Tamil Nadu politics and participated in national coalitions, demonstrating compatibility between regionalism and nationalism.

### Lessons and the Way Forward

Regional aspirations, when suppressed, can turn violent; democratic accommodation can channel them constructively. The Indian experience shows that federal flexibility, dialogue, and political representation can reconcile regional identity with national unity. Challenges in J&K, Punjab, and the North-East highlight the need for genuine autonomy, inclusive development, and respect for plural identities.

Body:
1. Regional aspirations: Regional parties mobilise caste identities to demand state autonomy (e.g., Dravidian parties, BSP in Uttar Pradesh, regional satraps). Caste serves as a marker of regional identity and backwardness.
2. Electoral manifestations: Democracy enables caste-based politicisation through reservations, vote-bank politics, and coalition governments. Caste arithmetic influences candidate selection and policy.

Conclusion: While both factors drive caste politics, they also pose challenges to national integration; need for balanced approach.

### Mains Prompt 2: UPSC CSE Mains 2014 (GS-II, Q2)

> Though the federal principle is dominant in our Constitution and that principle is one of its basic features, but it is equally true that federalism under the Indian Constitution leans in favour of a strong Centre, a feature that militates against the concept of strong federalism. Discuss.

**Source:** General Studies Paper 2  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** Introduction: Indian federalism is a unique blend; describe the federal-unitary debate.
Body:
1. Federal features: Dual polity, written constitution, division of powers, independent judiciary, bicameralism.
2. Unitary features: Strong Centre, single citizenship, all-India services, emergency provisions, Governor’s role, Parliament’s power to alter state boundaries.
3. Justification for strong Centre: Historical context of partition, need for national unity, socio-economic development.
4. Does it militate against strong federalism? Critics argue centralisation undermines state autonomy; supporters see it as functional necessity. Examples from chapter: special status under Article 370, regional autonomy demands.
Conclusion: The Indian model is a pragmatic ‘cooperative federalism’ that balances unity and diversity; emerging challenges require further devolution.
