## Master Summary

The chapter 'Rise of Popular Movements' examines the emergence of social and political movements in India from the 1970s onward, driven by popular discontent with formal party politics and the failures of planned development. It highlights how diverse groups—women, Dalits, farmers, and environmentalists—organized outside traditional party structures to demand rights and accountability. Key movements covered include the Chipko movement for ecological conservation, the Dalit Panthers against caste atrocities, the Bharatiya Kisan Union for farmers’ economic demands, and the anti-arrack movement for women’s empowerment. The chapter distinguishes between party-based and non-party political formations, emphasizing the role of voluntary organizations and civil society in deepening democratic participation and addressing local issues.

## Topic Notes

### Nature of Popular Movements

Popular movements are collective actions that arise from social discontent, often outside formal electoral politics. After the 1970s, disillusionment with political parties and the failure of planned development to address poverty and inequality led to 'new social movements.' These movements focus on identity, environment, and rights, using direct action rather than party channels. They may be social or political, with overlaps.

### Party-based vs. Non-party Movements

Party-based movements, like trade unions and kisan sabhas, are linked to political parties and influence policy through party channels. Non-party movements emerged from the decline of faith in parties; they are voluntary organizations (Voluntary Sector Organizations) that engage in mass mobilisation without contesting elections. These 'non-party political formations' aim to resolve local issues directly and reform democracy through citizen participation. Over time, many have become dependent on external funding.

### Chipko Movement

Originating in 1973 in Uttarakhand, villagers protested commercial logging by hugging trees. The movement demanded local control over natural resources, ecological balance, and minimum wages for forest workers. Women's active participation and their fight against alcoholism broadened the movement. It succeeded in getting a 15-year ban on tree felling in the Himalayas, symbolising grassroots environmental and livelihood struggles.

### Dalit Panthers

Founded in 1972 in Maharashtra, the militant Dalit youth organisation fought caste atrocities and demanded effective implementation of reservations. Dalit literary works, especially autobiographies, became tools of protest. Their sustained agitation led to the 1989 law on atrocities against Dalits. Later, electoral compromises and splits weakened the movement, giving way to groups like BAMCEF.

### Bharatiya Kisan Union (BKU)

An organisation of prosperous farmers from western Uttar Pradesh and Haryana, benefitting from the Green Revolution. In the 1980s, they protested against the government's economic policies, especially electricity rates and low crop prices. They used caste networks for mobilisation and operated as a pressure group, demanding higher floor prices, cheap power, and loan waivers. The movement highlighted the rural-urban divide and achieved some policy concessions.

### Anti-Arrack Movement

In the early 1990s, women in Andhra Pradesh spontaneously mobilised against the sale of arrack (locally brewed alcohol). The movement linked alcoholism to domestic violence, debt, and a crime-politics nexus. Women forced the closure of wine shops and demanded prohibition. It merged with the broader women's movement, raising issues of gender violence and political representation, and contributed to the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments reserving seats for women in local bodies.

### Civil Society and Popular Movements

Voluntary organisations form the backbone of non-party movements. They organise the marginalised, run constructive programmes, and lobby for policy change. Initially rooted in local initiatives, many now receive international funding, sometimes diluting local autonomy. Civil society complements the state by filling governance gaps and amplifying citizen voice, but it faces challenges of accountability and sustainability.
