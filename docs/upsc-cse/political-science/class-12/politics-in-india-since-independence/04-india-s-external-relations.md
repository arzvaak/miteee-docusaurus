## Master Summary

India's foreign policy after independence was shaped by the Cold War context, the legacy of colonialism, and domestic challenges. It adopted the policy of non-alignment, aiming to maintain strategic autonomy and promote peace. Under Nehru, foreign policy focused on sovereignty, territorial integrity, and economic development. Relations with China deteriorated due to border disputes and Tibet, leading to the 1962 war. Relations with Pakistan were marred by conflicts over Kashmir, leading to wars in 1965 and 1971, with the latter resulting in the creation of Bangladesh. India's nuclear policy evolved from a stance of peaceful use to a credible minimum deterrent.

## Topic Notes

### International Context and Foreign Policy Foundations

India's foreign policy was shaped by the post-World War II environment, decolonization, and the Cold War division into US and Soviet blocs. The Directive Principles of State Policy (Article 51) enshrine the promotion of international peace, just relations, respect for international law, and arbitration. India chose to stay away from military alliances, prioritizing sovereignty and development.

### Policy of Non-Alignment

Non-alignment was a strategic response to the Cold War, allowing India to maintain independence from both blocs. It involved active participation in decolonization struggles, anti-apartheid movements, and peacekeeping. India co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) with leaders like Nehru, Nasser, Tito, Sukarno, and Nkrumah. The Bandung Conference (1955) and the Belgrade Summit (1961) were key milestones. Despite occasional tilts (e.g., closer ties with USSR during certain periods), India broadly kept an independent stance and received aid from both blocs.

### Nehru's Role and Objectives

Jawaharlal Nehru, as both Prime Minister and Foreign Minister, defined India’s foreign policy from 1946–1964. The three major objectives were to preserve sovereignty, protect territorial integrity, and promote rapid economic development. Nehru advocated Asian unity, convened the Asian Relations Conference (1947), and championed decolonization. His policy faced domestic criticism from parties like the Jan Sangh and Swatantra Party, which favored closer ties with the US, but Nehru maintained a balanced approach.

### Peace and Conflict with China

India initially had friendly relations with China, recognizing its communist government in 1949. The Panchsheel Agreement (1954) outlined five principles of peaceful coexistence. However, tensions arose over China's annexation of Tibet (1950) and the border dispute in Aksai Chin and Arunachal Pradesh (then NEFA). The Dalai Lama's flight to India in 1959 worsened ties. In 1962, China launched a surprise attack, capturing territory but later declaring a unilateral ceasefire. The war exposed India's military unpreparedness, dented Nehru's prestige, and led to political upheaval, including a no-confidence motion and a split in the Communist Party (CPI vs. CPI-M). Diplomatic relations were restored in 1976, and subsequent leaders like Rajiv Gandhi enhanced engagement, shifting focus to trade.

### Wars and Peace with Pakistan

Indo-Pak relations were strained from Partition over Kashmir. A proxy war occurred in 1947–48, and the issue went to the UN. Cooperation existed on abducted women's recovery and the Indus Waters Treaty (1960). The 1965 war began with Pakistani attacks in the Rann of Kutch and J&K; India retaliated by advancing towards Lahore. The Tashkent Agreement (1966) restored peace. The 1971 crisis arose from Pakistan's crackdown in East Pakistan, leading to a massive refugee influx into India. India supported the Mukti Bahini and, after signing a friendship treaty with the USSR, launched a full-scale war in December 1971, resulting in the creation of Bangladesh. The Shimla Agreement (1972) formalized peace and set principles like resolving disputes bilaterally.

### Evolution of Nuclear Policy

India's nuclear policy initially focused on peaceful use of atomic energy and global disarmament. The 1974 'Smiling Buddha' test demonstrated nuclear capability but was termed a peaceful nuclear explosion. Subsequent tests in 1998 (Pokhran-II) marked a shift to a nuclear weapons state, adopting a policy of credible minimum deterrence with no-first-use and commitment to universal nuclear disarmament. This policy balances strategic autonomy with responsible nuclear stewardship.
