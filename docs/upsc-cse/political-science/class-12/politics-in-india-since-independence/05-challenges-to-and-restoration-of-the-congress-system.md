## Master Summary

This chapter examines the challenges to the Congress system in the 1960s and its restoration under Indira Gandhi. It covers the political succession from Nehru to Shastri and then to Indira Gandhi; the landmark 1967 elections that saw Congress dominance decline and the rise of non-Congress coalitions; the internal party split between Indira Gandhi and the Syndicate; and the 1971 election that restored Congress dominance under Indira Gandhi's leadership with a new ideological orientation.

## Topic Notes

### Challenge of Political Succession (1964-66)

Nehru's death in May 1964 raised concerns about India's democratic stability. Many feared the 'dangerous decade' of the 1960s could derail democracy. Succession proved smooth: K. Kamraj, Congress President, built consensus for Lal Bahadur Shastri, who became PM without controversy. Shastri was a nondescript, simple leader known for his integrity (resigned as Railway Minister after an accident). His tenure (1964-66) faced severe food crisis and the 1965 Pakistan war; he coined 'Jai Jawan Jai Kisan'. Died suddenly in Tashkent in January 1966. The second succession was competitive: Morarji Desai vs. Indira Gandhi. A secret ballot among Congress MPs gave Indira Gandhi a two-thirds majority, marking a peaceful power transition.

### Fourth General Elections, 1967: Context

Leading up to 1967, India faced acute economic crisis: failed monsoons, drought, food shortage, forex depletion, industrial decline, and rupee devaluation (Rs 4.75 to over Rs 7 per USD). Public protests over price rise and unemployment were treated as law-and-order issues, fueling dissatisfaction. Opposition parties united under 'non-Congressism', a strategy coined by Ram Manohar Lohia, arguing Congress rule was undemocratic and anti-poor. Anti-Congress fronts and seat adjustments were formed to consolidate votes.

### 1967 Election Results: 'Political Earthquake'

Congress won the Lok Sabha with its lowest seat tally and vote share since 1952. Half of Indira Gandhi's cabinet ministers lost, including stalwarts like Kamaraj, S.K. Patil, Atulya Ghosh, and K.B. Sahay. At the state level, Congress lost majority in seven states and failed to form governments in two others due to defections. In Madras (Tamil Nadu), DMK won a clear majority – the first non-Congress single-party majority in a state – riding on anti-Hindi agitation. In eight states, non-Congress coalition governments (Samyukt Vidhayak Dal, SVD) were formed, often with ideologically incompatible partners (e.g., Socialists, Jana Sangh, Communists together in Bihar).

### Coalition Politics and Defections

The 1967 elections marked the advent of coalition politics at the state level. SVD governments included disparate parties united only by anti-Congressism. These coalitions were unstable due to ideological differences and defections. The phrase 'Aya Ram, Gaya Ram' originated from Haryana MLA Gaya Lal, who changed parties thrice in a fortnight in 1967, highlighting rampant political opportunism. Defections played a key role in making and unmaking governments, leading to President's Rule in some states.

### Indira Gandhi vs. the Syndicate

Indira Gandhi's real challenge came from within her party. The 'Syndicate', a group of senior Congress leaders led by K. Kamraj, S.K. Patil, S. Nijalingappa, N. Sanjeeva Reddy, and Atulya Ghosh, had installed her as PM expecting her to be pliable. Instead, she sought to assert independence. She adopted a left-leaning Ten Point Programme (bank social control, general insurance nationalisation, land reforms, etc.) to build a popular base and sideline the Syndicate. Tensions erupted openly during the 1969 presidential election after President Zakir Hussain's death. The Syndicate nominated Sanjeeva Reddy as the official Congress candidate; Indira Gandhi supported Vice-President V.V. Giri as an independent. She called for a 'conscience vote', leading to Giri's victory. This formalised the party split into Congress (O) – Organisation, led by the Syndicate, and Congress (R) – Requisitionists, led by Indira Gandhi.

### Abolition of Privy Purse and Leftward Shift

Indira Gandhi championed the abolition of privy purses – hereditary allowances to former princes – as part of her socialist and pro-poor agenda. The move was opposed by Morarji Desai and the Syndicate. When a constitutional amendment failed in the Rajya Sabha, the government issued an ordinance, but the Supreme Court struck it down. Indira Gandhi used this as a major election plank in 1971, promising to remove legal hurdles. After her landslide victory, the Constitution was amended to abolish privy purses. This episode highlighted her ideological combat against entrenched privilege.

### The 1971 Election and Restoration of the Congress System

Despite the split, Indira Gandhi's minority government survived with issue-based support from CPI and DMK. She called early elections in 1971, campaigning on 'Garibi Hatao' (Remove Poverty) and pro-poor policies. The Congress (R) won a massive majority (352 out of 518 seats), restoring Congress dominance but under a transformed, more populist and centralized leadership. The opposition was fragmented; the old Syndicate leaders were decimated. This victory reshaped Indian politics, cementing Indira Gandhi's authority and marking the emergence of a plebiscitary politics.
