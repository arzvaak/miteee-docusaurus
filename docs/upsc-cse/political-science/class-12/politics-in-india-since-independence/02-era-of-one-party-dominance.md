## Master Summary

The chapter 'Era of One-Party Dominance' examines the first decade of electoral politics in independent India, focusing on the establishment of free and fair elections, the dominance of the Indian National Congress, and the emergence of opposition parties. Despite challenges like poverty and illiteracy, India successfully conducted its first general election in 1952, which became a landmark in democratic history. The Congress won an overwhelming majority in the first three general elections (1952, 1957, 1962), ruling both at the Centre and in most states. Its dominance was rooted in the legacy of the national movement, a broad social and ideological coalition, and effective faction management. The electoral system (first-past-the-post) amplified its seat share. Opposition parties like the Socialist Party and Communist Party of India existed but could not dent Congress's hegemony, except in Kerala (1957). The chapter highlights that this one-party dominance occurred under democratic conditions, distinguishing it from authoritarian one-party rule elsewhere.

## Topic Notes

### Challenge of Building Democracy

Independent India inherited a legacy of democratic commitment from the freedom struggle. Despite threats of national unity, leaders opted for democracy over authoritarian rule. The Constitution was framed, and the Election Commission was set up in January 1950 with Sukumar Sen as the first Chief Election Commissioner. Organizing the first general election was a mammoth task involving delimitation, electoral rolls, and training over 3 lakh personnel. It was the first experience of universal adult franchise in a largely illiterate and poor nation, described as the 'biggest gamble in history'. The election, held from October 1951 to February 1952, proved successful, with a turnout of more than half the electorate. The event confounded sceptics and established India's democratic credentials globally.

### Congress Dominance in the First Three General Elections (1952, 1957, 1962)

The Congress won 364 of 489 Lok Sabha seats in 1952, with the CPI a distant second (16 seats). The party maintained this dominance in 1957 (371 seats) and 1962 (361 seats), winning about three-fourths of the seats while securing less than 50% of the popular vote. The first-past-the-post system amplified its seat share, as non-Congress votes split among multiple opponents. In state assemblies, the Congress won majorities everywhere except Travancore-Cochin, Madras, and Orissa in 1952, later forming governments there too. The exception was Kerala in 1957, where the CPI-led coalition formed the government, marking the first democratically elected communist government in the world. The Congress dismissed this government in 1959 under Article 356, which was widely criticized as misuse of emergency powers.

### Nature of Congress Dominance

India's one-party dominance differed from authoritarian one-party states (e.g., PRI in Mexico, Soviet Union) because it occurred under free and fair competitive elections. The Congress's success is attributed to: (1) Legacy of the national movement providing widespread organizational reach and popular trust; (2) Charismatic leadership of Nehru; (3) 'First off the blocks' advantage as many parties formed only after Independence; (4) A catch-all, coalitional character that absorbed diverse groups, preventing opposition from staking distinct claims. The party functioned as an umbrella, accommodating ideological extremes, thus continually renewing consensus.

### Congress as Social and Ideological Coalition

Originally an elite, urban body, the Congress evolved into a mass party by bringing together peasants, industrialists, workers, owners, various castes, classes, religions, and linguistic groups. This 'rainbow coalition' was also ideological, including revolutionaries and pacifists, conservatives and radicals. Pre-Independence, it permitted internal parties like the Congress Socialist Party. Post-Independence, the Congress continued to manage internal differences and ambitions with high tolerance, making it difficult for opposition parties to distinguish themselves. The party's middle-of-the-road policies and inclusive approach kept it electorally dominant.

### Emergence of Opposition Parties

Opposition parties existed but remained weak. The Socialist Party, born from the Congress Socialist Party (1934), separated in 1948 after the Congress barred dual membership. It faced an identity crisis when the Congress adopted a socialist pattern of society (1955 Avadi session). The party underwent splits, forming groups like KMPP, PSP, and SSP. Key leaders included Rammanohar Lohia, Jayaprakash Narayan, and Asoka Mehta. The Communist Party of India (CPI) worked within the Congress until 1941. After Independence, it initially advocated violent revolution (Telangana uprising) but later embraced parliamentary politics in 1951. It won 16 seats in 1952, its support concentrated in Andhra, West Bengal, Bihar, and Kerala. The 1964 split led to CPI (pro-Soviet) and CPI(M) (pro-China). Other parties like Jana Sangh (precursor to BJP) also emerged but had limited success. The opposition's fragmentation and the Congress's broad coalition kept one-party dominance intact.

### Significance of the First General Election

The 1952 election disproved the notion that democracy was unsuitable for poor, illiterate societies. It established India’s electoral system and the legitimacy of democratic governance. The use of ballot boxes with symbols, the meticulous preparation, and peaceful transition of power set a global precedent. The Election Commission's role in ensuring fairness, including correcting electoral rolls to record women's names, reinforced institutional foundations.
