## Master Summary

The chapter examines the transformative political developments in India since the late 1980s, including the end of Congress dominance, the rise of coalition governments, the implementation of the Mandal Commission report for OBC reservations, the initiation of economic liberalisation, and the politics of Hindutva culminating in the Ayodhya demolition. It analyzes how these events reshaped Indian democracy, heightened identity politics, and sparked debates on secularism and development.

## Topic Notes

### Context of the 1990s

Five key developments marked the late 1980s and 1990s:
1. Defeat of Congress in 1989 (seats fell from 415 in 1984 to 197), ending the 'Congress system'.
2. Rise of the 'Mandal issue': In 1990, the National Front government decided to implement the Mandal Commission's recommendation of 27% job reservation for OBCs, leading to nationwide protests.
3. Economic policy shift: Initiation of structural adjustment programme (new economic reforms) in 1991, moving away from the earlier state-led model.
4. Ayodhya demolition: The disputed structure (Babri Masjid) was demolished in December 1992, intensifying Hindutva politics and secularism debates.
5. Assassination of Rajiv Gandhi in May 1991; Congress chose Narsimha Rao as Prime Minister.

### Era of Coalitions

Since 1989, no single party secured a Lok Sabha majority. Coalition governments became the norm:
- 1989: National Front (Janata Dal + regional parties) with outside support of BJP and Left Front; V.P. Singh as PM.
- 1996: United Front (similar composition) with Congress support to keep BJP out.
- 1998–2004: BJP-led NDA governments under Atal Bihari Vajpayee (1998–99 incomplete; 1999–2004 full term).
- 2004 onwards: UPA governments led by Congress.
The decline of Congress did not lead to a single-party replacement; regional parties became crucial, reflecting long-term social changes and the earlier 'coalition character' of the Congress itself.

### Political Rise of Other Backward Classes

The 1980s saw OBCs emerging as a political force. The Janata Party (1977) and later Janata Dal drew support from backward castes. The implementation of Mandal Commission (1990) spurred OBC identity politics and mobilisation. The commission, chaired by B.P. Mandal, recommended 27% reservation in central government jobs and educational institutions. Supreme Court upheld the decision in the Indira Sawhney case (1992). Dalit politics also grew: BAMCEF (1978) and BSP (1984) under Kanshi Ram championed 'Bahujan' (SC, ST, OBC, minorities) empowerment, achieving breakthrough in Uttar Pradesh.

### Communalism, Secularism, Democracy

BJP, formed in 1980, initially adopted 'Gandhian Socialism' but after 1986 shifted to Hindutva (defined by Savarkar). Two events catalysed this: the Shah Bano case (1985–86) and the unlocking of Babri Masjid (February 1986). The Ayodhya movement involved a Rath Yatra by Advani, kar seva, and culminated in the demolition of the mosque on 6 December 1992. This triggered countrywide communal riots, especially in Mumbai (1993) and Gujarat (2002). The events deepened debates on secularism, minority rights, and the use of religion in electoral politics, challenging India's democratic ethos.

### Mains Prompt 1: UPSC CSE Mains 2020, Political Science & IR Paper I, Q7(c)

> ‘Liberalisation of Indian Economy has not been accompanied with adequate reforms’. Comment.

**Source:** upsc-cse-mains-2020-political-science-and-ir-paper-i-c991891a91  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Define liberalisation and note the 1991 reforms as a shift from state-led to market-oriented economy. Mention that liberalisation mainly focused on industrial deregulation, trade, FDI, and financial sector.
2. Body:
   a. Reforms undertaken: Delicensing, trade liberalisation, current account convertibility, partial capital account convertibility, FDI liberalisation, tax reforms (GST later).
   b. Areas lacking adequate reforms: Labour market rigidities, land acquisition, power sector, agricultural marketing, disinvestment/privatisation, and judicial/administrative bottlenecks. Highlight piecemeal approach and political constraints.
   c. Consequences: Incomplete reforms led to jobless growth, agrarian distress, stalled manufacturing, and infrastructure gaps despite high growth periods.
3. Conclusion: While liberalisation brought dynamism, the absence of comprehensive second-generation reforms (labour, land, capital) has limited its potential; a renewed push is needed for inclusive growth.

### Mains Prompt 2: UPSC CSE Mains 2020, Political Science & IR Paper I, Q8(b)

> Examine the role of religion in Indian Electoral Politics in the contemporary times.

**Source:** upsc-cse-mains-2020-political-science-and-ir-paper-i-c991891a91  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Religion has been a persistent factor in Indian electoral politics, often used for identity-based mobilisation. Contemporary trends show it remains salient but is also contested by development/other identities.
2. Body:
   a. Historical context: Communal parties and mobilisation since Partition; rise of Hindutva politics (Ayodhya movement, BJP’s electoral success).
   b. Manifestations: Caste–religion nexus; polarisation along communal lines; use of religious symbols, rhetoric, and identity politics in campaigns.
   c. Constitutional/legal framework: Representation of the People Act, 1951 prohibits appeal on religious grounds; judicial pronouncements (e.g., 2017 verdict on Hindutva as a way of life).
   d. Consequences: Erosion of secular ethos, communal violence, impact on governance and policy, yet also counter-mobilisation by secular forces and civil society.
3. Conclusion: Religion remains a potent electoral tool, but its role is complex and contested; India’s democratic strength lies in balancing religious identity with inclusive development.
