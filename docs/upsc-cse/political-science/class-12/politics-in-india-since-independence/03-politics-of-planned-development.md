## Master Summary

This chapter examines India's post-independence path of planned development, highlighting the political contestations over development models. It discusses the consensus on state-led planning, the establishment of the Planning Commission, and the mixed economy approach. The First Five Year Plan (1951–56) focused on agriculture and irrigation, while the Second Plan (1956–61) under P.C. Mahalanobis prioritized rapid industrialization with a socialist pattern. Key debates—agriculture vs. industry and public vs. private sector—shaped policy. Outcomes included foundational infrastructure and partial land reforms, but poverty persisted. The chapter underscores that development decisions are inherently political, involving trade-offs and democratic deliberation.

## Topic Notes

### Introduction: Political Contestation over Development

Development decisions involve competing interests (e.g., industrialists, tribal communities, environmentalists). In a democracy, such major decisions must be taken or approved by the people's representatives, balancing expert advice with political judgment. After independence, India had to make interconnected decisions guided by a shared vision of economic growth with social justice.

### Ideas of Development

Post-independence, 'development' was equated with modernization on Western lines—industrialization, capitalism, and scientific rationality. Two models existed: liberal-capitalist (US, Europe) and socialist (USSR). In India, the Soviet model gained wide support among Congress socialists and Communists, while very few favored American-style capitalism. The nationalist consensus favored active state intervention for poverty alleviation and redistribution. Debates centered on industrialization versus agricultural priority.

### Planning and the Planning Commission

There was broad consensus that development could not be left to private actors; the state had to design a plan. The idea of planning gained global support after the Great Depression and Soviet growth. Even Indian industrialists drafted the Bombay Plan (1944) proposing state-led economic initiatives. The Planning Commission was established in March 1950 by a government resolution (not a constitutional body). It had an advisory role, and its recommendations became effective after cabinet approval. The Prime Minister was the Chairperson.

### Early Initiatives: The First Five Year Plan (1951–56)

The First Five Year Plan aimed to break the cycle of poverty. K.N. Raj advocated 'hasten slowly' to protect democracy. It focused on the agrarian sector, investing in dams (Bhakhra Nangal) and irrigation. Land reforms were identified as crucial for agricultural growth. It sought to raise national income by increasing savings, though results were modest. The plan emphasized land distribution and community development.

### Rapid Industrialisation: The Second Five Year Plan (1956–61)

The Second Plan, drafted under P.C. Mahalanobis, stressed rapid industrialization, especially heavy industries in the public sector. The Congress's Avadi Resolution (1955) declared the goal of a 'socialist pattern of society'. The plan imposed import tariffs to protect domestic industries, aiding both public and private sector growth. It marked a turning point but faced challenges: technological dependence, foreign exchange costs, and a looming food shortage due to industry's precedence over agriculture.

### Key Controversies: Agriculture vs. Industry

Critics argued the Second Plan lacked an agrarian strategy, causing rural suffering. Gandhian economists like J.C. Kumarappa advocated rural industrialization. Chaudhary Charan Singh emphasized agriculture's primacy, warning of urban-industrial bias. Defenders claimed the plan had an agrarian strategy—land reforms, community development, irrigation—but implementation failed due to powerful landowning classes. The debate highlighted the trade-off between industrial growth and agricultural well-being.

### Key Controversies: Public vs. Private Sector

India adopted a mixed economy, blending capitalist and socialist elements. Agriculture, trade, and small industry remained largely private, while the state controlled heavy industries, infrastructure, and regulated trade. Critics from the right argued the public sector stifled private enterprise through licenses and permits, leading to inefficiency and corruption. Left critics claimed the state did too little for health and education, and its interventions created a privileged middle class without reducing poverty significantly.

### Major Outcomes: Foundations and Land Reforms

The early plans laid the foundation for future growth through mega-dams (Bhakhra-Nangal, Hirakud), steel plants, and infrastructure. Land reforms achieved abolition of zamindari, weakening landlord power. However, land ceiling laws were widely evaded, and tenancy security remained poorly implemented. Political power remained with landowning classes, hindering further reform. Overall, the goal of social justice was the hardest to realize, and unequal development created vested interests that perpetuated inequality.
