## Topic Notes

### Environmental Concerns in Global Politics

Environmental issues became political from the 1960s. The 1972 Club of Rome's 'Limits to Growth' highlighted resource depletion. The 1987 Brundtland Report ('Our Common Future') stressed sustainability. The 1992 Earth Summit (Rio) consolidated environmental politics, attended by 170 states. It revealed different agendas: Global North focused on ozone depletion and global warming; Global South on development and environment linkage. Rio produced conventions on climate change, biodiversity, forestry, and Agenda 21 for sustainable development. Critics say Agenda 21 prioritized economic growth over ecology.

### Protection of Global Commons

Global commons are areas beyond national jurisdiction: atmosphere, Antarctica, ocean floor, outer space. Cooperation is difficult due to sovereignty and differing interests. Key agreements: 1959 Antarctic Treaty, 1987 Montreal Protocol (ozone), 1991 Antarctic Environmental Protocol. North-South inequalities influence governance; e.g., benefits from space exploitation are unequal.

### Common but Differentiated Responsibilities (CBDR)

Established at Rio 1992. Recognizes that developed countries have caused more environmental degradation historically and should take greater responsibility. Developing countries need space for industrialisation and poverty alleviation. UNFCCC incorporates CBDR and 'respective capabilities'. The Kyoto Protocol (1997) set binding emission targets for developed nations, exempting developing countries like India and China.

### Common Property Resources

Common property refers to resources shared by a community with defined rights and duties. In India, sacred groves exemplify community-managed resources, preserving biodiversity and culture. However, privatisation, population growth, and ecosystem degradation have eroded such commons.

### India's Stand on Environmental Issues

India signed and ratified the Kyoto Protocol in 2002. It argues per capita emissions are far lower than developed countries; development is priority. It supports CBDR and historical responsibility. India opposes binding commitments on developing countries under UNFCCC. Domestic efforts: National Auto-fuel Policy (cleaner fuels), Energy Conservation Act 2001, Electricity Act 2003 (renewable energy), National Mission on Biodiesel, large renewable energy programme. Demands technology transfer and financial resources from developed nations. Advocates SAARC common position on environment.

### Environmental Movements: One or Many?

Environmental movements are diverse: forest movements (Chipko in India, Brazil, Indonesia), anti-dam movements (Narmada Bachao Andolan, Franklin River in Australia), anti-mining campaigns (Philippines vs Western Mining Corporation). Southern movements often involve people living in forests, unlike Northern wilderness campaigns. Many emphasize non-violence and sustainable livelihoods.

### Resource Geopolitics

Resources (oil, minerals, timber) have been central to inter-state competition and colonialism. Western powers ensured resource supply through military presence, stockpiling, and alliances. Oil in West Asia and Central Asia remains strategic; control over its flow shapes geopolitics. Post-Cold War, resource security remains critical, including rare earth minerals. Resource conflicts also produce environmental damage and displacement.

## Mains Answer Practice

### Mains Prompt 1: UPSC CSE Mains 2017 Political Science & IR Paper II Q4(a)

> The recent move of USA to withdraw from the Paris Climate Agreement is a setback in the consensus achieved on protecting the world environment. In this context, assess the future prospects on climate control.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2017 Political Science & IR Paper II  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** Introduction: US withdrawal under Trump weakened the Paris Agreement's momentum, but the accord's architecture allowed resilience.

Body:
- Impact of withdrawal: loss of US climate finance, leadership vacuum, potential for other nations to backslide.
- Countervailing factors: EU, China, India reaffirmed commitments; subnational actors in US (states, cities) continued climate action.
- Return to agreement under Biden shows flexibility and enduring global consensus.
- Future prospects: need for enhanced Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs), technology transfer, climate finance from developed to developing nations; role of multilateralism and civil society.

Conclusion: Though withdrawal was a setback, the Paris Agreement's flexible framework and broad global support ensure continued progress in climate control, provided major emitters strengthen ambition.

### Mains Prompt 2: UPSC CSE Mains 2025 GS III Q17

> Mineral resources are fundamental to the country's economy and these are exploited by mining. Why is mining considered an environmental hazard? Explain the remedial measures required to reduce the environmental hazard due to mining.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2025 General Studies Paper III  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** Introduction: Mining is essential for economic growth but often causes severe environmental damage.

Body:
- Environmental hazards: deforestation, soil erosion, biodiversity loss, water pollution (acid mine drainage, heavy metal contamination), air pollution (dust, emissions), land subsidence, waste generation, displacement of communities.
- Remedial measures: sustainable mining practices, bio-remediation and afforestation, proper waste management, adoption of cleaner technologies, strict enforcement of environmental laws (EIA, closure plans), community participation in monitoring, rehabilitation of mined lands (backfilling, re-vegetation).

Conclusion: Balancing development with environmental protection requires green mining technologies, robust regulation, and corporate accountability to minimize mining's ecological footprint.
