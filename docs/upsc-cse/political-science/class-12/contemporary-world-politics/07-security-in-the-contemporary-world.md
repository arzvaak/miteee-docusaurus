## Master Summary

The chapter 'Security in the Contemporary World' explores the evolution of security concepts from a narrow state-centric military focus to broader frameworks encompassing human and global dimensions. Traditional security prioritizes national sovereignty, territorial integrity, and military threats, relying on deterrence, defence, balance of power, and alliances. It also acknowledges internal security challenges, especially in post-colonial states. Cooperation through disarmament, arms control, and confidence-building is recognized. Non-traditional security shifts the referent to individuals and communities, introducing human security (freedom from fear and want) and global security against transnational threats like terrorism, human rights violations, poverty, migration, and health pandemics. The chapter emphasizes that security is context-dependent and that contemporary challenges demand cooperative, multidimensional responses beyond military force.

## Topic Notes

### Introduction: Why Study Security?

Security is often invoked to silence debate, but in a democracy, citizens must understand its meaning. The chapter demystifies security, distinguishing between traditional and non-traditional approaches, and highlights how context shapes security perceptions. India’s security concerns are used as a backdrop to discuss these ideas.

### Defining Security

At its core, security means freedom from extremely dangerous threats to core values. Not every threat qualifies; only those that could irreparably damage core values. The definition depends on whose core values (state vs. ordinary people) and the intensity of threat. Security is a ‘slippery’ concept because societies and times differ in what they consider threatening.

### Traditional Notions: External

Traditional security centres on military threats from other states. The core values threatened are sovereignty, independence, and territorial integrity. Governments have three responses: (i) surrender, (ii) deterrence (prevent attack by making costs unacceptable), (iii) defence (resist attack). Security policy also involves:
- Balance of Power: ensuring no other state becomes overwhelmingly strong.
- Alliance Building: formal coalitions (e.g., NATO) to pool power; alliances shift with national interests (e.g., US support for Afghan mujahideen against USSR, later attack on Al Qaeda).
The international system lacks a central authority, so each state must secure itself; the UN has limited authority.

### Traditional Notions: Internal

Internal security is a key concern for many states, though after WWII, major powers felt internally secure. Post-colonial states face unique challenges:
- Fear of neighbours (border disputes, population control).
- Internal threats from separatist movements; external actors may support these.
Internal wars now constitute over 95% of armed conflicts, with a twelve-fold rise in civil wars between 1946 and 1991.

### Traditional Security and Cooperation

Cooperation in traditional security includes:
- Disarmament: eliminating weapon types (e.g., Biological Weapons Convention 1972, Chemical Weapons Convention 1992).
- Arms Control: regulating weapon development (e.g., Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty 1972, Strategic Arms Limitation Talks, Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty 1968).
- Confidence Building: sharing military information to prevent misperception and surprise attacks.
These measures accept that military force can be limited by shared norms.

### Non-Traditional Notions: Human and Global Security

Non-traditional security expands the referent from the state to individuals (‘human security’) or all humankind (‘global security’). Human security has two versions:
- Narrow: freedom from fear (violent threats to individuals).
- Broad: freedom from want (includes hunger, disease, natural disasters, threats to dignity).
Secure states do not automatically mean secure people; more people have been killed by their own governments than by foreign armies. Global security addresses transnational issues like climate change, terrorism, and pandemics that require international cooperation.

### New Sources of Threats

Non-traditional security highlights diverse threats:
- Terrorism: political violence deliberately targeting civilians to achieve political change; 9/11 brought global attention.
- Human Rights: three categories (political, economic/social, group rights); no consensus on universal rights; debate over UN intervention (e.g., Rwanda, Kuwait).
- Global Poverty: population growth in poor countries exacerbates inequality; linked to armed conflicts (sub-Saharan Africa).
- Migration: refugees vs. internally displaced persons; wars drive refugee flows.
- Health Epidemics: HIV/AIDS, bird flu, SARS spread across borders; drug resistance increases threats; demonstrate interdependence.
For an issue to qualify as a security problem, it must threaten the existence of the referent (state or group), and its nature varies by context (e.g., Maldives – sea level rise, Southern Africa – HIV/AIDS, Rwanda – genocide).

### Cooperative Security

Addressing non-traditional threats requires cooperation rather than military confrontation. International collaboration is essential for problems like terrorism, climate change, and health epidemics. The chapter concludes that security is dynamic and necessitates a mix of traditional and non-traditional approaches tailored to specific contexts.

### Mains Prompt 1: Mains 2019

> Indian Government has recently strengthened the anti-terrorism laws by amending the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act (UAPA), 1967 and the NIA Act. Analyze the changes in the context of prevailing security environment while discussing the scope and reasons for opposing the UAPA by human rights organizations. (Answer in 250 words)

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2019 GS III  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Context of rising terrorism; UAPA and NIA amendments aim to bolster counter-terrorism framework.
2. Key amendments: Expanded definition of terrorist acts, enhanced investigation powers, stricter bail conditions.
3. Security environment justification: Cross-border threats, lone-wolf attacks, digital radicalization necessitate robust laws.
4. Human rights concerns: Vague definitions enabling misuse, targeting activists and dissent, prolonged pre-trial detention, undermining due process.
5. Scope of opposition: Alleged violations of Articles 14, 19, 21; chilling effect on civil liberties; lack of accountability.
6. Balancing act: Need for judicial oversight, periodic review, adherence to international human rights standards.
7. Conclusion: Security laws must be effective yet tempered with safeguards to preserve democratic values.

### Mains Prompt 2: Mains 2024

> 'Terrorism has become a significant threat to global peace and security.' Evaluate the effectiveness of the United Nations Security Council's Counter Terrorism Committee (CTC) and its associated bodies in addressing and mitigating this threat at the international level. (Answer in 250 words)

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2024 GS II  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Terrorism as transnational threat; CTC established post-9/11 under UNSC Resolution 1373.
2. Mandate: Monitor state compliance, build counter-terrorism capacity, facilitate global legal frameworks.
3. Achievements: Near-universal reporting by states, promoting anti-terror legislation, financial sanctions against Al-Qaida/ISIS (1267 Committee).
4. Limitations: Lack of enforcement mechanism, selective implementation, political divisions on terrorism definition, no binding resolutions for all.
5. Associated bodies: CTED provides expert analysis; 1267 Sanctions Committee; but resource constraints.
6. Effectiveness assessment: Partially effective in norm-setting; limited in operational impact; gaps in addressing root causes and cyberterrorism.
7. Conclusion: Need for reform, inclusive global counter-terrorism treaty, enhanced cooperation, and addressing socio-economic drivers.
