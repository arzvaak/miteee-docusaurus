## Topic Notes

### What is South Asia?

South Asia typically includes Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka. The Himalayas and the Indian Ocean provide natural insularity, fostering a distinct linguistic, social, and cultural identity. While Afghanistan and Myanmar are often discussed in the regional context, and China is a major actor, the chapter uses the seven-country definition. The region exhibits immense diversity yet forms a single geo-political space.

### Political Systems and Democratisation

The political systems vary: India and Sri Lanka have sustained multi-party democracy since independence. Pakistan and Bangladesh have oscillated between civilian and military rule. Nepal transitioned from monarchy to a nascent republic. Bhutan and the Maldives have recently initiated democratic reforms. Despite weak institutionalisation, surveys show widespread popular support for democracy across the region, expanding the global democratic imagination.

### The Military and Democracy in Pakistan

Pakistan's history is marred by repeated military coups (Ayub Khan, Yahya Khan, Zia-ul-Haq, Musharraf). Elected governments under Z.A. Bhutto, Benazir Bhutto, and Nawaz Sharif were cut short. Factors include the dominance of the military, clergy, and landed aristocracy; the India–Pakistan conflict empowering pro-military groups; and Western backing for military regimes during the Cold War and ‘war on terror’. Yet a vibrant civil society and free press express pro-democracy sentiment.

### Democracy in Bangladesh

East Pakistan's resentment over linguistic and political domination led to a mass movement under Sheikh Mujibur Rahman. India's support during the 1971 war resulted in an independent Bangladesh. Its constitution initially embraced secularism, democracy, and socialism. Sheikh Mujib's shift to one-party rule ended with his assassination in 1975. Military rulers Ziaur Rahman and H.M. Ershad later held managed elections. A popular pro-democracy movement forced Ershad's ouster in 1990, restoring multi-party parliamentary democracy.

### Monarchy and Democracy in Nepal

Nepal was a constitutional monarchy until the king retained sweeping powers. The 1990 pro-democracy movement extracted a democratic constitution, but unstable governments and a Maoist insurgency led to a triangular conflict. In 2002, King Gyanendra dismissed parliament. The 2006 mass movement, led by the Seven Party Alliance and Maoists, forced the king to restore the House of Representatives. Nepal is now moving towards a constituent assembly to frame a new constitution, with the Maoists joining peaceful politics.

### Ethnic Conflict and Democracy in Sri Lanka

Despite democratic continuity, Sri Lanka suffered from Sinhala-majority chauvinism that marginalised the Tamil minority, leading to militant Tamil nationalism and the demand for a separate Tamil Eelam. The LTTE fought an armed insurgency from 1983. India's 1987 intervention with the IPKF proved counterproductive and the force withdrew in 1989. The conflict continued violently, though efforts by Norway and others sought peace. Remarkably, Sri Lanka achieved high human development and economic growth during the civil war.

### India–Pakistan Conflicts

The core dispute is over Kashmir, resulting in wars in 1947–48 and 1965 and unresolved Line of Control. The 1971 war saw India's decisive victory. Strategic flashpoints include the Siachen glacier and an arms race that escalated with nuclear tests in 1998. Pakistan allegedly uses low-intensity conflict through cross-border terrorism, aiding militants in Kashmir and elsewhere. India accuses Pakistan's ISI of supporting insurgencies in Punjab and the Northeast. Pakistan blames India for fomenting unrest in Sindh and Balochistan. Water disputes were partially settled by the Indus Waters Treaty (1960); Sir Creek and river-water interpretation remain contentious.

### India and Its Other Neighbours

India–Bangladesh: Differences over Ganga/Brahmaputra water sharing, illegal immigration, and security cooperation. Economic ties have improved under India's Look East policy. Cooperation on disaster management and environment is growing. India–Nepal: A unique open-border treaty fosters people-to-people ties, but trade disputes, Nepal's China ties, and Maoist suspicions of India cause friction. Energy cooperation and water management link them. India–Sri Lanka: The Tamil ethnic crisis strains relations; India's IPKF intervention soured ties. Post-1990, bilateral relations have stabilised, with economic cooperation and FTA. Other neighbours (Bhutan, Maldives) maintain friendly ties, with India playing a security guarantor role (as in the 1988 Maldives coup attempt).

### Regional Cooperation: SAARC and SAFTA

SAARC (established 1985) is the primary regional forum, but progress is hampered by mutual suspicion, especially the India–Pakistan rivalry. SAFTA (signed in 2004, effective 2006) aims for a free trade area, with non-LDCs reducing duties to 0–5% by 2013. However, implementation has been slow. The lack of regional economic integration contrasts with the shared cultural and civilisational heritage.

### Mains Prompt 4: SAFTA vs BIMSTEC FTA

> Compared to the South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA), the Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multisectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation Free Trade Area (BIMSTEC FTA) seems to be more promising. Critically evaluate.

**Source:** UPSC CSE Mains 2011, GS Paper 2, Q1(c)  

**Outline hint (AI-generated study aid):** 1. Introduction: Brief description of SAFTA and BIMSTEC FTAs; context of South Asian trade integration. 2. SAFTA: Merits – first regional FTA, tariff reduction schedules. Demerits – low intra-regional trade (<5%), large sensitive lists, non-tariff barriers, India-Pakistan political deadlock. 3. BIMSTEC FTA: Merits – links South Asia with Southeast Asia, includes Myanmar and Thailand, energy and transport focus, less political baggage, India’s Act East policy synergy. Demerits – still under negotiation, varying levels of development, connectivity deficits. 4. Comparison: BIMSTEC avoids India-Pakistan rivalry; economic complementarity with ASEAN; might catalyse trade more effectively. However, SAFTA is institutionally older; BIMSTEC lacks a legal framework yet. 5. Recent developments: BINMSTEC as a vehicle for regional cooperation post-SAARC stalemate; emphasis on connectivity and security. 6. Conclusion: While BIMSTEC holds greater promise due to its geopolitical composition and focus, both platforms need political will and infrastructure to realise their potential.
