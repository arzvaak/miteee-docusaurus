## Topic Notes

### India's Response to the Cold War

India, as a leader of NAM, adopted a two-fold policy: (1) staying away from military alliances, and (2) opposing alignment of newly decolonized countries. Unlike neutrality, non-alignment was active—India mediated in the Korean War and sought to activate international organizations outside the superpower blocs. Nehru envisioned a 'genuine commonwealth of free and cooperating nations' to reduce Cold War tensions.

### Direct Benefits of Non-Alignment for India

1. Allowed India to take independent international decisions aligned with national interests rather than superpower dictates. 2. Enabled balancing one superpower against the other, preventing either from bullying India. This pragmatic policy ensured strategic autonomy.

### Criticisms of Non-Alignment

Two major criticisms: (1) Unprincipled—India avoided taking firm stands on crucial global issues in the name of national interest. (2) Inconsistent—despite opposing military alliances, India signed the 20-year Treaty of Peace, Friendship and Cooperation with the USSR in 1971, seen by critics as joining the Soviet bloc. India defended the treaty as a necessity for diplomatic and military support during the Bangladesh crisis, asserting it did not hinder relations with other countries.

### Relevance of NAM After the Cold War

With the USSR's disintegration in 1991, NAM lost immediate relevance as a strategic bloc. However, its core values endure: solidarity among decolonized states, rejection of great power dominance, pursuit of independent foreign policy, and democratization of the global order. These ideals remain relevant for the Global South.

### Arms Control Treaties During the Cold War

The Cold War witnessed both an arms race and arms control efforts. Key treaties: LTBT (1963) banned nuclear tests in atmosphere, space, and underwater. NPT (1968) froze the nuclear weapon status of five states (US, USSR, UK, France, China). SALT I (1972) limited ABM systems and strategic offensive arms. SALT II (1979) furthered limitations. START I (1991) and START II (1993) initiated actual reduction of strategic nuclear weapons.
