## Master Summary

Globalisation is the increasing interconnectedness and integration of economies, cultures, and political systems worldwide, driven by technology, trade liberalisation, and the movement of capital, goods, services, and people. It reduces state sovereignty, homogenises cultures, and creates economic interdependence, with profound impacts on developing countries like India, offering growth opportunities but also fostering inequality and cultural tensions.

## Topic Notes

### Meaning and Dimensions of Globalisation

Globalisation refers to the increasing interdependence and integration of countries through the flow of goods, services, capital, ideas, and people across borders. It is a multidimensional phenomenon encompassing economic, political, cultural, and technological aspects. It transforms the world into a 'global village' where events in one part affect others instantly. Key terms: 'deterritorialisation', 'time-space compression', 'global interconnectedness'.

### Causes and Drivers of Globalisation

Technological revolution in communication (internet, mobile phones) and transport (aviation, containerisation) has been the primary enabler. Economic policies of liberalisation, privatisation, and deregulation adopted by many countries since the 1980s facilitated the free flow of capital and trade. The role of international institutions like the WTO, IMF, and World Bank in promoting trade liberalisation and structural reforms. The end of the Cold War and the expansion of capitalism globally.

### Political Consequences of Globalisation

Globalisation challenges traditional notions of state sovereignty. States lose some control over economic policy due to international financial institutions and market pressures. Global governance institutions and regimes gain influence. However, states remain central actors; they adapt by forming regional blocs (EU, ASEAN) and engaging in multilateral diplomacy. The concept of 'interdependence' grows, but power inequalities persist, with developed countries often setting the rules.

### Economic Consequences: Opportunities and Inequality

Globalisation has led to increased trade, foreign direct investment (FDI), and economic growth. Developing countries can access larger markets, technology, and capital. However, benefits are unevenly distributed. Trade liberalisation often favours developed nations, leading to deindustrialisation in some sectors. Rising income inequality within and between countries. The role of MNCs in influencing economic policies and labour conditions. India's 1991 liberalisation exemplifies both rapid growth and growing disparities.

### Cultural Dimensions: Homogenisation vs. Heterogenisation

Globalisation spreads Western culture and consumerism, leading to fears of cultural homogenisation ('McDonaldisation'). However, it also triggers cultural heterogenisation: local cultures adapt and reinterpret global influences (glocalisation). The rise of global civil society, social media, and multiculturalism. India's experience: adoption of English, fast food, and Western attire alongside the revival of traditional practices and the global popularity of yoga, Bollywood, and Indian cuisine.

### India and Globalisation

India actively integrated into the global economy after the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis, adopting structural reforms: dismantling the License Raj, reducing tariffs, encouraging FDI, and opening services. This spurred high GDP growth, expansion of IT and service sectors, and a larger global footprint. Challenges: agrarian distress, jobless growth, vulnerability to global shocks (e.g., 2008 crisis), and cultural concerns. India’s role in the WTO: balancing agricultural subsidies with market access demands, asserting TRIPS flexibilities, and leading developing country coalitions.

### Resistance and Anti-Globalisation Movements

Globalisation has faced criticism and protests from diverse groups: trade unions worried about job losses, environmentalists concerned over deregulation, farmers opposing seed patents and import surges, and cultural activists defending local identities. The 1999 Seattle protests at the WTO ministerial meeting marked a high point. Movements like the World Social Forum advocate 'alter-globalisation' for a more equitable world order. India has seen protests against FDI in retail and genetic engineering.

### Debate and Future of Globalisation

Proponents argue globalisation enhances efficiency, innovation, and peace through interdependence. Critics highlight rising inequality, homogenisation, and erosion of sovereignty. Recent trends: backlash in the form of protectionism, Brexit, and trade wars suggest a possible deglobalisation. However, digital connectivity and climate change require global cooperation. The debate continues on whether globalisation can be tamed to be more inclusive and sustainable.
