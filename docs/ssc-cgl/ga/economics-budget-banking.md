## 1. Measure Output Without Double Counting

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) measures the value of final goods and services produced **within a country’s domestic territory** during a period. Counting only final output, or summing value added at each stage, prevents double counting.

| Measure | Compact relation | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | output within domestic territory | location is central |
| GNP/GNI | GDP + net factor income from abroad | adds residents’ net factor income |
| NDP | GDP − depreciation | removes capital consumption |
| NNP | GNP − depreciation | national rather than domestic, net rather than gross |
| Real GDP | output valued at constant prices | removes price-change effect |
| Nominal GDP | output valued at current prices | includes current prices |

Depreciation is consumption of fixed capital through wear or obsolescence. “Gross” includes it; “net” deducts it.

**Worked example**

If GDP is 1,000 units and net factor income from abroad is −20, then GNP is $1{,}000+(-20)=980$ units. A negative value means factor income paid abroad exceeds factor income received from abroad.

**Self-check**

Which measure is designed to compare output growth after removing price changes: nominal GDP or real GDP?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Real GDP. It values output at constant prices, so a rise is not merely the result of higher prices.
</details>

## 2. Diagnose Inflation by Level, Cause, and Effect

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, not merely one product becoming expensive. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) tracks retail prices faced by consumers. India’s Wholesale Price Index (WPI) tracks price movements of a basket of goods at the wholesale level and does not cover services in the same way as CPI.

Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand outruns available output. Cost-push inflation arises from higher input or production costs. A supply shock can raise prices while weakening output, so never assume every inflation episode comes from excessive demand.

Unexpected inflation hurts a fixed-income lender and benefits a borrower repaying in money with lower purchasing power. Deflation is a sustained fall in the price level; disinflation means inflation slows.

**Worked example**

Inflation falls from 7% to 5%. Prices are still rising, but more slowly. This is **disinflation**, not deflation.

**Self-check**

Does a rise in the price of onions alone prove general inflation?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

No. Inflation concerns a broad and sustained rise in the general price level. One commodity can rise because of a specific supply shock.
</details>

## 3. Understand RBI Functions and Monetary Tools

The Reserve Bank of India is India’s central bank. Its durable functions include monetary-policy implementation, currency management under the legal framework, banking regulation and supervision, banker to governments, and management of foreign-exchange reserves.

| Tool | Basic mechanism | Usual direction of effect |
|---|---|---|
| Repo operation/rate | RBI supplies short-term liquidity against eligible collateral | easier liquidity when made cheaper, other things equal |
| Liquidity-absorption facility | RBI absorbs surplus liquidity | reduces surplus funds |
| CRR | prescribed cash balance banks keep with RBI | higher ratio restricts lendable funds |
| SLR | prescribed liquid assets maintained by banks | higher ratio ties more funds in liquid assets |
| Open-market operation | RBI buys or sells government securities | purchase injects; sale absorbs liquidity |

The operating framework can evolve. Verify current rates, ratios, and policy stance from the dated RBI release.

**Self-check**

What happens to system liquidity when RBI purchases government securities in an open-market operation?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Liquidity is injected because RBI pays funds into the system in exchange for the securities.
</details>

## 4. Distinguish Banks, Deposits, and Credit Risks

Commercial banks accept deposits and extend credit under banking law and regulation. Cooperative banks have a cooperative ownership structure. Regional Rural Banks were created to serve rural credit needs. An NBFC can perform specified financial activities but is not automatically a bank and does not have every banking privilege.

Demand deposits can be withdrawn on demand; time deposits have a specified term. A cheque directs a bank to pay; a demand draft is issued by a bank after receiving value.

An asset becomes non-performing under regulatory criteria when payment remains overdue beyond the prescribed norm. Exact classification and provisioning rules can be amended, so use RBI’s current rules for a date-specific question.

**Worked example**

Prompt: “Which deposit is withdrawable on demand?” The category is **deposit maturity**, so the answer is a demand deposit, such as a current or savings account subject to its terms—not a fixed-term deposit.

**Self-check**

Is every NBFC a commercial bank?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

No. NBFCs and banks are distinct regulatory categories, even though both may provide credit or other financial services.
</details>

## 5. Read the Union Budget as an Accounting Statement

The Annual Financial Statement is provided for under Article **112** of the Constitution. Learn receipts and expenditure before memorising any deficit.

| Budget item | Meaning | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue receipts | do not create liability or reduce assets | tax and non-tax revenue |
| Capital receipts | create liability or reduce assets | borrowing, recovery of loans, disinvestment proceeds |
| Revenue expenditure | does not directly create assets or reduce liabilities | salaries, interest, subsidies |
| Capital expenditure | creates assets or reduces liabilities | infrastructure assets, loans, debt repayment |

The central formulae are:

$$\text{Revenue deficit}=\text{Revenue expenditure}-\text{Revenue receipts}$$

$$\text{Fiscal deficit}=\text{Total expenditure}-\text{Revenue receipts}-\text{Non-debt capital receipts}$$

$$\text{Primary deficit}=\text{Fiscal deficit}-\text{Interest payments}$$

Current Budget estimates, revised estimates, allocations, and tax thresholds belong in a dated card sourced to the official Budget documents.

**Self-check**

Which deficit removes interest payments from the fiscal deficit?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The primary deficit.
</details>

## 6. Separate Tax and External-Sector Terms

A direct tax is imposed directly on a person or entity, such as income tax. An indirect tax is collected through transactions, such as GST. Statutory incidence and the eventual economic burden are different ideas, but SSC questions usually test the basic classification. Current rates and slabs must be verified for the question’s year.

The balance of trade records exports minus imports of **goods**. The current account is broader: it includes goods, services, primary income, and transfers. The balance of payments records transactions between residents and the rest of the world across current and capital/financial accounts.

Currency **depreciation** generally refers to a market-driven fall in value under a flexible system; **devaluation** is an official downward change under a fixed or managed parity arrangement. A weaker currency can make exports cheaper and imports costlier, but actual trade effects depend on demand, contracts, and timing.

**Self-check**

Are software-service exports included in the balance of trade in goods?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

No. The balance of trade refers to goods. Services appear in the broader current account.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice and Mastery

### Question 1

What converts GDP into NDP?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Deduct depreciation: $NDP=GDP-\text{depreciation}$.
</details>

### Question 2

Inflation falls but remains above zero. What is this called?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Disinflation.
</details>

### Question 3

Which RBI action normally injects liquidity: buying or selling government securities?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Buying government securities injects liquidity; selling them absorbs liquidity.
</details>

### Question 4

Borrowing by the government is classified as which type of receipt?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

A capital receipt because it creates a liability.
</details>

### Question 5

Which is broader: balance of trade or current account?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The current account. It includes goods, services, income, and transfers; the balance of trade covers goods.
</details>
