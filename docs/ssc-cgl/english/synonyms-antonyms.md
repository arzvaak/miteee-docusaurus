*Fix the target word’s sense and part of speech, then compare meaning, degree, connotation, and register.*

Synonyms share a relevant sense; they are rarely interchangeable in every sentence. Antonyms oppose the sense used in context, not every dictionary sense of the word. Direct items have one clear relation. Mark and return when two options differ only in a shade that the sentence does not resolve.

## 1. Fix the Contextual Sense First

A word can have several senses. *Bright* may mean luminous, intelligent, cheerful, or promising. The sentence selects the branch:

- “a bright lamp” → luminous;
- “a bright student” → intelligent;
- “a bright future” → promising.

Substitute a plain phrase for the target before reading options. This prevents a familiar but irrelevant meaning from taking control.

**Worked example**

In “The judge gave a **sound** decision,” *sound* means **well-founded or reliable**, not audible.

**Self-check**

Choose the synonym of *issue* in “The office will issue new identity cards”: *problem, distribute, edition,* or *result*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Distribute**. Here *issue* is a verb meaning officially provide or release.
</details>

## 2. Match Part of Speech and Sentence Role

The replacement should perform the same grammatical job. *Brief* can be an adjective (“a brief note”), noun (“prepare a brief”), or verb (“brief the team”). An option with a related idea but the wrong form is not an exact replacement.

Word families help: *decide* (verb), *decision* (noun), *decisive* (adjective), and *decisively* (adverb). Check whether the blank modifies a noun, names an idea, or completes a verb phrase.

**Worked example**

For *abrupt* in “an abrupt ending,” **sudden** is a matching adjective. *Suddenly* expresses the related idea but cannot directly modify *ending* in that phrase.

**Self-check**

Choose the best synonym of *retain* in “The wall can retain heat”: *retention, preserve, permanent,* or *reserved*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Preserve** is the matching verb and expresses keeping heat rather than losing it.
</details>

## 3. Compare Connotation, Degree, and Register

Near-synonyms can differ in emotional colour and intensity.

| Word pair | Shared area | Important difference |
|---|---|---|
| economical / cheap | low cost | *cheap* can suggest poor quality; *economical* suggests avoiding waste |
| confident / arrogant | strong self-belief | *arrogant* adds unjustified superiority |
| slim / skinny | thin | *slim* is often favourable; *skinny* may be critical |
| request / beg | ask | *beg* is stronger and more urgent |

Register also matters. *Commence* and *begin* overlap, but *commence* is more formal. In an official notice either may fit; in casual dialogue *begin* is more natural.

**Worked example**

In “The scientist made a **cautious** claim,” **careful** is closer than *fearful*. *Cautious* signals restraint in judgement, not necessarily fear.

**Self-check**

Which best replaces *frugal* in “a frugal household”: *wasteful, thrifty, impoverished,* or *lavish*?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Thrifty**. Both suggest careful use of resources. *Impoverished* means poor and does not imply voluntary economy.
</details>

## 4. Identify the Type of Opposition

Antonyms can relate in different ways:

- **gradable**: hot/cold, generous/stingy; intermediate values exist;
- **complementary**: legal/illegal in a stated legal classification; one excludes the other;
- **relational**: buy/sell, lend/borrow; the same relation viewed from opposite roles;
- **directional**: enter/leave, expand/contract.

Negating a word does not always produce its best antonym. *Not happy* may mean neutral, while *miserable* is strongly negative. Match the degree required by the target.

**Worked example**

The antonym of *scarce* in “scarce rainfall” is **abundant**, not merely *available*.

**Self-check**

Choose the contextual antonym of *rigid* in “a rigid schedule”: *solid, flexible, firm,* or *fixed*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Flexible**. The relevant sense is “not easily changed,” so the opposite allows adjustment.
</details>

## 5. Use Roots and Affixes as Clues, Not Proof

Prefixes can suggest direction: *bene-* relates to good in *beneficial*; *mal-* relates to bad in *malfunction*; *anti-* signals opposition in *antidote*. Suffixes can reveal word class: *-ous* often forms adjectives and *-tion* nouns.

But forms are not perfectly predictable. *Inflammable* means easily set on fire, not “not flammable”; *invaluable* means extremely valuable. Let context and known usage confirm the clue.

Build memory through a small semantic family: *concise* → brief but complete; *verbose* → using more words than needed. A contrast pair with a sample sentence is more durable than a long isolated list.

**Self-check**

The prefix suggests that *benevolent* is closer to *kind* or *hostile*. Which, and why?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Kind**. *Benevolent* describes wishing to do good; the *bene-* clue supports that sense, which context should still confirm.
</details>

## 6. Use a Four-Lock Option Method

For each target:

1. Paraphrase its sense in the sentence.
2. Match part of speech.
3. Compare degree and connotation.
4. Substitute the option and re-read.

Reject an option that is merely associated. *Hospital* is associated with *doctor* but is not a synonym. Reject a broader category when the target is specific: *vehicle* is not an exact synonym of *ambulance*. If the item gives no context, choose the most established core relation and avoid assuming a rare sense.

**Worked example**

For *mitigate* in “Trees can mitigate urban heat,” paraphrase “make less severe.” **Alleviate** fits; *eliminate* is too absolute.

**Self-check**

Why is *silent* not always a synonym of *reserved* when describing a person?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

*Reserved* means restrained or not readily expressive; a reserved person may still speak. *Silent* means not speaking at that time and is narrower.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice and Mastery

### Question 1

Choose the synonym of *plain* in “The instructions were plain”: *ornate, clear, rural,* or *severe*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Clear**. The relevant sense is easy to understand.
</details>

### Question 2

Choose the antonym of *transient* in “a transient problem”: *temporary, recurring, permanent,* or *minor*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Permanent**. *Transient* means lasting only a short time.
</details>

### Question 3

Choose the synonym of *restrain* in “Officers tried to restrain the crowd”: *encourage, hold back, describe,* or *assemble*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Hold back** matches the verb’s sense of limiting movement or action.
</details>

### Question 4

Choose the antonym of *deliberate* in “a deliberate response”: *careful, planned, impulsive,* or *measured*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Impulsive** opposes the sense “carefully considered or intentional.”
</details>

### Question 5

In “The evidence is compelling,” which is closer: *persuasive* or *compulsory*?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Persuasive**. Here *compelling* means convincing; *compulsory* belongs to the separate sense of forcing an action.
</details>
