![Concept map for improving a sentence without changing its meaning](/img/ssc-cgl/sentence-improvement-map.svg)
*Diagnose the underlined unit, predict the repair, and choose the smallest option that restores grammar, clarity, and intended meaning.*

Sentence improvement is more than error detection: several options may be grammatical, but only one may fit the original meaning and construction. Direct items have a clear local repair. Mark and return when an option subtly changes time, agency, comparison, or emphasis.

## 1. Diagnose Before Reading Every Option

Read the complete sentence and identify what the underlined part must do. Check:

1. subject and finite verb;
2. time and aspect;
3. complement or required preposition;
4. pronoun reference and modifier placement;
5. parallel structure and comparison;
6. tone and intended meaning.

Predict a repair in plain language. This reduces attraction to an elegant option that answers a different sentence.

**Worked example**

“The report **discusses about** three causes of delay.” The verb *discuss* takes a direct object, so the minimal repair is “**discusses three causes**.”

**Self-check**

Improve: “The furniture in these rooms **are** new.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **is**. *Furniture* is an uncount noun and is singular in this construction: “The furniture in these rooms **is** new.”
</details>

## 2. Repair Agreement and Time Logic

The verb agrees with the true subject, not a nearby noun. Phrases beginning with *of, with, along with,* and *as well as* do not normally change the head subject.

Choose tense from the relationship between events. Use present perfect for an unfinished period or present relevance without a finished-time marker; use simple past with a completed past time. Use past perfect when an earlier past event needs to be distinguished.

**Worked example**

“Neither the manager nor the assistants **was** available” is improved to “Neither the manager nor the assistants **were** available” under the standard proximity pattern because the nearer subject is plural.

**Self-check**

Improve: “I **have submitted** the form last Friday.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use the simple past with the finished time: “I **submitted** the form last Friday.”
</details>

## 3. Place Modifiers and Clarify Reference

An opening participial phrase should logically modify the subject that follows. “While crossing the road, the bus was seen” makes the bus appear to cross. Write “While crossing the road, **I saw the bus**.”

Pronouns must point clearly to an antecedent. Repetition is preferable to ambiguity. Limiting words such as *only, almost,* and *even* should sit next to what they limit.

**Worked example**

“She almost worked for six hours” normally means she did not quite work. If the intended duration is slightly under six hours, write “She worked for **almost six hours**.”

**Self-check**

Improve: “After reviewing the application, **several omissions were found**.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Supply the reviewer as subject: “After reviewing the application, **the officer found several omissions**.”
</details>

## 4. Preserve Parallelism and Valid Comparison

Coordinate items should share form and level. “The post involves **planning schedules, supervising staff, and to prepare reports**” should use **preparing reports**.

Paired structures must join equivalent units:

| Pair | Parallel example |
|---|---|
| both … and | both accurate and concise |
| either … or | either revise the plan or postpone it |
| not only … but also | not only reduced costs but also improved access |
| rather than | verify the figure rather than guess it |

Compare like with like and make the comparison complete. “The rainfall in Pune is higher than Mumbai” should be “higher than **that in Mumbai**” or “higher than **Mumbai’s rainfall**.”

**Worked example**

“This route is shorter than **any route** in the district” incorrectly includes itself in the comparison. Use “shorter than **any other route** in the district.”

**Self-check**

Improve: “The new process is **more efficient and saves more time** than the old one.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The sentence is grammatical, but a more parallel comparison is “The new process is **more efficient and less time-consuming** than the old one.” Choose this only if the options test parallel adjective form; do not label the original ungrammatical without that context.
</details>

## 5. Prefer Concise, Idiomatic, and Register-Fit Wording

Remove true redundancy: *return back* → *return*; *repeat again* → *repeat*; *the reason is because* → *the reason is that* or simply *because*. Do not remove a word that adds real emphasis or contrast.

Use established complements: *capable of doing, insist on doing, comply with a rule, prefer X to Y*. A formal sentence should avoid needless slang, but plain language is not inferior to inflated wording.

**Worked example**

“Due to the fact that the road was flooded, the bus was delayed” can be improved to “**Because the road was flooded**, the bus was delayed.” The causal meaning is unchanged and the wording is tighter.

**Self-check**

Improve: “The officer **emphasised on** the need for accurate records.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use “The officer **emphasised the need** for accurate records.” *Emphasise* takes a direct object in this sense.
</details>

## 6. Choose No Improvement When the Sentence Works

Do not replace a correct expression merely because another is possible. Standard British and Indian examination English accepts forms such as *at the weekend* and *on the weekend* in their appropriate varieties; an item should provide a genuine grammatical contrast.

Check meaning after inserting each option. An option may repair grammar but alter agency: “The policy reduced costs” is not equivalent to “Costs reduced the policy.” Prefer the smallest complete repair, not the longest option.

**Self-check**

Does this need improvement? “The data suggest that demand has stabilised.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

No improvement is required. In formal plural use, *data suggest* is standard, and the present perfect appropriately describes a present result.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice and Mastery

### Question 1

Improve: “She is senior **than** me.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use the established construction “She is senior **to** me.”
</details>

### Question 2

Improve: “The number of complaints **have fallen**.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **has fallen** because the subject is singular *the number*. Compare “A number of complaints **have** been received.”
</details>

### Question 3

Improve: “No sooner did the bell ring **when** the students left.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **than**: “No sooner did the bell ring **than** the students left.”
</details>

### Question 4

Improve: “The programme aims at improving literacy and **to increase** employment.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use parallel -ing forms: “aims at **improving literacy and increasing employment**.”
</details>

### Question 5

Does this need improvement? “Hardly had we reached the station when the train arrived.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

No improvement is required. The accepted pair is **hardly … when**; the past perfect correctly marks the first event.
</details>
