*Find the sentence core first, then inspect agreement, time logic, reference, placement, and idiomatic form before choosing an error.*

Error spotting is not a hunt for unfamiliar-looking words. Read the whole sentence, identify its intended meaning, and test one relationship at a time. Direct items have a visible agreement or tense mismatch. Mark and return when two analyses are genuinely possible or the answer depends on a subtle change of meaning.

## 1. Match the Verb to the Real Subject

Ignore interrupting phrases when locating the subject. In “The quality of the mangoes **is** good,” the subject is *quality*, not *mangoes*.

| Subject pattern | Usual agreement | Example |
|---|---|---|
| each/every/either/neither + singular noun | singular | Each applicant **has** a card. |
| A as well as B | agrees with A | The captain, as well as the players, **is** ready. |
| either A or B / neither A nor B | usually nearest subject | Neither the teachers nor the principal **is** present. |
| amount, distance, or period viewed as one unit | singular | Ten kilometres **is** a long walk. |

Collective nouns can be singular when the group acts as a unit and plural when members act separately. Examination questions should supply enough context; do not call either form wrong without that distinction.

**Worked example**

“One of the files **are** missing” is incorrect. The subject is *one*, so the verb should be **is**.

**Self-check**

Correct: “The list of shortlisted candidates are on the noticeboard.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **is**: “The list of shortlisted candidates **is** on the noticeboard.” The plural phrase *of shortlisted candidates* does not control the verb.
</details>

## 2. Keep Tense and Aspect Consistent with Time

Choose tense from the time relationship, not from a memorised trigger alone. The simple past reports a completed past event; the present perfect connects a past event to the present and normally does not accompany a finished-time expression such as *yesterday* or *in 2022*.

Use the past perfect only when it helps establish an earlier past event: “The train **had left** before we reached the station.” After *since*, the clause naming the starting point is commonly simple past: “It has been five years since she **moved** here.”

Stative verbs such as *know, believe,* and *belong* are not normally progressive when they express a state. Context can change this: “I am thinking about the proposal” describes an activity, not possession of an opinion.

**Worked example**

“I have met her yesterday” mixes present perfect with a finished past time. Standard correction: “I **met** her yesterday.”

**Self-check**

Correct: “By the time the auditor arrived, the team completed the review.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **had completed** to make the earlier past action explicit: “By the time the auditor arrived, the team **had completed** the review.”
</details>

## 3. Control Articles, Determiners, and Quantity Words

Use *a/an* for one non-specific countable item and *the* for an identifiable or already specified referent. The choice between *a* and *an* follows sound, not spelling: *an honest answer*, but *a university*.

Plural count nouns and uncount nouns can appear without an article when used generally: “Books are useful”; “Information travels quickly.” Do not use *an* directly with an uncount noun: say *a piece of advice*, not *an advice*.

In conventional examination English, *many/few/fewer* modify count nouns, while *much/little/less* modify uncount nouns. *Less* is also idiomatic with measurements viewed as a single quantity, such as “less than ten kilometres.”

**Worked example**

“She gave me an useful suggestion” becomes “She gave me **a** useful suggestion” because *useful* begins with the consonant sound /y/.

**Self-check**

Correct: “There were less errors in the revised report.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **fewer errors** because *errors* is a plural count noun: “There were fewer errors in the revised report.”
</details>

## 4. Make Pronoun Reference and Modifier Placement Clear

A pronoun must have a clear antecedent and the required case. Use subject forms (*I, he, she, we, they*) as subjects and object forms (*me, him, her, us, them*) as objects. In coordinated phrases, temporarily remove the other person: “between you and **me**,” because “between me” is correct.

Singular *they* is standard when gender is unknown or irrelevant: “Every candidate should keep **their** admit card.” Do not manufacture an error merely because an older exercise insists on *his or her*.

Place a modifier next to what it modifies. “Walking through the gate, the rain began” illogically makes *the rain* the walker. A limiting word can also change meaning: “She **only** checked the first page” differs from “She checked **only the first page**.”

**Worked example**

“After reading the email, the mistake was obvious” has a dangling modifier. A clear version is “After reading the email, **Rina noticed the mistake**.”

**Self-check**

Correct: “The manager told Rohan that he would lead the meeting,” when the intended leader is the manager.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The pronoun is ambiguous. Write “The manager told Rohan, ‘**I will lead the meeting**’” or “The manager told Rohan that **the manager** would lead it,” depending on the required style.
</details>

## 5. Preserve Preposition, Parallel, and Comparison Logic

Some adjective–preposition and verb–preposition combinations are conventional: *interested in, responsible for, capable of, comply with,* and *prefer X to Y*. Learn them in phrases and confirm that the meaning fits the sentence.

Items in a series should have matching grammatical form. “The role requires planning, coordination, and **to report**” should end with **reporting**. With paired structures such as *not only … but also*, place the pair before equivalent elements.

Comparisons must compare like with like. “Mumbai’s population is larger than Delhi” compares a population with a city; write “larger than **Delhi’s**.” Use the comparative for two and the superlative for three or more, unless an established construction requires otherwise.

**Worked example**

“She prefers tea **than** coffee” is corrected to “She prefers tea **to** coffee.”

**Self-check**

Correct: “The new software is easier and has greater reliability than the old version.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Make the complements parallel: “The new software is **easier and more reliable** than the old version.”
</details>

## 6. Run a Meaning-First Error Scan

Use this order:

1. Read for intended meaning and time.
2. Find the finite verb and its subject.
3. Check complements, pronouns, and modifiers.
4. Check articles, number, prepositions, and parallel form.
5. Re-read the complete corrected sentence.

Do not “correct” an acceptable sentence merely because another wording sounds more formal. A style preference is not a grammatical error. Likewise, an unfamiliar but standard British form is not wrong in an Indian examination context. Choose *No error* when every relationship works.

**Self-check**

Does this sentence contain an error? “Neither of the two proposals is financially viable.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

No error. *Neither* is singular here, so **is** agrees with it; *financially viable* is also correctly formed.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice and Mastery

### Question 1

Correct the error: “Each of the machines have been inspected.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **has**: “Each of the machines **has** been inspected.” *Each* is the singular subject.
</details>

### Question 2

Correct the error: “She is working here since 2021.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use the present perfect progressive for an action continuing from 2021: “She **has been working** here since 2021.”
</details>

### Question 3

Correct the error: “He is an European diplomat.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **a European diplomat** because *European* begins with the consonant sound /y/.
</details>

### Question 4

Correct the error: “The policy aims to reduce waste, improving safety, and to lower costs.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Make all three items parallel: “The policy aims **to reduce waste, improve safety, and lower costs**.”
</details>

### Question 5

Is there an error? “The committee has submitted its unanimous recommendation.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

No error. The committee acts as one unit, and *its unanimous recommendation* supports singular agreement.
</details>
