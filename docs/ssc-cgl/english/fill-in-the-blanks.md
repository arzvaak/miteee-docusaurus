*Predict the missing idea and grammatical form, then test collocation, logic, tone, and the completed sentence.*

A blank is constrained by both structure and meaning. Do not begin by asking which option is familiar; ask what job the missing word must perform. Direct items have a strong grammar or collocation lock. Mark and return when two options remain possible until the wider sentence or paired blank is resolved.

## 1. Predict the Missing Idea Before Inspecting Options

Read the complete sentence and replace the blank with a plain phrase. Note whether the context is positive, negative, causal, contrasting, formal, or tentative.

“Although the route was longer, it was ___ because it avoided the flooded bridge.” *Although* sets up an apparent disadvantage followed by a practical advantage. A prediction such as “safer/more reliable” is stronger than an unrelated positive word.

**Worked example**

“The instructions were so ___ that even first-time users completed the setup.” The result predicts **clear/easy to follow**. *Clear* fits; *lengthy* does not explain successful first use.

**Self-check**

Choose: “Despite repeated setbacks, the team remained ___ and completed the project”: *persistent, careless, doubtful,* or *accidental*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Persistent**. *Despite repeated setbacks* and *completed* together show continued effort.
</details>

## 2. Lock Part of Speech, Form, and Agreement

Use the words around the blank to predict form.

| Frame | Required form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| article + blank + noun | adjective | a **practical** solution |
| modal + blank | base verb | may **increase** |
| preposition + blank | noun or -ing form | without **causing** delay |
| have/has + blank | past participle | has **improved** |

Then check subject–verb agreement, countability, tense, and comparative form. A semantically related option can fail grammatically: *success* cannot replace *successful* before a noun.

**Worked example**

“The measures have ___ air quality” requires a past participle after *have*. **Improved** fits; *improvement* does not.

**Self-check**

Choose: “Neither of the explanations ___ convincing”: *are, were being, is,* or *have*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Is** in the unmarked present sentence. *Neither* is singular here, and *convincing* is its complement.
</details>

## 3. Test Articles, Prepositions, and Collocation

Articles follow reference and sound: *an honest response*, *a useful tool*, *the report mentioned earlier*. Countability matters: *a suggestion* but *some advice*.

Prepositions often belong to an established construction: *depend on, comply with, interested in, capable of, responsible for*. Learn the complete phrase.

Collocation narrows choices further. We *make a decision, draw attention, raise an objection, meet a deadline,* and *pose a risk*. Another verb may be understandable but not standard in that combination.

**Worked example**

“The committee arrived ___ a decision after two meetings” takes **at**: *arrive at a decision*.

**Self-check**

Choose: “The laboratory adheres ___ strict safety standards”: *with, at, to,* or *for*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **to**: *adhere to standards*.
</details>

## 4. Follow Connectors and Sentence Logic

Connectors encode relationships:

- *because/since* introduce reason;
- *therefore/consequently* introduce result;
- *although/whereas/however* introduce contrast;
- *moreover/furthermore* add support;
- *unless* means “if not” and sets a condition.

Punctuation helps. *However* commonly links independent statements and is set off by punctuation; *although* introduces a dependent clause. Do not choose purely by meaning if the syntax does not fit.

**Worked example**

“The road was closed; ___, the buses were diverted.” **Therefore** fits the cause–result relation and the punctuation.

**Self-check**

Choose: “___ the sample was small, the researchers reported the result cautiously”: *Because, Therefore, Unless,* or *Moreover*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Because** introduces the reason for caution and correctly begins a dependent clause.
</details>

## 5. Match Exact Meaning, Connotation, and Register

Near-synonyms differ in degree and attitude. *Concerned* may show reasonable worry; *alarmed* is stronger. *Economical* suggests efficient use of resources; *cheap* may also imply low quality.

Match the setting. A formal circular may *request* information; an informal speaker may *ask for* it. Avoid an ornate word when a plain option is the normal collocation.

Use selection restrictions: people can be *reluctant*; a plan can be *impractical*; evidence can be *conclusive*. A word may have the right tone but be unsuitable for the noun it describes.

**Self-check**

Choose: “The audit found the records ___, so no firm conclusion could be drawn”: *incomplete, infinite, generous,* or *obedient*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Incomplete**. Missing information directly explains why a firm conclusion was impossible.
</details>

## 6. Solve Paired Blanks as a System

In a double-blank item, do not finalise the first blank alone. Predict both, test option pairs, and re-read the whole sentence. One blank often sets the relation for the other.

Example: “The policy was praised for being ___, but critics questioned whether it was ___.” The contrast suggests a positive quality followed by a limitation, such as *ambitious / practical*. A pair with two unrelated positive words would weaken *but*.

Eliminate in this order:

1. grammar failure in either blank;
2. broken collocation;
3. wrong connector logic;
4. wrong degree or register;
5. repetition or contradiction not intended by the sentence.

**Self-check**

Choose the pair: “The speaker was ___ in tone but ___ in evidence”: (A) calm / weak, (B) calmly / weakness, (C) angry / carefully.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**A: calm / weak**. Both adjectives complete *was*, and *but* contrasts controlled delivery with poor support.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice and Mastery

### Question 1

“The new timetable will ___ students who travel from distant villages”: *benefit, beneficial, benefit from,* or *benefits*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Benefit**. After *will*, use the base verb; here it directly takes *students* as object.
</details>

### Question 2

“She gave me ___ useful piece of advice”: *a, an, the,* or no article.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**A**. *Useful* begins with the consonant sound /y/, and *piece* is a singular count noun.
</details>

### Question 3

“The evidence was limited; ___, the claim was presented as provisional”: *consequently, nevertheless, similarly,* or *otherwise*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Consequently**. The limited evidence causes the cautious presentation.
</details>

### Question 4

“The manager insisted ___ checking every entry before publication”: *on, to, for,* or *with*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**On**. The construction is *insist on + noun/-ing form*.
</details>

### Question 5

Choose the pair: “The proposal is financially ___ but technically ___”: (A) viable / demanding, (B) viability / demand, (C) viable / demandingly.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**A**. Both adjectives complement *is* and express a meaningful contrast: affordable in financial terms but difficult to execute technically.
</details>
