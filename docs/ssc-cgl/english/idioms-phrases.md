*Separate literal from figurative use, preserve the expression’s form, and choose the meaning that fits the full sentence.*

An idiom’s overall meaning cannot usually be calculated word by word. Yet context, grammar, and imagery still help. Direct items use an established expression in a clear situation. Mark and return when the phrase may be literal or figurative until the surrounding action resolves it.

## 1. Decide Whether the Phrase Is Figurative

First read the sentence without replacing the phrase. Ask whether a literal reading is physically and logically possible.

“Ravi broke the ice before the workshop by inviting everyone to introduce themselves” uses **break the ice** figuratively: he eased initial social tension. In “The ship broke the ice with its reinforced bow,” the same words are literal.

Do not choose a meaning merely because one word appears in both the idiom and the option. The unit *spill the beans* means reveal a secret; it is not about food or dropping objects.

**Worked example**

“Her brief joke helped **break the ice** at the meeting” means **make people feel more relaxed and willing to talk**.

**Self-check**

What does *hit the nail on the head* mean in “Your diagnosis hit the nail on the head”?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

It means **identified the matter exactly or said precisely the right thing**.
</details>

## 2. Use Situation, Cause, and Result as Clues

The clause before or after an idiom often paraphrases it. Contrast words can also reveal direction.

“The team was **at sixes and sevens**, so nobody knew which version to submit.” The result shows disorder or confusion. “The delay **added fuel to the fire**; the already angry customers became more upset.” The second clause shows that the situation worsened.

**Worked example**

“He visits the remote office **once in a blue moon**, perhaps once every few years.” The frequency clue confirms **very rarely**.

**Self-check**

Infer *under the weather*: “Nila stayed home because she was feeling under the weather.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

It means **slightly ill or unwell**. The decision to stay home supports that reading.
</details>

## 3. Preserve the Established Form and Grammar

Many idioms allow normal tense or pronoun changes but keep their key words and prepositions. We say *take something with a pinch of salt*, *keep an eye on*, and *make ends meet*. Changing the fixed component may destroy the idiom.

The expression still occupies a grammatical role. In “They **called it a day**,” the idiom is the verb phrase and changes tense normally: “We will call it a day.” In “a **blessing in disguise**,” the idiom functions as a noun phrase.

Varieties of English sometimes have accepted variants, such as *a pinch/grain of salt*. An examination item should not mark a standard variant wrong unless the options clearly test a fixed local form.

**Worked example**

“Please **keep an eye on** my bag” means watch it, and the preposition **on** is part of the established phrase.

**Self-check**

Correct the phrase: “After finishing the urgent tasks, we called for a day.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The idiom is **called it a day**, meaning stopped working for that period.
</details>

## 4. Distinguish Idioms, Phrasal Verbs, and Proverbs

An idiom is a fixed figurative unit; a phrasal verb combines a verb with a particle, often creating a special meaning; a proverb states a general piece of traditional wisdom.

| Type | Example | Meaning in context |
|---|---|---|
| idiom | beat around the bush | avoid the main point |
| phrasal verb | put off the meeting | postpone the meeting |
| proverb | A stitch in time saves nine. | early action prevents greater trouble |

Some boundaries overlap, but the task is usually to identify meaning, not argue over labels. For a phrasal verb, check whether it is separable: *put off the meeting* / *put it off*, but not *look the matter into* for *look into the matter*.

**Self-check**

What does *look into* mean in “The committee will look into the complaint”?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

It means **investigate or examine** the complaint.
</details>

## 5. Match Meaning, Tone, and Register

Idioms can be informal, neutral, humorous, or critical. *Kick the bucket* means die but is informal and can sound insensitive; *pass away* is a polite euphemism. A formal notice is unlikely to use a comic idiom unless quoting someone.

Choose the option with the right intensity. *A storm in a teacup* is a great fuss over a small matter; it does not describe every disagreement. *The last straw* is the final additional problem that exhausts someone’s patience, not simply any serious problem.

**Worked example**

“The missing signature was **the last straw** after weeks of repeated errors.” It was the **final irritation that made the situation intolerable**.

**Self-check**

What does *by and large* mean in “By and large, residents supported the plan”?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

It means **generally or on the whole**, not unanimously.
</details>

## 6. Eliminate Literal, Partial, and Reversed Options

Use four checks:

1. Does the option describe the whole expression rather than one word?
2. Does it fit the event’s cause and result?
3. Does it preserve intensity and attitude?
4. Does the completed sentence remain grammatical?

Common distractors are literal images, meanings of a related but different idiom, and reversals. *Let the cat out of the bag* means reveal a secret, while *keep something under one’s hat* means keep it secret; they point in opposite directions.

**Self-check**

Why does *work very late* fit *burn the midnight oil* better than *waste electricity*?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The expression conventionally means **work or study late into the night**. *Waste electricity* follows the image literally but misses the established meaning.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice and Mastery

### Question 1

Meaning of “The manager asked him to stop **beating around the bush**.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

He should **stop avoiding the main point and speak directly**.
</details>

### Question 2

Meaning of “The rumour **added fuel to the fire**.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The rumour **made an already bad or angry situation worse**.
</details>

### Question 3

Meaning of “Do not **take his prediction at face value**.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Do not accept it **without questioning or checking it**.
</details>

### Question 4

Meaning of “Finding the missing records was **a blessing in disguise**.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Something that first seemed unfortunate ultimately **produced a beneficial result**.
</details>

### Question 5

Meaning of “Everyone avoided discussing **the elephant in the room**.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

They avoided an **obvious and important problem that people were reluctant to acknowledge**.
</details>
