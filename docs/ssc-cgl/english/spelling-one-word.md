*Break a word into meaningful parts, check the suffix boundary, and match a substitution to every part of its definition.*

Spelling is more reliable when tied to structure and word family than when guessed from sound. One-word substitution is a definition-matching task, not a display of rare vocabulary. Direct items have a clear morpheme or definition lock. Mark and return when a definition is broader than every familiar option.

## 1. See the Word’s Structure, Not Just Its Sound

English spelling preserves roots and relationships even when pronunciation shifts. *Sign* keeps the *g* visible in *signature*; *define* connects to *definition*. Say the word, but also inspect prefix, root, and suffix.

Common prefixes usually retain their identity: *mis + spell = misspell* has *s* at the end of the prefix and another at the start of the root. *Dis + satisfy = dissatisfy* behaves similarly.

**Worked example**

*Necessary* can be anchored as **one c, two s’s** and linked to *necessity*. The family check is more dependable than stretching the pronunciation.

**Self-check**

Which is correct: *accomodation* or *accommodation*?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Accommodation** has double *c* and double *m*. Link it to *accommodate* rather than relying on sound alone.
</details>

## 2. Apply Suffix Changes with Conditions

When adding a suffix:

- a final silent *e* often drops before a vowel suffix: *hope → hoping*;
- final consonant + *y* usually changes to *i* before many suffixes: *happy → happiness, carry → carried*;
- keep *y* before *-ing*: *carry → carrying*;
- keep *e* when dropping it would change pronunciation or obscure form in words such as *noticeable* and *courageous*.

Rules have exceptions, so confirm with a familiar family. *True → truly* drops the *e*; *due → duly* changes more than a simple attachment.

**Worked example**

*Apply + -cation* becomes **application**. The spelling follows the established derived form, not *applycation*.

**Self-check**

Form the noun from *happy* using *-ness*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Happiness**. Change consonant + *y* to *i* before *-ness*.
</details>

## 3. Double Final Consonants by Stress and Pattern

A one-syllable word ending consonant–vowel–consonant often doubles the final consonant before a vowel suffix: *run → running, big → bigger*. In longer words, doubling commonly depends on stress falling on the final syllable: *begin → beginning, prefer → preferred*.

Do not normally double final *w, x,* or *y*: *snowing, fixed, playing*. A final consonant is not doubled when stress is earlier: *visit → visited, open → opening*.

British spelling often doubles *l* in forms such as **travelling, travelled, traveller**, while standard American spelling commonly uses one *l*. Use the British/Indian examination form consistently unless the question explicitly tests a variety.

**Worked example**

*Occur + -ence* becomes **occurrence**: double *c* in *occur* remains, and the stressed final consonant is doubled before the suffix.

**Self-check**

Choose the standard British form: *travelling* or *traveling*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **travelling** in standard British examination English. *Traveling* is standard American spelling, not a universally incorrect form.
</details>

## 4. Handle Vowel Sequences and Confusable Words Carefully

The slogan “i before e except after c” has too many exceptions to function as a complete rule. Learn stable families: *believe/belief, receive/reception, deceive/deception,* and *seize/seizure*.

Meaning distinguishes homophones and near-confusables:

| Word | Meaning | Contrast |
|---|---|---|
| complement | something that completes or matches | compliment = praise |
| principal | main; or head of an institution | principle = rule or belief |
| stationary | not moving | stationery = writing materials |
| affect | usually a verb meaning influence | effect = usually a noun meaning result |

Part of speech is a clue, but some words have additional uses. *Effect* can be a formal verb meaning bring about; context decides.

**Self-check**

Choose: “The new lighting had a positive affect/effect on safety.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Use **effect**, the noun meaning result. *Had a positive effect* is the complete phrase.
</details>

## 5. Match Every Part of a One-Word Definition

Find the definition’s head:

- “a person who …” requires a person noun;
- “the practice/system of …” requires a process or system;
- “capable of being …” often requires an adjective;
- “a place where …” requires a place noun.

Then test scope. An **ornithologist** studies birds; a *zoologist* studies animals more broadly. **Anonymous** means of unknown or undisclosed name; *pseudonymous* means using a false name. Related is not exact.

**Worked example**

“A person who compiles dictionaries” is a **lexicographer**. *Linguist* is broader and does not specifically name dictionary compilation.

**Self-check**

Choose the precise word for “a statement that appears self-contradictory but may contain truth.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

A **paradox**. A *contradiction* simply asserts incompatible claims; a paradox has the specified appearance and possible insight.
</details>

## 6. Verify Form, Meaning, and Register

For spelling:

1. identify the base and suffix;
2. apply any *e, y,* or doubling change;
3. compare with a known family;
4. check British spelling consistency.

For substitution:

1. identify person/place/system/quality/action;
2. underline limiting words such as *cannot, all, official,* or *unknown*;
3. reject a broader, narrower, or differently toned option;
4. insert the word into a sentence.

Avoid selecting a rare word merely because it looks specialised. Precision beats obscurity.

**Self-check**

Why is *illegible* a better match than *invisible* for “writing that cannot be read”?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Illegible** specifically means not clear enough to read. *Invisible* means unable to be seen at all and is broader in a different direction.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice and Mastery

### Question 1

Choose the correct spelling: *separate* or *seperate*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Separate**. The middle vowel is *a*; link it to *separation*.
</details>

### Question 2

Choose the correct form: “The permit was renewed after an unexpected ___”: *occurence* or *occurrence*.

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Occurrence** has double *c* and double *r*.
</details>

### Question 3

Choose the precise word for “one who studies birds.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Ornithologist**.
</details>

### Question 4

Choose the precise word for “a person who cannot read or write.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Illiterate** in the intended educational sense. Do not confuse it with *illegible*, which describes writing that is hard to read.
</details>

### Question 5

Choose: “Please accept this as a compliment/complement on your clear presentation.”

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Compliment** means an expression of praise.
</details>
