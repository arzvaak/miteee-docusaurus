*Identify the passage’s main movement, locate evidence, and reject options that add, distort, or overstate what the writer says.*

Reading comprehension tests disciplined interpretation, not outside knowledge. A correct answer must be supported by the passage. Direct detail questions can be answered from a precise line. Mark and return when two inference options survive until you compare their strength and scope.

## 1. Find the Central Idea, Not Merely the Topic

The topic names what the passage discusses; the central idea states what the writer says about it. “Urban transport” is a topic. “Reliable buses can reduce congestion only when routes connect people to where they actually travel” is a central idea.

Watch the passage’s movement: problem → cause → response; claim → evidence → qualification; old view → new view; or process → consequence. The main idea must cover the whole passage rather than one vivid example.

**Worked example**

If a paragraph describes how a lake was polluted, explains the effect on fishing, and ends with restoration measures, “Fishing in the lake” is too narrow. “Pollution damaged the lake’s ecology and livelihood, prompting restoration” covers the full movement.

**Self-check**

A passage says that digital records improve access but require good indexing and privacy controls. Which is the better title: *Computers in Offices* or *Benefits and Conditions of Digital Records*?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**Benefits and Conditions of Digital Records**. It includes both the advantage and the qualifications; *Computers in Offices* is broader and less informative.
</details>

## 2. Answer Detail and Reference Questions Precisely

For an explicit-detail question, locate the relevant sentence and read one line before and after it. Options often copy words from the passage while reversing a relationship or attaching a detail to the wrong person.

Reference questions ask what words such as *this, they, former,* or *such a change* point to. Replace the reference word with each possible antecedent and test grammar and meaning. The nearest noun is not automatically correct.

| Option defect | What it does |
|---|---|
| reversal | swaps cause and effect or before and after |
| scope shift | turns *some* into *all* or a local claim into a general one |
| wrong attachment | assigns a fact to the wrong person, place, or period |
| half-truth | repeats one true phrase but adds an unsupported claim |

**Worked example**

Passage: “The library extended weekend hours after students requested more study space.” Question: Why were hours extended? Answer: **because students requested more study space**, not simply because the library was busy.

**Self-check**

Passage: “The old bridge was narrow and costly to repair. This led the council to approve a replacement.” What does *This* refer to?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

It refers to the combined problem that the bridge was **narrow and costly to repair**, which motivated the replacement decision.
</details>

## 3. Infer Only What the Evidence Requires

An inference is an unstated conclusion that follows reasonably from stated facts. It must require little or no extra assumption. Distinguish:

- **must follow**: supported by the passage’s facts;
- **may be true**: possible, but not established;
- **contradicted**: conflicts with the passage;
- **outside scope**: addresses something the passage does not discuss.

Extreme words such as *always, never, entirely,* and *only* need equally strong evidence. A cautious passage rarely supports an absolute option.

**Worked example**

Passage: “After the town introduced separate food-waste collection, the volume sent to landfill fell, although total household waste changed little.” A sound inference is that **some waste was diverted from landfill**. It does not prove that households produced less waste overall.

**Self-check**

Passage: “Mira carried an umbrella despite the clear morning sky after checking the afternoon forecast.” What can be inferred?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Mira expected that rain **might occur later**. The sentence does not prove that it eventually rained.
</details>

## 4. Identify Tone and Purpose from Language

Tone is the writer’s attitude; purpose is what the writer is trying to do. Use textual signals rather than mood guesses.

| Tone | Typical evidence |
|---|---|
| analytical | weighs causes, evidence, or alternatives |
| critical | points out defects with reasons |
| cautiously optimistic | recognises difficulties but expects improvement |
| persuasive | urges acceptance or action |

Words such as *perhaps, appears,* and *may* signal qualification; *must, urgent,* and *cannot ignore* can signal advocacy.

**Worked example**

“The pilot programme is promising, but its small sample and short duration make firm conclusions premature.” The tone is **cautiously evaluative**, not celebratory or dismissive.

**Self-check**

What is the main purpose of a paragraph that defines heat islands, explains why they form, and lists two consequences?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Its purpose is **to explain** the phenomenon and its effects. Unless it urges a remedy, *to persuade authorities to act* goes beyond the paragraph.
</details>

## 5. Derive Vocabulary Meaning from Local Context

Replace the target word with a plain phrase, then test options in the sentence. Use contrast, example, cause, and definition clues. The required answer must match the word’s part of speech and its contextual sense.

A familiar word can carry an unfamiliar sense. In “The report **qualified** its conclusion,” *qualified* means limited or made less absolute, not certified. Connotation also matters: *persistent* may be approving in one context and critical in another.

**Worked example**

“The first estimate was **tentative**, because several surveys were incomplete.” The explanation after *because* shows that *tentative* means **provisional or not yet certain**.

**Self-check**

In “The new evidence undermined the earlier explanation,” what does *undermined* mean?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

It means **weakened the support or credibility of** the earlier explanation.
</details>

## 6. Build an Evidence Ladder and Eliminate Options

Use a repeatable sequence:

1. Label the question: detail, central idea, inference, tone, purpose, reference, or vocabulary.
2. Predict a short answer before comparing options.
3. Locate the supporting line or passage movement.
4. Reject contradiction, distortion, excessive scope, and outside knowledge.
5. Prefer the option that says exactly enough.

For “according to the passage” questions, do not debate the writer with external facts. For a title, reject options that are too narrow, too broad, or sensational. For inference, ask, “What additional assumption does this option require?” The option needing the least unsupported assumption is usually stronger.

**Self-check**

Two options are: (A) “The scheme helped every resident” and (B) “The scheme improved access for residents in the surveyed wards.” The passage reports improvement only in three surveyed wards. Which survives?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**B**. It preserves the passage’s limited scope. A changes a local finding into a universal claim.
</details>

## 7. Mixed Practice — Passage Mastery

Read the passage:

“Public clocks once performed a civic function beyond telling time. In towns where few people owned watches, a clock tower coordinated markets, school hours, and transport. Personal devices later made that shared signal less necessary. Yet several cities continue to maintain historic clocks. Their value now lies partly in identity: residents use them as meeting points, and visitors recognise them as landmarks. Maintenance is not effortless; old mechanisms require specialist care, and a stopped clock quickly becomes a symbol of neglect. For this reason, some cities have fitted discreet electronic controls while preserving the visible face and hands. The compromise does not satisfy every preservationist, but it keeps the clock accurate and the streetscape familiar.”

### Question 1

What is the central idea?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Historic public clocks have shifted from essential shared timekeepers to civic landmarks, and cities balance authenticity with reliable maintenance.
</details>

### Question 2

Why did clock towers once coordinate daily activity?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

Few people owned personal watches, so the public clock provided a common time signal.
</details>

### Question 3

What does “the compromise” refer to?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

It refers to using discreet electronic controls while retaining the historic clock’s visible face and hands.
</details>

### Question 4

Which inference is justified: (A) every preservationist opposes electronic controls, or (B) electronic controls can improve accuracy without greatly changing outward appearance?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

**B** is supported. The passage says the controls are discreet and keep the clock accurate. It says the solution does not satisfy *every* preservationist, not that all oppose it.
</details>

### Question 5

What is the passage’s tone?

<details>
<summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

The tone is **balanced and explanatory**: it describes historical value, practical difficulty, and a contested compromise without urging one absolute position.
</details>
