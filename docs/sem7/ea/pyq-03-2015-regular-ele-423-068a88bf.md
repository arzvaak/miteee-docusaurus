# Historical PYQ 03 - 2015 Regular (ELE 423)

## Question 1

### 1A (05)

**Source wording:** A centrifugal pump operates at 30 m3/hr and at 1440 RPM. The pump operating efficiency is 65% and the motor efficiency is 89%. The discharge pressure gauge shows a reading of 3.4 kg/cm 2. The suction is 3m below the pump centerline. If the speed of the pump is reduced by 25%, estimate the following
a) Pump Flow
b) Pump Head
c) Motor Power
Assume the efficiency of motor & pump remains same at reduced speeds.

**Topic:** Pump affinity laws; hydraulic-to-motor power chain.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**
Reduced speed: N₂ = 1440 × (1 − 0.25) = 1080 RPM, so N₂/N₁ = 0.75.
a) Flow: Q₂ = Q₁ × (N₂/N₁) = 30 × 0.75 = 22.5 m³/hr.
b) Head: taking 1 kg/cm² ≈ 10 m of water column, initial head H₁ = (3.4 × 10) + 3 (suction lift below pump centerline) = 34 + 3 = 37 m. Then H₂ = H₁ × (N₂/N₁)² = 37 × 0.75² = 37 × 0.5625 = 20.8125 m.
c) Motor Power: hydraulic power = ρgQ₂H₂ = 1000 × 9.81 × (22.5/3600) × 20.8125 ≈ 1276 W ≈ 1.276 kW. Pump shaft power = 1.276/0.65 ≈ 1.963 kW. Motor input power = 1.963/0.89 ≈ 2.21 kW.

**Final answer:** a) Pump flow ≈ 22.5 m³/hr; b) Pump head ≈ 20.81 m; c) Motor power ≈ 2.21 kW.

**Verification:** At full speed, hydraulic power = 1000 × 9.81 × (30/3600) × 37 ≈ 3.025 kW → motor power = 3.025/(0.65 × 0.89) ≈ 5.23 kW; affinity laws give power ∝ N³, so 5.23 × 0.75³ = 5.23 × 0.421875 ≈ 2.21 kW, matching part (c).

### 1B (02)

**Source wording:** Explain how energy efficient motors reduce its intrinsic losses.

**Topic:** Loss reduction in energy-efficient induction motors.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**
Energy efficient motors cut each intrinsic loss by design/material improvements: stator copper (I²R) loss is reduced by using larger conductor cross-sections and shorter end turns; iron (core) loss is reduced by using low-loss, thinner-gauge high-grade electrical steel and increased core length/lower flux density; rotor losses are lowered by improved rotor bar/slot design (e.g., die-cast copper bars); stray load losses are minimized by optimized slot/air-gap geometry and better winding distribution; friction and windage losses are cut with better bearings and aerodynamically efficient fans. Lower total losses also mean lower operating temperature, further improving life and efficiency.

**Final answer:** They reduce losses through bigger conductors (less copper loss), superior low-loss laminations/core design (less iron loss), improved rotor bars (less rotor loss), optimized slotting/air gap (less stray loss), and better bearings/fans (less friction-windage loss).

**Verification:** These five categories cover the complete loss breakdown of an induction motor (stator Cu + rotor Cu + iron + stray + mechanical), so the explanation accounts for every intrinsic loss component.

### 1C (03)

**Source wording:** For complete combustion of 1 kg of a typical coal 12 kg of air is required. Calorific value of coal is 4200 kCal/kg with ash content of 22%. What is the quantity (in kg) flue gas generated by burning 5 kg coal?

**Topic:** Combustion mass balance for flue gas quantity.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**
By overall mass balance, the flue gas leaving equals the fuel fired plus the combustion air supplied (the calorific value does not affect mass): per kg of coal, flue gas = 1 + 12 = 13 kg. For 5 kg of coal, air supplied = 5 × 12 = 60 kg, so flue gas = 5 + 60 = 65 kg.

**Final answer:** 65 kg of flue gas.

**Verification:** Mass balance check: mass in = 5 kg coal + 60 kg air = 65 kg = mass of combustion products; the stated CV (4200 kCal/kg) has no bearing on mass, confirming it is extraneous. (Note: if the 22% ash — 1.1 kg — is instead taken to leave separately as solid bottom ash excluded from the flue gas stream, the flue gas would be 65 − 1.1 = 63.9 kg.)

### 3A

**Source wording**

> 3A. The exterior areas of a Compressor House are illuminated by twenty wall-mounted 1000W Tungsten Halogen, single lamp, luminaires. The lamps burn 12 hours a day, throughout the year. The energy and cost savings that could be realized by changing to a more efficient light source were investigated.
>
> With reference to data given below suggest the suitable retrofit for annual energy saving and the simple payback period.
>
> | Lamp Source | Luminous Flux (lm) | Cost/Lamp (INR) | Rated Life (hrs) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 1000 W Tungsten Halogen | 22700 | 5000 | 1350 |
> | 250 W HPSV | 24600 | 6500 | 6000 |
> | 400 W Metal Halide | 27000 | 6000 | 8000 |
>
> Assume Plant Operating Hours: 12 hours per day, 365 days per year & Electricity Costs: Rs 7.00/kWh    (05)

**Topic:** Lighting energy audit — selection of a suitable lamp retrofit, annual energy/cost saving and simple payback period.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Luminous efficacy of each source:
- 1000 W Tungsten Halogen: 22700 / 1000 = 22.7 lm/W
- 250 W HPSV: 24600 / 250 = 98.4 lm/W
- 400 W Metal Halide: 27000 / 400 = 67.5 lm/W

Suitable retrofit: **250 W HPSV** — highest efficacy, and its 24600 lm per point exceeds the existing 22700 lm, so illuminance is maintained.

Operating hours/year = 12 × 365 = 4380 h.

| Quantity | Existing (1000 W TH) | Retrofit (250 W HPSV) |
|---|---|---|
| Connected load (20 points) | 20 × 1000 W = 20 kW | 20 × 250 W = 5 kW |
| Annual energy consumption | 20 × 4380 = 87600 kWh | 5 × 4380 = 21900 kWh |

Annual energy saving = 87600 − 21900 = 65700 kWh/year.
Annual cost saving = 65700 × Rs 7.00 = Rs 459900/year.
Investment (existing lamp cost treated as sunk) = 20 × Rs 6500 = Rs 130000.
Simple payback = 130000 / 459900 = 0.28 year ≈ 3.4 months.

Notes: at 4380 h/year the TH lamp needs replacement about every 1350 h (~3.2 times/year) versus every 6000 h (~0.73 times/year) for HPSV, so additional lamp-replacement savings exist but cannot be quantified from the given data. If only the incremental lamp cost (Rs 6500 − 5000 = 1500 × 20 = Rs 30000) were charged, payback = 30000 / 459900 ≈ 0.065 year (~24 days).

**Final answer:** Retrofit all 20 points with 250 W HPSV; annual energy saving = 65700 kWh; annual cost saving = Rs 459900; investment = Rs 130000; simple payback ≈ 0.28 year (~3.4 months).

**Verification:** Per-point cross-check: (1.0 − 0.25) kW × 4380 h × 20 = 65700 kWh ✓. Cost check: 65700 × 7.00 = 459900 ✓. Payback check: 130000 / 459900 = 0.2827 ✓, well under one year, consistent with the large saving-to-investment ratio. Illuminance check: 24600 lm > 22700 lm per point ✓.

### 3B

**Source wording**

> 3B. How is energy savings achieved through electronic ballast in a fluorescent tube light in comparison to the conventional magnetic ballast?    (02)

**Topic:** Energy-efficient lighting auxiliaries — electronic versus electromagnetic (conventional magnetic) ballasts.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Energy savings arise because:
- Loss substitution: the iron-cored magnetic choke, which has continuous copper (I²R) and iron/core losses, is replaced by low-loss electronic circuitry; ballast dissipation falls (typically from about 10–15 W to about 3–5 W per tube).
- High-frequency operation: the lamp runs at roughly 20–60 kHz instead of 50 Hz, which raises the discharge's luminous efficacy, so the same lumen output is delivered from less lamp power; flicker and electrode losses are also reduced.
- Improved power factor: built-in correction raises input power factor towards unity (versus roughly 0.5 lagging for a magnetic ballast), reducing line current and associated cable/distribution losses.

Combined effect: for equal light output the fixture draws appreciably less input power (commonly around 25–30% less), with cooler operation extending lamp life.

**Final answer:** Savings come from (i) near-lossless electronic circuitry replacing the lossy choke winding/core, (ii) high-frequency lamp operation that improves luminous efficacy, and (iii) near-unity corrected power factor that lowers line losses — together reducing input power for the same light output compared with a conventional magnetic ballast.

**Verification:** Qualitative (02-mark) question; the three mechanisms are independent, jointly covering ballast loss, lamp efficiency and system-level power factor, and are consistent with standard published ballast performance behaviour; no numeric source data were required.

### 3C

**Source wording**

> 3C. What is Demand Side Management? Briefly list down the benefits of Demand Side Management with two examples.    (03)

**Topic:** Demand Side Management (DSM) — definition, benefits and examples.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Definition: Demand Side Management is the planning, implementation and monitoring of utility activities designed to influence the way customers use electricity, modifying the magnitude and time pattern (load shape) of demand to reduce peak demand and/or total consumption while maintaining the level of service to the consumer.

Benefits:
- Lower system peak demand, deferring investment in new generation and transmission/distribution capacity.
- Reduced electricity bills for participating consumers.
- Improved load factor, better utilisation of existing assets and greater supply reliability.
- Environmental benefits through avoided fuel consumption and emissions.
- Capacity freed up becomes available for other consumers/economic growth.

Two examples:
1. Time-of-day (off-peak) tariffs that shift agricultural pump-set and industrial loads to night hours (load shifting away from peak).
2. Utility-driven bulk replacement of incandescent lamps with CFLs/LEDs (strategic conservation clipping the evening peak).

**Final answer:** DSM is planned utility action to influence customers' electricity-use patterns (reducing or reshaping demand); benefits include peak reduction, deferred capacity investment, bill savings, higher load factor/reliability and emission reductions — illustrated by off-peak tariff load-shifting and CFL/LED replacement programmes.

**Verification:** Definition corresponds to the standard DSM load-shape objectives (peak clipping, valley filling, load shifting, strategic conservation); exactly two examples are provided as the question demands; no data beyond standard theory were assumed.

## Question 4

### 4A

**Source wording**

> 4A.   What is the aim of the Electricity Act, 2003? Explain reforms brought by the Electricity Act, 2003 in the areas of Generation, Transmission & Distribution. (05) Reforms in Tariff principles & the Role of CEA

**Topic:** Indian power-sector legislation — objectives of the Electricity Act, 2003 and its reforms in Generation, Transmission, Distribution, tariff principles, and the role of the Central Electricity Authority (CEA).

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

*Aim:* To replace the multiplicity of earlier laws (Indian Electricity Act 1910, Electricity (Supply) Act 1948, Electricity Regulatory Commissions Act 1998) with a single legislation consolidating the law relating to generation, transmission, distribution, trading and use of electricity; to promote competition; protect interests of consumers; ensure supply of electricity to all areas; rationalise electricity tariffs; ensure transparent policies regarding subsidies; and create a conducive environment for investment in the power sector.

*Reforms — Generation:* Generation is de-licensed (freely permitted to any person/company); only hydro generation needs prior concurrence of the CEA (for river utilisation and dam safety); captive generation is freely permitted; generating companies may sell to any licensee/consumer through open access.

*Reforms — Transmission:* Transmission is a licensed activity with open access mandated; unbundling of State Electricity Boards separates transmission (State Transco) from other businesses; creation of Central/State Transmission Utilities; independent load dispatch centres for grid discipline.

*Reforms — Distribution:* Distribution separated into wire (wheeling) and supply businesses; phased open access in distribution; multiple licences permitted in the same area; mandatory metering; standards of performance with compensation to consumers; universal service obligation to supply on demand.

*Reforms in Tariff principles (Sections 61–64):* Tariffs must progressively reflect cost of supply, reward efficiency in performance, avoid undue cross-subsidy, be guided by commercial principles without returning excessive profit, and safeguards for consumers; State Government subsidies, if any, must be declared and paid in advance; Multi-Year Tariff frameworks encouraged; regulatory commissions (CERC/SERCs) determine tariff, with an Appellate Tribunal for appeals.

*Role of CEA:* Statutory technical adviser to the Government; assists in formulating the National Electricity Policy and National Electricity Plan; develops safety and grid standards; specifies technical standards for construction of electrical plants, electric lines and connectivity; concurrence for hydro-electric schemes; collects and publishes power-sector statistics.

**Final answer:** The Act's aim is consolidation of electricity law with competition, consumer protection, universal supply, rational tariffs and investment climate; key reforms are de-licensed/captive generation, open-access transmission with independent dispatch, unbundled distribution with metering and performance standards, cost-of-supply-based transparent tariffs with advance-paid subsidies, and the CEA acting as the apex technical/policy-planning adviser.

**Verification:** Points map one-to-one onto the Act's Preamble/objectives and its Parts (generation, licensing, open access, Sections 61–64 tariff principles, Section 73 CEA functions), so coverage matches the three named areas plus tariff and CEA asked in the question.

### 4B

**Source wording**

> 4B.   A 37.5 kW rated induction motor burns out in a process plant. The financial manager of the plant wants to rewind the motor for Rs.10000 to save money. The Energy Manager wants to buy a new energy efficient motor for Rs.50,000/- after selling burnt out motor for Rs. 8,000. He claims that he can save much more money in the next five years than the cost difference of the above two options.
> Other data is as under:
> Operating hours/year = 7800; Rewound motor efficiency at 75% loading = 89%; New energy efficient motor efficiency at 75% loading = 94%; Motor loading = 75%; Electricity cost =Rs 5/kWh
>
> a) How much money does the energy manager actually save over 5 years and what is the simple pay back period ?
> b) The financial manager claims the financial risk is still too high because operating hours may go down drastically from 7800 to 3000 hrs in the next 5 years. The energy manager still insists that even if the motor’s annual operating hours become 1/3 rd of the annual 3000 operating hours as predicted by financial manager, the proposal will still recover the cost difference within next 5 years (ignoring interest burden). Do you agree with the energy manager’s claim? (05)

**Topic:** Economics of replacing a failed induction motor with an energy-efficient motor — shaft-power-based input comparison, incremental investment, simple payback, and sensitivity to reduced operating hours.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Shaft output at 75% loading = 37.5 × 0.75 = 28.125 kW (identical for both motors).
Input, rewound motor = 28.125 / 0.89 = 31.601 kW; Input, new motor = 28.125 / 0.94 = 29.920 kW.
Power saving = 31.601 − 29.920 = 1.681 kW.

*a)* At 7800 h/year: annual energy saving = 1.681 × 7800 = 13,111 kWh; annual money saving = 13,111 × 5 = Rs 65,556.
Gross saving over 5 years = 65,556 × 5 = Rs 327,778.
Incremental cost of the new-motor option = Rs 50,000 − Rs 8,000 (salvage) − Rs 10,000 (rewind avoided) = Rs 32,000.
Net saving over 5 years = 327,778 − 32,000 = **Rs 295,778**.
Simple payback = 32,000 / 65,556 = 0.488 year ≈ **5.9 months**.

*b)* Strict reading of the claim — hours fall to 1/3 of 3000 = 1000 h/year: annual saving = 1.681 × 1000 × 5 = Rs 8,405/year; over 5 years = Rs 42,023 > Rs 32,000, so the cost difference is still recovered (payback = 32,000 / 8,405 = 3.81 years < 5 years). Even at the full 3000 h/year, saving = 1.681 × 3000 × 5 = Rs 25,214/year and payback = 1.27 years. Hence the energy manager's claim holds under either reading.

**Final answer:** a) Net saving over 5 years ≈ Rs 295,778 (gross ≈ Rs 327,778) with a simple payback of ≈ 0.49 year (about 5.9 months). b) Yes — the claim is agreed with; break-even is only ≈ 762 operating hours/year, so recovery within 5 years survives even a drop to 1000 h/year.

**Verification:** Reverse checks: 31.601 × 0.89 = 28.125 kW and 29.920 × 0.94 = 28.125 kW confirm equal shaft outputs; 0.488 year × Rs 65,556 ≈ Rs 32,000 confirms the payback; break-even hours = 32,000 ÷ (1.681 kW × Rs 5 × 5 yr) ≈ 762 h/year, below both 3000 h/year and 1000 h/year, confirming part b).

### 5A

**Source wording:**

> An efficiency assessment test was carried out for a standard squirrel cage induction motor in a process plant. The motor specifications are as under:-
> Motor rated specification = 37 kW, 415 Volt, 62 Amps, 1480 rpm, 3 phase, delta connected
>
> The following data was collected during the no load test on the motor.
> Voltage = 415 Volts; Current = 18 Amps ;Frequency = 50 Hz; Stator resistance per phase =0.275 Ohms at 300°C; No load power = 1164 Watts
>
> Calculate the following:
> a) Iron plus friction and windage losses.
> b) Stator resistance at 120°C.
> c) Stator copper loss at full load at operating temperature of 120°C.
> d) Full load slip and rotor input assuming rotor losses are slip times rotor input.
> e) Motor input assuming that stray losses are 0.5% of the motor rated output power.
> f) Motor full load efficiency and full load power factor. (06)

**Topic:** Induction motor losses, temperature-corrected stator resistance, slip, input power, efficiency and power factor.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**
Note: the printed "300°C" is read as the cold-measurement reference 30 °C (standard form of this problem); taken literally, resistance would fall toward 120 °C, contradicting "operating temperature."

- a) Delta-connected: no-load stator copper loss = 3 × (I_line/√3)² × R = I_L² × R/phase = 18² × 0.275 = 89.1 W. Iron + friction + windage = no-load power − stator copper loss = 1164 − 89.1 = 1074.9 W.
- b) R₁₂₀ = R₃₀ × (235 + 120)/(235 + 30) = 0.275 × 355/265 = 0.3684 Ω per phase.
- c) Full-load phase current = 62/√3 = 35.8 A; stator copper loss = 3 × 35.8² × 0.3684 = 62² × 0.3684 = 1416.2 W.
- d) Synchronous speed = (120 × 50)/4 = 1500 rpm (4-pole, 50 Hz). Slip s = (1500 − 1480)/1500 = 0.0133 = 1.33 %. Rotor losses = s × rotor input and shaft output = (1 − s) × rotor input, so rotor input = 37/(1 − 0.0133) = 37.5 kW (rotor loss ≈ 0.5 kW).
- e) Stray losses = 0.5 % × 37 kW = 0.185 kW. Motor input = rotor input + stator copper loss + iron/friction/windage + stray = 37.5 + 1.416 + 1.075 + 0.185 = 40.18 kW.
- f) Efficiency = 37/40.18 = 0.921 = 92.1 %. Power factor = input/(√3 × V × I) = 40176/(1.732 × 415 × 62) = 40176/44,566 = 0.90.

**Final answer:** a) ≈ 1074.9 W (≈ 1.075 kW); b) ≈ 0.3684 Ω/phase; c) ≈ 1416 W; d) slip = 1.33 %, rotor input = 37.5 kW; e) ≈ 40.18 kW; f) efficiency ≈ 92.1 %, power factor ≈ 0.90.

**Verification:** a) 89.1 + 1074.9 = 1164 W = measured no-load power ✓; b) 0.275 × (355/265) recomputes to 0.3684 Ω ✓; c) 3 × (35.8)² × 0.3684 = 62² × 0.3684 = 1416 W by both routes ✓; d) 37.5 × 0.0133 = 0.5 kW loss and 37.5 − 0.5 = 37 kW output ✓; f) √3 × 415 × 62 × 0.90 = 40.1 kW ≈ computed input 40.18 kW (< 0.2 % apart), consistent with rated 62 A ✓.

## Question 6

### 6A — Integrated Energy Policy and Energy Security of India (04)

**Source wording:**
> 6A.   Explain the need for Integrated Energy Policy. Also explain, how Integrated Energy Policy aims to
>       strengthen the Energy Security of India.                                                                     (04)

**Topic:** National energy scenario – Integrated Energy Policy (IEP); energy security of India.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

*Need for IEP:*
- Energy administration was fragmented across separate ministries (Power, Coal, Petroleum & Natural Gas, New & Renewable Energy, Atomic Energy), producing sector-specific policies, inconsistent pricing and sub-optimal allocation of resources.
- Rapid economic growth drives sharply rising energy demand while domestic oil/gas (and progressively coal) resources are inadequate, causing heavy and growing import dependence.
- A unified long-term framework (planning horizon up to 2031–32) was required to cover *all* carriers — coal, oil, gas, hydro, nuclear, renewables, electricity — together with energy access, equity, affordability and environmental sustainability.
- It introduces a level playing field among energy options/technologies, market-oriented pricing to signal scarcity and curb wasteful consumption, and a coherent demand-side conservation agenda (via the Energy Conservation Act, 2001).

*How IEP aims to strengthen Energy Security:*
- Demand management: energy efficiency and demand-side management treated as the least-cost "supply".
- Diversification of the fuel basket and of import sources/geographies; augmentation of domestic supply through exploration & production and coal-sector reforms.
- Expansion of domestic, non-imported options — renewables, hydro, nuclear — to reduce import dependence; capability for fuel-switching between energy forms.
- Strategic crude-oil reserves, international energy diplomacy and acquisition of equity oil/gas assets abroad.

**Final answer:** IEP is needed to replace fragmented ministry-wise policies with one integrated long-term framework meeting demand sustainably; it strengthens energy security chiefly through efficiency-led demand moderation, diversified and domesticated supply, strategic reserves and overseas equity assets.

**Verification:** Answer addresses both halves of the question (need; security measures); all listed measures map to the published themes of the Planning Commission's Integrated Energy Policy (2006); weightage consistent with (04).

### 6B — Textile plant MTOE computation and DC status under EC Act, 2001 (03)

**Source wording:**
> 6B.   In a textile plant, the average monthly energy consumption is 700000 kWhr of purchased electricity
>       from grid, 40 kL of furnace oil (specific gravity – 0.92) for thermic fluid heater, 60 tonnes of coal for
>       steam boiler & 10 kL of HSD (specific gravity – 0.885) for material handling equipment.
>       Given data: 1 kWhr = 860 kCal; GCV of coal – 3450 kCal/kg; GCV of furnace oil – 10500 kCal/kg)
>            a) Calculate the energy consumption in terms of MTOE for the plant & calculate the
>               percentage share of energy sources used based on consumption in MTOE basis.
>            b) Comment whether this textile plant qualifies as a notified DC under EC Act, 2001. [Cut off-
>               3000 MTOE)                                                                                          (03)

**Topic:** Energy units and conversions (MTOE); designated-consumer threshold under the Energy Conservation Act, 2001.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Convention adopted (standard definition, since not supplied in the paper): 1 kg of oil equivalent = 10,000 kCal ⇒ 1 TOE = 10⁷ kCal ⇒ 1 MTOE = 10¹³ kCal.

*a) Monthly energy and MTOE shares:*

| Source | Quantity → mass | Heat value | Monthly energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchased electricity | 700,000 kWhr | 860 kCal/kWhr | 700,000 × 860 = 6.02 × 10⁸ kCal |
| Furnace oil | 40 kL × 0.92 kg/L = 36,800 kg | 10,500 kCal/kg | 36,800 × 10,500 = 3.864 × 10⁸ kCal |
| Coal | 60 tonnes = 60,000 kg | 3,450 kCal/kg | 60,000 × 3,450 = 2.07 × 10⁸ kCal |
| HSD | 10 kL × 0.885 kg/L = 8,850 kg | GCV not supplied in the paper | not computable from given data |

Sub-total (sources with given heat values) = 6.02 + 3.864 + 2.07 = **1.1954 × 10⁹ kCal/month**.

| Source | MTOE/month (kCal ÷ 10¹³) | Percentage share |
|---|---|---|
| Electricity | 6.02 × 10⁻⁵ | 6.02/11.954 = 50.36 % |
| Furnace oil | 3.864 × 10⁻⁵ | 32.32 % |
| Coal | 2.07 × 10⁻⁵ | 17.32 % |
| **Total** | **1.1954 × 10⁻⁴ MTOE/month** | **100.00 %** |

Annualised: 1.1954 × 10⁻⁴ × 12 = **1.43448 × 10⁻³ MTOE/year** (= 1,434.48 TOE/year).

*b) DC qualification:* The printed cutoff "[Cut off- 3000 MTOE)" corresponds to the EC Act, 2001 designated-consumer norm, conventionally expressed as 3000 TOE of annual energy consumption. Under either reading the result is identical: annual consumption 1.43448 × 10⁻³ MTOE (≈ 1,434 TOE) is far below 3000. The omitted HSD term cannot change this: to lift the annual total to 3000 TOE, HSD's 106,200 kg/year would need a GCV of ≈ 1.566 × 10¹⁰ ÷ 1.062 × 10⁵ ≈ 147,000 kCal/kg, which exceeds any liquid fuel by an order of magnitude.

**Final answer:** a) Plant consumes ≈ 1.1954 × 10⁻⁴ MTOE per month (1.43448 × 10⁻³ MTOE/year, excluding HSD whose GCV is not supplied); shares — electricity 50.36 %, furnace oil 32.32 %, coal 17.32 %. b) The plant does **not** qualify as a notified DC under EC Act, 2001, being well below the 3000 cutoff on an annualised basis.

**Verification:** Masses cross-check via density: 40 kL × 0.92 t/kL = 36.8 t and 10 kL × 0.885 t/kL = 8.85 t ✓; shares sum to 100.00 % ✓; reverse check 1.43448 × 10⁻³ MTOE × 10¹³ kCal/MTOE ÷ 12 = 1.1954 × 10⁹ kCal/month, matching the direct sum ✓.
