## Question 1

### 1A

**Source wording:** Explain the three classification of energy, with examples. (02 marks)

**Topic:** Classification of energy for energy-management purposes.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Energy is classified under three schemes:

1. **Primary and Secondary energy** — Primary energy is available in nature in raw form and undergoes no conversion before use: coal, crude oil, natural gas, uranium, sunlight, wind, biomass, hydro. Secondary energy is produced by converting primary energy into a more convenient form: electricity, steam, coke, petrol, diesel, LPG.
2. **Commercial and Non-commercial energy** — Commercial energy is bought and sold in the market at a known price: electricity, coal, lignite, furnace oil, diesel, natural gas. Non-commercial energy is traditionally gathered free by users and does not pass through the market: firewood, cattle dung, agro-waste such as bagasse and rice husk.
3. **Renewable and Non-renewable energy** — Renewable energy is continuously replenished in nature and is virtually inexhaustible: solar, wind, hydro, biomass, geothermal, tidal. Non-renewable energy exists in finite deposits and depletes with use: coal, crude oil, natural gas, uranium.

**Final answer:** The three classifications are Primary–Secondary, Commercial–Non-commercial, and Renewable–Non-renewable energy, with the examples listed above for each category.

**Verification:** Exactly three classification schemes are presented as the question demands; each gives contrasting categories with examples, and membership is mutually consistent (e.g., coal is primary, commercial and non-renewable; electricity is secondary and commercial).

### 1C

**Source wording:** Explain the methodology followed for carrying out assessment of lighting systems employed in Industries. Also, explain any two ECON measures for Lighting Systems. (04 marks)

**Topic:** Assessment methodology and energy-conservation opportunities (ECONs) for industrial lighting.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

*Assessment methodology:*

1. Prepare a complete inventory area-wise: lamp type and rating, number of fittings, ballast/choke type, luminaire condition, mounting height.
2. Measure actual illuminance (lux) at the work plane with a calibrated lux meter at representative points and compare with the value recommended for the task (e.g., IS 3646).
3. Record daily operating hours and the switching/control arrangement to establish usage patterns.
4. Compute connected load = Σ(lamp watts + ballast loss watts × quantity)/1000 kW, and energy consumption = kW × operating hours.
5. Derive performance indices: installed load efficacy and lighting power density (W/m²), lamp-circuit efficacy (lm/W), and percentage of over-lighting relative to the recommended level.
6. Check electrical parameters: supply voltage, choke/ballast power factor and losses.
7. Identify wastage sources: over-illumination, dirt depreciation of lamps/luminaires, inefficient lamp types, absence of controls or daylight utilisation.
8. Quantify savings for feasible improvements and rank them by payback period.

*Two ECON measures:*

- **ECON 1 – Replacement by high-efficacy lamps/ballasts:** substitute incandescent lamps (roughly 10–18 lm/W) with CFL/LED equivalents, and conventional T12 tube lights with copper-choke ballasts by T5/LED tubes with electronic ballasts. Saving arises because the same lumens are delivered at much lower watts and lower ballast loss, with the added benefit of reduced heat load on ventilation/air-conditioning.
- **ECON 2 – Controls and daylight utilisation:** fit occupancy sensors in intermittently used areas (stores, corridors, washrooms), timers/photocells on outdoor and security lighting, and exploit daylight (roof sheets, windows) to switch off artificial circuits when daylight suffices; combine with periodic cleaning of lamps and luminaires to retain designed light output. Saving arises from reduced burning hours at unchanged visual-task performance.

**Final answer:** Assessment follows inventory → lux measurement against recommended standards → load/operating-hour computation → efficacy and W/m² indices → identification and ranking of improvements; the two ECONs explained are lamp/ballast replacement by efficient types, and sensor/timer/daylight-based control with good maintenance.

**Verification:** The methodology covers both measurement and computational steps expected of an industrial lighting audit; exactly two ECON measures are explained, satisfying "any two"; each ECON states its saving mechanism, which can be cross-checked against standard energy-audit literature.

### 2B

**Source wording**
> 2B. With respect to the Electricity Act, 2003, explain the reforms brought in generation,
> transmission and distribution sector. 03

**Topic**: Electricity Act 2003 — sector-wise power-sector reforms.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**
- Generation (de-licensed): Statutory clearance/licence abolished for thermal, gas, etc.; any person may generate without a licence (hydro/nuclear retain statutory concurrence requirements). Captive generation is freely permitted without licence.
- Transmission (open access and unbundling): Open access in inter-state/intra-state transmission mandated; SEBs unbundled so transmission is run by a government/utility company separate from generation and distribution; independent State/Regional/National Load Despatch Centres created for scheduling and dispatch; trading requires a licence; National Electricity Policy and National Electricity Plan provided for.
- Distribution (competition and regulation): Distribution through licensed distributors, with provision for more than one distributor in the same area; phased open access for consumers beyond a notified threshold; compulsory metering of all supplies; tariff determination transferred to CERC/SERCs with progressive reduction of cross-subsidies; Standards of Performance with compensation/penalties for default.

**Final answer**: Generation de-licensed with free captive generation; transmission unbundled with open access and independent load despatch centres; distribution regulated with open access, mandatory metering, SERC-set tariffs and reduced cross-subsidy. (Weighting: 03 marks.)

**Verification**: Points map one-to-one onto the Act's principal reform themes (Sections on generation without licence, transmission open access/LDCs, distribution licensing, metering, tariff regulation) and match standard Energy Management textbook summaries of the Act's impact on the three sectors.

### 3A. Boiler steam pipeline insulation project (06 Marks)

**Source wording**

> A plant which runs for 8000 hours a year has a boiler with an efficiency of 80%. The boiler produces steam at 15 kg/cm2. Steam must travel via pipeline to the plant, located at some distance. The diameter of steam pipeline is 100mm and the total length of pipe from the boiler to the plant is 300m. The surface temperature of the non-insulated part of pipeline, which is 150m in length, is 195 °C. The energy manager proposes a project to properly insulate the non-insulated length of the pipeline with 70mm of glass wool with aluminum cladding, so as to reduce the surface temperature to 70 ° C. Find out the fuel and costs savings and the payback period for the project if the proposal were to be accepted. Assume fuel cost is Rs 15/kg with GCV= 14250 kCal/kg, ambient temperature of 25 °C and insulation material is available at Rs 4000 per sq. meter.

**Topic:** Thermal energy auditing — heat loss from uninsulated surfaces, fuel savings and payback period for insulation investment.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

- Exposed surface area: A = π × D × L = π × 0.100 m × 150 m = 47.12 m²
- Assumed surface heat-loss rates (standard insulation tables, still air at 25 °C): bare pipe at 195 °C (ΔT = 170 °C) ≈ 3,200 kCal/hr·m²; 70 mm glass wool with aluminium cladding at 70 °C (ΔT = 45 °C) ≈ 290 kCal/hr·m²
- Heat loss before = 3,200 × 47.12 = 1,50,784 kCal/hr
- Heat loss after = 290 × 47.12 = 13,665 kCal/hr
- Heat saved = 1,50,784 − 13,665 = 1,37,119 kCal/hr
- Useful heat per kg fuel = 0.80 × 14,250 = 11,400 kCal/kg → fuel saving = 1,37,119 ÷ 11,400 = 12.03 kg/hr
- Annual fuel saving = 12.03 × 8,000 = 96,240 kg/year
- Annual cost saving = 96,240 × Rs 15 = Rs 14,43,600/year
- Insulation cost = 47.12 × Rs 4,000 = Rs 1,88,480
- Payback = 1,88,480 ÷ 14,43,600 = 0.131 year ≈ 1.6 months

**Final answer:** Fuel saving ≈ 96,240 kg/year (cost saving ≈ Rs 14.44 lakh/year); payback period ≈ 0.13 year (~1.6 months). *(Values depend on the assumed heat-loss rates; the exam expects stated assumptions.)*

**Verification:** Cross-check: monthly saving = 14,43,600 ÷ 12 = Rs 1,20,300; 1,88,480 ÷ 1,20,300 = 1.57 months ✓. Unit chain kCal/hr → ÷ (efficiency × GCV) → kg/hr → × hours → × Rs/kg is dimensionally consistent ✓.

### 3B. Maximum demand and power factor improvement (02 Marks)

**Source wording**

> During May-2015, a plant has recorded a maximum demand of 750 kVA and an average power factor of 0.66 lag is observed. The minimum average power factor to be maintained is 0.95 lag as per the independent utility supplier & every dip of 0.05 from minimum average power factor attracts a penalty of Rs 8,000/- in each month’s electricity bill.
> a) Calculate the improvement in power factor for June 2015 by installing 150 kVAR capacitors.
> b) Calculate penalty to be paid, if any, for the month of June 2015.

**Topic:** Electrical energy auditing — power factor correction using shunt capacitors and PF penalty computation.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

a) Load kW = 750 × 0.66 = 495 kW; cos φ₁ = 0.66 → φ₁ = 48.71°, tan φ₁ = 1.139 → initial reactive load = 495 × 1.139 = 563.9 kVAr. After adding 150 kVAr: net kVAr = 563.9 − 150 = 413.9 kVAr. tan φ₂ = 413.9/495 = 0.836 → φ₂ = 39.90° → PF₂ = cos 39.90° ≈ 0.767 lag. Improvement = 0.767 − 0.66 = 0.107 lag.

b) New PF 0.767 < 0.95, so penalty applies. Dip = 0.95 − 0.767 = 0.183. On the usual slab basis (PF band 0.95–0.90, 0.90–0.85, 0.85–0.80, 0.80–0.75): 0.767 lies in the 4th slab → penalty = 4 × Rs 8,000 = Rs 32,000. (If only complete dips of 0.05 are charged: ⌊0.183/0.05⌋ = 3 → Rs 24,000.)

**Final answer:** a) Improved power factor ≈ 0.767 lag (improvement of ≈ 0.107 over 0.66). b) Penalty payable ≈ Rs 32,000 for June 2015 (slab-wise basis; Rs 24,000 if only completed full dips are counted).

**Verification:** kVA₂ = √(495² + 413.9²) = √(2,45,025 + 1,71,313) = √4,16,338 = 645.2 kVA; PF = 495/645.2 = 0.767 ✓. Slab count: four 0.05 bands cover 0.95 → 0.75, which brackets 0.767 ✓.

### 4A — Three-phase induction motor: loss segregation and full-load performance

**Source wording (verbatim):**

> 4A.   A 34 kW/45 HP, 415 Volt Delta connected 3 Phase IM has a full load current of 57 A
>       at 1475 RPM. The No Load data is, Applied Voltage = 415 V; No load current = 16.1
>       A, Frequency = 50 HZ; Stator phase resistance at 30 °C = 0.264 Ohms & No Load
>       power = 1063.74W. Calculate
>          a) Core + FW losses
>          b) Stator copper losses if the operating temperature is 120 °C
>          c) Motor input (assuming IEC standards)
>                                                                                                   05
>          d) Motor efficiency at full load & full load power factor.

#### 4A (a) Core + FW losses

- **Topic:** Separation of fixed losses (core + friction & windage) from the no-load test of a delta-connected squirrel-cage induction motor.
- **Worked solution - not an official answer:**
  At no load the input covers stator copper loss plus core + FW losses (rotor copper loss negligible at near-zero slip).

  - No-load phase current (Delta): I₀/ph = 16.1 / √3 = 9.296 A
  - Stator copper loss at no load (using 30 °C resistance): P_Cu0 = 3 × 9.296² × 0.264 = 3 × 86.408 × 0.264 = 68.44 W
  - Core + FW losses = No-load power − stator copper loss = 1063.74 − 68.44 = **995.30 W**
- **Final answer:** Core + FW losses ≈ **995.3 W (≈ 0.995 kW)**.
- **Verification:** 68.44 W + 995.30 W = 1063.74 W, exactly matching the measured no-load input; the split is physically sensible because at no load the copper loss component is small compared with rotational/core losses.

#### 4A (b) Stator copper losses at 120 °C

- **Topic:** Temperature correction of winding resistance (copper) and full-load stator copper loss.
- **Worked solution - not an official answer:**
  Copper resistance correction: R₂ = R₁ × (235 + t₂)/(235 + t₁)

  - R₁₂₀ = 0.264 × (235 + 120)/(235 + 30) = 0.264 × 355/265 = 0.35366 Ω per phase
  - Full-load phase current (Delta): I_fl/ph = 57 / √3 = 32.909 A
  - Stator copper loss at 120 °C: P_Cu = 3 × 32.909² × 0.35366 = 3 × 1083.0 × 0.35366 = **1149.04 W**
- **Final answer:** Stator copper loss at full load, 120 °C ≈ **1149 W (≈ 1.149 kW)**.
- **Verification:** Cross-check: full-load copper loss at 30 °C = 3 × 1083.0 × 0.264 = 857.74 W; scaling by 355/265 = 1.3396 gives 857.74 × 1.3396 = 1149.1 W — identical result.

#### 4A (c) Motor input (IEC basis)

- **Topic:** Reconstruction of total losses (stator Cu, rotor Cu, core + FW, stray load) to obtain full-load input power.
- **Worked solution - not an official answer:**
  Assumptions (stated, since the source gives no extra data): 4-pole machine, Nₛ = 1500 RPM at 50 Hz; stray load loss taken as the conventional 0.5 % of rated output per IEC loss-segregation practice; core + FW and stator Cu losses from parts (a) and (b).

  - Slip: s = (1500 − 1475)/1500 = 0.01667
  - Rotor copper loss = [s/(1 − s)] × P_out = (0.01667/0.98333) × 34,000 = 576.27 W
  - Stray load loss = 0.005 × 34,000 = 170.00 W
  - Total losses = 995.30 (core + FW) + 1149.04 (stator Cu) + 576.27 (rotor Cu) + 170.00 (stray) = 2890.61 W
  - Motor input = 34,000 + 2890.61 = **36,890.61 W ≈ 36.89 kW**
- **Final answer:** Full-load motor input ≈ **36.89 kW** (total losses ≈ 2.89 kW).
- **Verification:** Component sum re-checked: 995.30 + 1149.04 + 576.27 + 170.00 = 2890.61 W; total losses are ~8.5 % of rated output, a typical magnitude for a motor of this size, confirming internal consistency.

#### 4A (d) Full-load efficiency and full-load power factor

- **Topic:** Full-load efficiency and input power factor from computed input power.
- **Worked solution - not an official answer:**

  - Efficiency: η = P_out/P_in = 34,000/36,890.61 = 0.9216 → **92.16 %**
  - Full-load apparent power: S = √3 × V × I = √3 × 415 × 57 = 40,971.7 VA
  - Power factor: cos φ = P_in/S = 36,890.61/40,971.7 = 0.9004 → **0.90 lagging**
- **Final answer:** Full-load efficiency ≈ **92.16 %**; full-load power factor ≈ **0.90 lagging**.
- **Verification:** Cross-check: η × cos φ = 0.9216 × 0.9004 = 0.8298, and directly P_out/S = 34,000/40,971.7 = 0.8298 — the two routes agree exactly.

### 4B — Energy Efficient Motors (short technical note)

**Source wording (verbatim):**

> 4B.   Write a short technical note on Energy Efficient Motors.                                     02

- **Topic:** Design features, performance characteristics, economics and application of energy-efficient (EE) motors.
- **Worked solution - not an official answer:**

  An Energy Efficient Motor is designed to deliver the same shaft output as a standard motor with 2–8 % lower electrical losses, i.e., higher efficiency over the whole load range. Loss-reduction measures include:

  - **Reduced stator & rotor copper losses:** larger conductor cross-sections, more copper in slots, improved slot fill and rotor bar/slot geometry.
  - **Reduced iron (core) losses:** low-loss (high-grade silicon) electrical steel, thinner laminations, better lamination insulation, and lower operating flux densities.
  - **Reduced friction & windage losses:** optimised fan and airflow design; better bearings/seals.
  - **Thermal margins:** Class F insulation operated at Class B temperature rise, giving longer insulation life and tolerance to voltage dips/unbalance and higher ambient temperatures.

  Benefits: cooler operation and longer life, stable high efficiency down to partial loads (where many motors actually run), better tolerance to supply variations, and often improved power factor; efficiency classes are standardised (e.g., IEC 60034-30-1: IE1–IE4; in India, BEE star labelling). Limitations: purchase price is typically 20–30 % higher, and EE motors tend to run at slightly higher speeds, which matters for centrifugal loads. Selection is justified by life-cycle costing: high running hours (e.g., ≥4000 h/yr) and moderate-to-high load factors give short paybacks, whereas lightly used or always-throttled applications may not justify replacement until failure.
- **Final answer:** An energy-efficient motor cuts all major loss components (copper, iron, friction/windage) through superior materials and design, runs cooler and lasts longer, costs 20–30 % more upfront, and is economically attractive mainly for continuously running, high-load-factor drives selected on life-cycle cost.
- **Verification:** Qualitative check: every loss term identified in 4A (a)–(c) — stator Cu, rotor Cu, core, FW, stray — is addressed by a corresponding design measure listed above, so the technical note is consistent with the loss model used in the numerical part.

### 5A

**Source wording:** A community has 500 people. The source of water to the community are borewells and supply of water from the borewell is through hand pump. The depth of the borewell is 20m. Six hand pumps are used to meet the water requirement. Using the following details, compute the cost of water per liter if the project life cycle is 20 years.

- Per capita water consumption – 40 lit/day
- Cost of each hand pump – INR 5000/-
- Cost of digging a borewell – INR 250 per meter
- Life span of a hand pump – 10 years
- Annual Maintenance Cost – INR 1250/- per hand pump
- Rate of interest – 10%
- Inflation rate – 7%

(Marks: 04)

**Topic:** Life-cycle costing (LCC) of water supply — present worth, escalation, capital recovery factor.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Assumptions: one borewell serves all six hand pumps; hand pumps are replaced once (at year 10) since project life is 20 years vs 10-year pump life; recurring/replacement costs escalate at inflation 7%; money is discounted at interest rate 10%.

1. Water demand: 500 × 40 = 20,000 lit/day → 20,000 × 365 = 73,00,000 lit/year → × 20 years = 14,60,00,000 lit.
2. Capital cost (year 0): borewell = 250 × 20 = INR 5,000; hand pumps = 6 × 5,000 = INR 30,000; total = **INR 35,000**.
3. Pump replacement at year 10 (inflated): 30,000 × (1.07)^10 = 30,000 × 1.96715 = INR 59,014.5; present worth = 59,014.5 / (1.10)^10 = 59,014.5 / 2.59374 = **INR 22,752.7**.
4. Maintenance: 6 × 1,250 = INR 7,500 in year 1, growing 7%/yr for 20 yrs; PW = 7,500 × [1 − (1.07/1.10)^20] / (0.10 − 0.07) = 7,500 × (1 − 0.57523)/0.03 = 7,500 × 14.159 = **INR 106,192.5**.
5. Total present worth = 35,000 + 22,752.7 + 106,192.5 = **INR 163,945**.
6. Levelized annual cost via CRF(10%, 20 yr) = 0.10×(1.10)^20 / [(1.10)^20 − 1] = 0.11746: EUAC = 163,945 × 0.11746 ≈ INR 19,257/year.
7. Cost of water per liter = 19,257 / 73,00,000 ≈ **INR 0.00264 per liter** (≈ 26 paise per 100 liters).

**Final answer:** Cost of water ≈ INR 0.0026 per liter (life-cycle levelized basis, assumptions stated above). *(Worked solution - not an official answer)*

**Verification:** EUAC by components: 35,000×0.11746 = 4,111; 22,752.7×0.11746 = 2,673; 106,192.5×0.11746 = 12,473; sum = 19,257/yr ✓. ### 5B

**Source wording:** A food containing 80% water is to be dried at 100 °C, down to moisture content of 10%. If the initial temperature of food is 16 °C, calculate the quantity of heat energy required per unit weight of original material, for drying under atmospheric pressure. The specific heat capacity of the food is 3.8 kJ/kg °C. Also find the energy required per unit of water removed. (Marks: 04)

**Topic:** Drying/dehydration heat balance — sensible heating plus evaporation latent heat.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Basis: 1 kg of original material (wet basis interpretation of final 10% moisture).

1. Initial: water = 0.80 kg, dry solids = 0.20 kg. Final mass (solids = 90%): 0.20/0.9 = 0.2222 kg; final water = 0.0222 kg.
2. Water evaporated = 0.80 − 0.0222 = **0.7778 kg**.
3. Sensible heat, 16 °C → 100 °C: Q₁ = 1 × 3.8 × (100 − 16) = **319.2 kJ**.
4. Latent heat at atmospheric pressure (h_fg at 100 °C ≈ 2257 kJ/kg): Q₂ = 0.7778 × 2257 = **1755.4 kJ**.
5. Total heat = 319.2 + 1755.4 = **2074.6 kJ per kg of original material**.
6. Energy per unit water removed = 2074.6 / 0.7778 = **2667.3 kJ per kg water removed**.

**Final answer:** Heat required ≈ 2074.6 kJ per kg of original material; energy per unit water removed ≈ 2667.3 kJ/kg of water. *(Worked solution - not an official answer)*

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

## Question 6

**Q6A (03)**

- **Source wording:**
  > 6A. Explain the Kaizen management principle. How is it implemented? What are the benefits of Kaizen management principle? 03

- **Topic:** Kaizen as a continuous-improvement tool in energy management.
- **Worked solution - not an official answer:**
  *Principle:* "Kaizen" (Japanese: *kai* = change, *zen* = for the better) means continuous, gradual, never-ending improvement involving every employee, from top management to shop floor. It is process-oriented, favours many small low-cost/no-cost improvements over large investments, and aims at eliminating *muda* (waste), making it directly applicable to energy efficiency.
  *Implementation:* (i) top-management commitment and policy deployment with measurable targets; (ii) total employee participation via suggestion schemes, quality circles and small-group activities; (iii) 5S housekeeping (Seiri-sort, Seiton-set in order, Seiso-shine, Seiketsu-standardise, Shitsuke-sustain); (iv) continuous repetition of the Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) cycle; (v) training/awareness, standardised procedures, visual display of measured results and recognition/rewards for contributors.
  *Benefits:* steady reduction of waste, energy and operating costs; improved quality, productivity, safety and housekeeping; early detection of abnormal losses; higher employee morale, teamwork and skills; builds a lasting improvement culture and competitiveness at minimal capital cost.
- **Final answer:** Kaizen is continuous incremental improvement with total employee involvement, implemented through management commitment, PDCA, 5S, suggestion systems and small-group activities; its benefits are sustained reductions in waste/cost and gains in quality, productivity, safety and morale.
- **Verification:** All three requested elements (principle, implementation, benefits) are covered in order; definitions agree with standard energy-audit references (e.g., BEE Guide Book 1 coverage of Kaizen).
