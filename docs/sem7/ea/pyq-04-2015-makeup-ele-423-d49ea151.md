## Question 1

### 1A. Centrifugal pump at reduced speed

**Source wording:** A centrifugal pump operates at 30 m3/hr and at 1440 RPM. The pump operating efficiency is 65% and the motor efficiency is 89%. The discharge pressure gauge shows a reading of 3.4 kg/cm2. The suction is 3m below the pump centerline. If the speed of the pump is reduced by 25%, estimate the following
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;a) Pump Flow
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;b) Pump Head
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;c) Motor Power
&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;Assume the efficiency of motor & pump remains same at reduced speeds. (05)

**Topic:** Affinity laws for centrifugal pumps; converting pressure gauge readings to head; hydraulic-shaft-motor power chain.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**
Reduced speed N₂ = 1440 × (1 − 0.25) = 1080 RPM, so the speed ratio k = 1080/1440 = 0.75.
Rated head: taking 1 kg/cm2 ≈ 10 m of water column, discharge head = 3.4 × 10 = 34 m; since the suction is 3 m below the pump centerline, the suction lift adds to it, giving H₁ = 34 + 3 = 37 m.
Affinity laws with efficiencies unchanged: flow ∝ speed, head ∝ speed², power ∝ speed³.

- a) Pump Flow: Q₂ = 30 × 0.75 = 22.5 m3/hr
- b) Pump Head: H₂ = 37 × 0.75² = 37 × 0.5625 = 20.8125 ≈ 20.81 m
- c) Motor Power: hydraulic power at rated duty = (Q₁ × H₁ × ρ × g)/(3.6 × 10⁶) = (30 × 37 × 1000 × 9.81)/(3.6 × 10⁶) = 3.025 kW; pump shaft power = 3.025/0.65 = 4.654 kW; motor input power = 4.654/0.89 = 5.229 kW. After the speed cut, P₂ = 5.229 × 0.75³ = 5.229 × 0.421875 ≈ 2.21 kW

**Final answer:** a) 22.5 m3/hr; b) ≈ 20.81 m; c) ≈ 2.21 kW

**Verification:** Direct recomputation at the new duty point: hydraulic power = (22.5 × 20.8125 × 1000 × 9.81)/(3.6 × 10⁶) = 1.276 kW; shaft = 1.276/0.65 = 1.963 kW; motor input = 1.963/0.89 = 2.206 kW ≈ 2.21 kW, matching the speed-cubed scaling result.

### 1B. Energy-efficient motors and intrinsic losses

**Source wording:** Explain how energy efficient motors reduce its intrinsic losses. (03)

**Topic:** Design measures reducing the loss components of energy-efficient induction motors.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**

- Iron (core) losses: premium low-loss electrical steel, thinner well-insulated laminations and lower operating flux density reduce hysteresis and eddy-current losses.
- Stator copper (I²R) losses: greater conductor cross-section (higher slot fill) and shorter end-turns lower winding resistance.
- Rotor losses: larger/optimized rotor bar cross-section and improved bar material reduce rotor I²R losses.
- Stray load losses: refined slot/air-gap geometry and tighter manufacturing tolerances reduce leakage and high-frequency losses.
- Friction and windage: low-loss bearings and aerodynamically efficient cooling fans cut mechanical losses.

Collectively the same shaft output is delivered at lower input power, i.e., higher efficiency than a standard-efficiency motor.

**Final answer:** Energy-efficient motors reduce intrinsic losses through low-loss/thinner core steel (iron losses), more copper and lower-resistance windings (stator and rotor I²R losses), optimized rotor-bar, slot and air-gap design (stray losses), and improved bearings and fan design (friction and windage losses).

**Verification:** Each measure maps onto exactly one component of the standard five-way motor loss split (core, stator copper, rotor copper, stray load, friction/windage), covering all intrinsic losses without overlap or contradiction.

### 1C. Flue gas mass from burning 5 kg coal

**Source wording:** For complete combustion of 1 kg of a typical coal 12 kg of air is required. Calorific value of coal is 4200 kCal/kg with ash content of 22%. What is the quantity (in kg) flue gas generated by burning 5 kg coal? (02)

**Topic:** Combustion stoichiometry and overall mass balance.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**
Air needed for 5 kg coal = 5 × 12 = 60 kg, so total mass entering = 5 + 60 = 65 kg.
Ash remains as solid refuse and does not leave with the flue gas: ash = 0.22 × 5 = 1.1 kg.
Mass conservation gives flue gas = total input − ash = 65 − 1.1 = 63.9 kg.
The calorific value (4200 kCal/kg) is thermal data and is not required for the mass balance.

**Final answer:** 63.9 kg of flue gas

**Verification:** Product-side closure: 63.9 kg flue gas + 1.1 kg ash = 65 kg, equal to 5 kg coal + 60 kg air supplied.

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |

**(05)**

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

- S_PE = 2395680 − (5000 × 4284)/9 = 15680
- S_PP = 2820800 − 5000²/9 = 43022.22
- S_EE = 2080976 − 4284²/9 = 41792

Slope b = S_PE/S_PP = 15680/43022.22 = **0.3645 TOE/Ton**
Intercept a = Ē − b·P̄ = 476 − (0.3645 × 555.56) = **273.52 TOE/Month**

Best-fit line: **E = 273.52 + 0.3645·P**

Pearson coefficient: r = S_PE/√(S_PP · S_EE) = 15680/√(43022.22 × 41792) = 15680/42402.6 = **0.370** (r² ≈ 0.137 — weak positive linear association).

**Topic:** Workplace-organisation / good-housekeeping tool used in energy management systems.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**

5S is a Japanese systematic workplace-organisation and good-housekeeping methodology in which every item, area and practice is sorted, arranged, cleaned, standardised and sustained, making waste and abnormalities (including energy wastes like leaks and dirty heat-transfer surfaces) visible so they can be eliminated. ### 3B. Laws of Conservation and Skim-Milk Composition (04)

**Source wording:** "What are the laws of conservation? Skim milk is prepared by the removal of some of the fat from whole milk. This skim milk is found to contain 90.5% water, 3.5% protein, 5.1% carbohydrate, 0.1% fat and 0.8% ash. If the original milk contained 4.5% fat, calculate its composition assuming that only fat was removed to make the skim milk and that there are no losses in the process. (04)"

**Topic:** Conservation laws applied to a steady-state material (mass) balance with no generation, consumption or losses.

**Worked solution - not an official answer:**

Laws of conservation state that neither matter nor energy can be created or destroyed, only transformed: (i) law of conservation of mass — for a process without accumulation, total mass in = total mass out, and each non-reacting constituent is conserved individually; (ii) law of conservation of energy — total energy of an isolated system is constant (basis of the first law of thermodynamics and the energy-audit equation).

Basis: 100 kg of original (whole) milk.
- Non-fat constituents in it = 100 − 4.5 = 95.5 kg; since only fat is removed and there are no losses, all 95.5 kg passes into the skim milk.
- These constituents form 100 − 0.1 = 99.9% of the skim-milk mass.
- Skim milk produced: S = 95.5 / 0.999 = 95.596 kg.
- Fat removed: F = 100 − 95.596 = 4.404 kg.

Each constituent retains its skim-milk percentage applied to S, giving its mass in the original 100 kg of whole milk:

| Constituent | In skim milk (%) | Mass from 95.596 kg skim (kg) | Original whole milk (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 90.5 | 0.905 × 95.596 = 86.514 | 86.51 |
| Protein | 3.5 | 0.035 × 95.596 = 3.346 | 3.35 |
| Carbohydrate | 5.1 | 0.051 × 95.596 = 4.875 | 4.88 |
| Fat | 0.1 | 0.001 × 95.596 = 0.096 (plus 4.404 removed) | 4.50 (given) |
| Ash | 0.8 | 0.008 × 95.596 = 0.765 | 0.76 |
| Total | 100.0 | 100.000 | 100.00 |

**Final answer:** Original milk composition (by weight): water 86.51%, protein 3.35%, carbohydrate 4.88%, fat 4.50%, ash 0.76% — equivalently, per 100 kg of whole milk, 95.60 kg of skim milk (0.1% fat) plus 4.40 kg of extracted fat are obtained.

**Verification:** Constituent sum: 86.51 + 3.35 + 4.88 + 4.50 + 0.76 = 100.00%. Overall mass balance closes: 95.596 + 4.404 = 100 kg. Fat balance closes: 0.096 + 4.404 = 4.50 kg. All balances are consistent under the stated assumptions (only fat removed, no losses), confirming the calculation.

### Q4A — Electricity Act, 2003 Reforms (Generation, Transmission & Distribution), Tariff Principles & Role of CEA

**Source wording**

> Explain reforms brought by the Electricity Act, 2003 in the areas of
> Generation, Transmission & Distribution.
> Reforms in Tariff principles & the Role of CEA                                             (05)

**Topic**: Indian power sector reform legislation — Electricity Act 2003: delicensing, open access, unbundling, tariff principles under Sections 61–64, and functions of the Central Electricity Authority (CEA).

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

*Reforms in Generation*
- Generation has been de-licensed; no licence is required to set up a generating station (Section 7), except hydro projects which need concurrence of the Central Electricity Authority regarding scheme features.
- Captive generation is freely permitted without any licence or clearance requirement.
- Competition introduced among generating companies; they may sell to licensees and consumers subject to availability of open access.

*Reforms in Transmission*
- Unbundling of State Electricity Boards: separate entities for generation, transmission and distribution.
- Open access to transmission networks mandated so that generators, traders and bulk consumers can use the grid on payment of charges.
- Creation of Central Transmission Utility (CTU) and State Transmission Utilities (STU) as non-profit service providers; independent Load Dispatch Centres for grid operation.

*Reforms in Distribution*
- Distribution licensed separately; multiple licences allowed in the same area (with conditions).
- Progressive introduction of retail open access for consumers above specified loads.
- Mandatory metering of all electricity supplied; obligation to supply; standards of performance and compensation for failure.
- Rural electrification encouraged through village electrification and franchise schemes.

*Reforms in Tariff principles*
- Tariffs determined by Regulatory Commissions following statutory principles (Sections 61–64): encourage efficiency, economical use of resources, good performance, optimum investments, and reward for efficiency improvement.
- Cross-subsidies to be progressively reduced so tariffs progressively reflect cost of supply; surcharge limits defined where consumers move to open access.
- National Electricity Policy and Tariff Policy to guide Commissions; no retrospective tariff fixation; multi-year tariff frameworks encouraged.

*Role of CEA*
- Principal technical adviser to the Central Government.
- Prepares the National Electricity Plan covering short-term and perspective planning.
- Concurrence/approval of hydroelectric scheme designs; framing safety and grid standards; specification of meters and technical norms.
- Assists Regulatory Commissions/Appellate Tribunal on technical matters; collects and publishes power-sector statistics.

**Final answer**: The Electricity Act, 2003 de-licensed generation (except CEA-concurred hydro) and freed captive generation, unbundled SEBs with open access and CTU/STU in transmission, brought licensed distribution with mandatory metering, performance standards and phased retail open access, replaced ad hoc pricing with regulator-set, cost-reflective tariffs with declining cross-subsidy under Ss. 61–64 guided by national policies, and made the CEA the Government's technical adviser preparing the National Electricity Plan and approving hydro schemes.

**Verification**: All five asked areas are covered in order — Generation (delicensing/captive), Transmission (open access/unbundling/CTU-STU), Distribution (licensing/metering/open access), Tariff principles (Sections 61–64, cross-subsidy reduction), Role of CEA (adviser/National Electricity Plan/hydro concurrence). Content matches standard provisions of the Electricity Act, 2003; nothing invented beyond the statute's known provisions; mark weight (05) consistent with five-part coverage.

### Q4B — Force Field Analysis in Energy Policy & Planning; SMART Goal with Example

**Source wording**

> Explain how Force Field Analysis helps in energy policy & planning. What is a SMART
> goal? Give an example and perform a Force Field Analysis for the goal.                              (05)

**Topic**: Energy management tools — Lewin's Force Field Analysis (driving vs restraining forces) applied to energy policy/planning; SMART goal formulation with a worked analysis.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

*How Force Field Analysis (FFA) helps in energy policy & planning*
- FFA represents any proposed change (e.g., adopting an energy-management programme) as a state between Driving Forces (pressures favouring change) and Restraining Forces (obstacles resisting it); change occurs only when driving forces exceed restraining forces.
- It forces planners to identify all stakeholders' incentives and barriers systematically before implementation, so policy measures can strengthen drivers (incentives, awareness, regulations) and weaken restrainers (cost, skills gap, inertia) instead of blindly pushing harder.
- It provides a visual, communicable basis for prioritising actions, budgeting, and monitoring progress toward energy-efficiency and conservation targets.

*SMART goal definition*: A SMART goal is one that is **S**pecific (clearly defined objective), **M**easurable (quantified indicator), **A**chievable (attainable with available resources), **R**elevant (aligned with organisational/policy aims), and **T**ime-bound (fixed deadline).

*Example goal*: "Reduce specific electricity consumption of the plant's compressed-air system by 10% (kWh/m³ of air delivered) within 12 months."

*Force Field Analysis for this goal*

| Driving forces (for the change) | Restraining forces (against the change) |
|---|---|
| Rising electricity tariffs raise operating cost | Capital cost of leak-repair/new compressors |
| Energy conservation regulations & audit requirements | Lack of trained maintenance staff |
| Management commitment to cost reduction | Production downtime needed for retrofitting |
| Proven payback of leak-sealing & pressure optimisation | Operator resistance to new practices |
| Availability of VFDs and efficient compressors | Fear that reduced pressure affects output |

Planning implication: strengthen drivers (tariff-awareness campaigns, management support, quick-payback leak repairs first) and reduce restrainers (training, phased retrofits during scheduled shutdowns, operator involvement).

**Final answer**: FFA helps energy policy/planning by listing driving and restraining forces around a proposed change so planners can tip the balance toward adoption; a SMART goal is one that is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound; for the example goal "reduce compressed-air specific energy by 10% in 12 months", driving forces include rising tariffs, regulation, management support and proven payback, while restraining forces include capital cost, skills gaps, downtime, resistance and output concerns — each addressed through training, phasing and incentives.

**Verification**: All four asked elements are present — role of FFA in energy policy/planning, SMART definition (all five attributes expanded), a concrete example goal, and a performed FFA (tabulated driving vs restraining forces plus planning response). The tabulated analysis directly matches the stated goal; no data invented; depth suits the (05) weighting.

### 5A — Efficiency test on a 37 kW squirrel cage induction motor

**Source wording**

> An efficiency assessment test was carried out for a standard 4 pole squirrel cage induction motor in a process plant. The motor specifications are as under:-
>
> Motor rated specification = 37 kW, 415 Volt, 62 Amps, 1480 rpm, 3 phase, delta connected
>
> The following data was collected during the no load test on the motor.
> Voltage= 415 Volts; Current = 18 Amps ;Frequency = 50 Hz;
> Stator resistance per phase =0.275 Ohms at 30°C; No load power = 1164 Watts
>
> Calculate the following:
> a) Iron plus friction and windage losses.
> b) Stator resistance at 120°C.
> c) Stator copper loss at full load at operating temperature of 120°C.
> d) Full load slip and rotor input assuming rotor losses are slip times rotor input.
> e) Motor input
> f) Motor full load efficiency and full load power factor. (06)

**Topic:** Induction motor loss segregation from a no-load test; temperature correction of stator resistance; slip, rotor input, efficiency and power factor.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

- a) No-load stator copper loss = 3 × 18² × 0.275 = 267.3 W; iron + friction and windage = 1164 − 267.3 = **896.7 W**.
- b) Copper winding: R₁₂₀ = 0.275 × (235 + 120)/(235 + 30) = 0.275 × 355/265 = **0.3684 Ω per phase**.
- c) Full-load stator copper loss = 3 × 62² × 0.3684 = **≈ 4248 W (≈ 4.25 kW)**.
- d) Ns = (120 × 50)/4 = 1500 rpm; slip s = (1500 − 1480)/1500 = **0.0133 (1.33%)**; rotor input = output/(1 − s) = 37/(1 − 0.0133) = **37.5 kW**.
- e) Rotor loss = s × rotor input = 0.0133 × 37.5 kW ≈ 0.5 kW; motor input = 37,000 + 896.7 + 4248 + 500 ≈ **42,645 W (≈ 42.64 kW)**.
- f) Efficiency η = 37,000/42,645 ≈ **86.8%**; power factor = 42,645/(√3 × 415 × 62) = 42,645/44,566 ≈ **0.957 lagging**.

**Final answer:** a) 896.7 W; b) 0.3684 Ω/phase at 120°C; c) ≈ 4.25 kW; d) slip = 1.33%, rotor input = 37.5 kW; e) ≈ 42.64 kW; f) η ≈ 86.8%, pf ≈ 0.957 lagging.

**Verification:** a) 267.3 + 896.7 = 1164 W equals the recorded no-load power. b) Reverse conversion 0.3684 × 265/355 returns 0.275 Ω at 30°C. c) 3 × 62² × 0.275 = 3171.3 W at 30°C; scaled by 355/265 gives 4248 W. d) 37.5 kW × (1 − 0.0133) = 37 kW rated shaft output, with rotor loss 500 W = slip × rotor input as assumed. e) Losses sum 896.7 + 4248 + 500 = 5648 W, giving input ≈ 42,648 W, matching item (e). f) √3 × 415 × 62 × 0.957 ≈ 42,650 W equals computed input, and 0.868 × 42,645 ≈ 37,000 W output.

### 5B — Percentage loading by the slip method

**Source wording**

> In a 75 kW four pole induction motor operating at 49.8 Hz and rated for 415 V and 1440 RPM, the actual measured speed is 1470 RPM. Find out the percentage loading of the motor if the voltage applied is 428 V. (02)

**Topic:** Estimation of motor loading from measured slip with voltage correction.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**

Rated synchronous speed = 1500 rpm; rated slip = (1500 − 1440)/1500 = 0.04. Synchronous speed at 49.8 Hz = (120 × 49.8)/4 = 1494 rpm; operating slip = (1494 − 1470)/1494 = 0.0161. % loading = (0.0161/0.04) × (415/428)² × 100 = 0.4025 × 0.9402 × 100 ≈ **37.8%**.

**Final answer:** ≈ 37.8% loading.

**Verification:** Uncorrected slip-ratio estimate = 0.0161/0.04 = 40.2%; multiplying by the voltage correction factor (415/428)² = 0.9402 gives 40.2 × 0.9402 ≈ 37.8%. Cross-check: 1494 rpm is consistent with a 4-pole machine at 49.8 Hz, and the measured slip of 24 rpm is well below the rated slip of 60 rpm, confirming partial load.

### 6A

**Source wording:**
> A community has 500 people. The source of water to the community are bore wells & supply of water from bore well is through hand pumps. Six hand pumps are used to meet the water requirement. Using the following details, compute the cost of water per liter if the project life cycle is 20 years. (Assume no inflation)
> -   Per capita water consumption – 40 lit/day
> -   Cost of each hand pump – INR 5000
> -   Depth of bore well – 20 m
> -   Cost of digging the bore well – INR 250 per meter.
> -   Life span of a hand pump = 10 years
> -   Annual Maintenance Cost – INR 1250/- per hand pump
> -   Rate of interest = 10%                                                                       (05)

**Topic:** Life-cycle costing / cost of water per litre for a community water-supply system.

**Worked solution - not an official answer**
Assumptions: one bore well serves all six hand pumps; all six pumps are replaced once at year 10; no salvage value; costs discounted at 10% p.a.

1. Water demand: 500 × 40 = 20,000 lit/day → 20,000 × 365 = 7,300,000 lit/year → 7,300,000 × 20 = 146,000,000 lit over project life.
2. Initial capital cost: bore well = 20 m × INR 250/m = INR 5,000; hand pumps = 6 × INR 5,000 = INR 30,000; total = INR 35,000.
3. Present value (PV) of pump replacement at year 10: 30,000 / (1.10)^10 = 30,000 / 2.59374 = INR 11,566.3.
4. Annual maintenance: 6 × INR 1,250 = INR 7,500/year; PV = 7,500 × [1 − (1.10)^−20]/0.10 = 7,500 × 8.51356 = INR 63,851.7.
5. Total life-cycle PV cost = 35,000 + 11,566.3 + 63,851.7 = INR 110,418.0.
6. Capital recovery factor (10%, 20 y) = 0.10 × (1.10)^20 / [(1.10)^20 − 1] = 0.11746; equivalent annual cost (EAC) = 110,418 × 0.11746 = INR 12,969.7/year.
7. Cost of water = 12,969.7 / 7,300,000 = INR 0.00178 per litre (≈ 0.18 paise/litre).

**Final answer:** Cost of water ≈ **INR 0.0018 per litre** (≈ 0.18 paise/litre), based on an equivalent annual cost of ≈ INR 12,970/year against 7.3 million litres/year.
