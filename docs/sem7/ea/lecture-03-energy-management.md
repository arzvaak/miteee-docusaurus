## Slides 1–10

### Slide 1 — Title: Energy Monitoring and Targeting

Lecture topic: **Energy Monitoring and Targeting**. The motivating maxim given on the slide is *"you cannot manage what you do not measure"* — i.e., measurement is the precondition for managing energy use. [Source: slide 1]

### Slide 2 — What Energy Monitoring and Targeting Is

- It essentially combines the **principles of energy use** and **statistics**.
- It is primarily a **management technique** that uses **energy information** as a basis to:
  - **eliminate waste**,
  - **reduce** and **control** current level of energy use, and
  - **improve** the existing operating procedures. [Source: slide 2]

> Exam trap: the slide defines M&T as a *management* technique built on energy-use principles + statistics — not merely meter reading. All four verbs (eliminate, reduce/control, improve) belong together in exam answers.

### Slide 3 — A Rationale for Monitoring, Targeting and Reporting (Part 1)

Monitoring, targeting and reporting lets you know:

1. whether your **current energy** is **better** or **worse** than before;
2. **trends in energy consumption** that reflect **seasonal**, weekly, and other operational parameters;
3. how much your **future energy use** is likely to vary if you **change** aspects of your **business**;
4. **specific areas** of wasted energy. [Source: slide 3]

### Slide 4 — A Rationale for Monitoring, Targeting and Reporting (Part 2)

Continuing the rationale, it also gives you:

5. **comparison** with other businesses with **similar characteristics** — this "**benchmarking**" process provides valuable indications of the effectiveness of your operations as well as energy use;
6. how much your business has **reacted to changes** in the past;
7. how to **develop performance targets** for an energy management program. [Source: slide 4]

Note that slide 4 introduces benchmarking as the comparison-with-similar-businesses idea, which slide 7 then defines formally.

### Slide 6 — Key Steps in Energy M&T

Six steps, in the deck's stated sequence:

| Step | Meaning (as worded on slide) |
|---|---|
| **Analyzing** | correlating energy consumption to a measured output, such as production quantity |
| **Comparing** | comparing energy consumption to an appropriate standard or benchmark |
| **Setting Targets** | setting targets to reduce or control energy consumption |
| **Monitoring** | comparing energy consumption to the set target on a regular basis |
| **Reporting** | reporting the results including any variances from the targets which have been set |
| **Controlling** | implementing management measures to correct any variances which have occurred |

[Source: slide 6]

> Exam traps:
> - Do **not** merge *Comparing* (vs. a standard/benchmark) with *Monitoring* (vs. the set target, regularly) — the reference point differs.
> - *Analyzing* specifically means correlation against a **measured output** (e.g., production quantity).
> - *Controlling* is corrective action on **variances**, not target-setting.

### Slide 7 — Adopt Benchmarking

Working definition: to **evaluate (something) by comparison with a standard**.

Formal definition quoted on the slide: "**Benchmarking** is the process of **identifying**, **understanding**, **adopting**, **outstanding practices** and **processes** from **organizations anywhere** in the world to help **your organization improve** its performance." [Source: slide 7]

> Exam trap: recall the keyword chain — identify → understand → adopt outstanding practices/processes, sourced from organizations *anywhere*, aimed at improving *your organization's* performance.

### Slide 8 — Energy Monitoring Requires Quantitative Information

Three required inputs:

1. **Energy billings data** — including electrical demand and consumption, fuel consumption, costs.
2. **Consumption measurements** at some level (for example, the entire building, a production department, an individual energy-consuming system).
3. Key **independent variables that influence energy consumption**, such as **production** of a manufacturing system.

In essence: energy monitoring is the technique of quantitatively **relating consumption information** to the **critical independent variables**. [Source: slide 8]

> Exam trap: bills alone are insufficient — the independent variables (drivers like production) are what let you relate consumption to activity quantitatively.

### Slide 9 — CuSum (Cumulative Sum of Differences) Analysis

- **CUSUM** = Cumulative sum of differences.
- It is a powerful technique for developing **management information regarding the energy performance** of:
  - a plant, or
  - an energy-consuming system such as an **oven or furnace**, for example.
- It **distinguishes between significant events affecting performance — faults or improvements — and noise**. [Source: slide 9]

> Exam trap: expand CUSUM correctly (*Cumulative sum of differences*) and state its diagnostic role: separating significant events (faults/improvements) from noise.

## Slides 11–20

### Slide 11 — CuSum Analysis

**CUSUM** stands for **'CUmulative SUM of differences'**, where 'difference' refers to the difference between the **actual consumption** and the **consumption you expect** on the basis of **some established pattern** — what we have called the **energy performance model**. CuSum represents the difference between **expected and actual consumption points over a baseline period**. [Source: slide 11]

- It is a useful technique: it not only provides a trendline, it also **calculates savings/losses to date** and **shows when the performance changes**.
- If consumption continues to follow the established pattern, the cumulative sum of these differences over time, **CUSUM, stays near zero**.
- Once a **change in pattern** occurs — due to the presence of a **fault** or to some **improvement** in the process being monitored — the distribution of the differences about zero becomes **less symmetrical**, and their cumulative sum, **CUSUM, increases or decreases with time**.

Formula note (formalizing the slide's definition):

$$\mathrm{CUSUM}_t=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\left(E_{\text{actual},i}-E_{\text{expected},i}\right)$$

> Exam traps:
> - Near-zero CUSUM ⇒ consumption still follows the energy performance model; a sustained drift up or down ⇒ the pattern has changed (fault **or** improvement — either direction of drift is possible).
> - CUSUM offers more than a trendline: it quantifies **savings/losses to date** and dates **when performance changed**.

### Slide 13 — Targeting

Properties of a target, as stated on the slide:

- It is a statement of what **management wishes to achieve**.
- It is determined from a **position of knowledge**.
- It must **challenge the organization** but be **achievable**.
- It conveys **management priorities**.
- It has **two essential components**: **an amount** and **a time limit**. [Source: slide 13]

> Exam trap: quote *both* essential components — an amount **and** a time limit. Also keep the balance in mind: challenging **yet** achievable.

### Slide 14 — Target Setting and Performance Variability (Theoretical and Equipment Levels)

When monitoring a **site**, a **system**, or an **individual piece of equipment**, there are **four levels of energy use** that can be considered. The slide defines the first two:

- **Theoretical kWh/Tonne** — the energy required when the **optimum amount of equipment** is operating at **design efficiency**.
- **Equipment kWh/Tonne** — the energy consumed by the equipment working at its **actual current efficiency**. [Source: slide 14]

> Exam trap: the Theoretical level assumes both optimum equipment count **and design efficiency**; the Equipment level substitutes **actual current efficiency** — the distinguishing factor between the two is design vs current efficiency.

### Slide 15 — Target Setting and Performance Variability (System and Actual Levels)

The remaining two levels:

- **System kWh/Tonne** — the energy required when the **operator and machine influences** are included — this takes into account **operational techniques** and **maintenance practices**.
- **Actual kWh/Tonne** — the energy use taking into account any **responses of the operators and supervisors** to variations and external influences, **and the time lag in responding**. [Source: slide 15]

Recap of the four levels (wording per slides 14–15):

| Level | Definition (as given) |
|---|---|
| Theoretical kWh/Tonne | energy required when the optimum amount of equipment operates at **design efficiency** |
| Equipment kWh/Tonne | energy consumed by equipment working at **actual current efficiency** |
| System kWh/Tonne | energy required when **operator and machine influences** are included (operational techniques, maintenance practices) |
| Actual kWh/Tonne | energy use including **operators'/supervisors' responses** to variations and external influences, and the **time lag in responding** |

> Exam trap: *Equipment* isolates machine efficiency; *System* adds operator + maintenance influences; *Actual* further layers in human responses **and response time lag**.

### Slide 16 — From Monitoring to a Realistic Reduction Target

The monitoring of **actual energy consumption** reveals the **actual consumption levels** and the **variability** that characterizes them. This information, together with insight into the potential **system and equipment improvements**, leads to a **realistic, achievable reduction target**. [Source: slide 16]

### Slide 17 — Data and Information Analysis

**Electricity bills and other fuel bills** should be **collected periodically** and analyzed as below:

- **Graphing the data** — to know **what drives energy consumption**: **weather or demand**.
- **Use of bar chart**. [Source: slide 17]

### Slide 18 — Steps for CUSUM Analysis

In the deck's stated sequence:

1. Plot the **Energy – Production graph** for the first X months — **before** the system was changed or modified.
2. Draw the **best fit straight line**.
3. Derive the **equation of the line** — straight line / regression analysis.
4. Calculate the **expected energy consumption** based on the equation.
5. Calculate the **difference** between actual and calculated energy use.
6. Compute **CUSUM** (the cumulative sum of those differences). [Source: slide 18]

> Exam trap: the baseline line is fitted to **pre-change data only** ("before the system was changed or modified"); the equation is then applied to the whole period to expose the change.

### Slide 19 — Worked Example (Part 1): Baseline Model by Regression

Problem (as worded): Energy consumption and production data were collected for a plant over a period of **18 months**. During **month 9**, a **heat recovery system** was installed. Using the plant monthly data, **estimate the savings made with the heat recovery system**. [Source: slide 19]

| Month | Monthly Energy Use (KWH) | Productions (tonnes) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 340 | 380 |
| 2 | 340 | 440 |
| 3 | 380 | 460 |
| 4 | 380 | 520 |
| 5 | 300 | 320 |
| 6 | 400 | 520 |
| 7 | 280 | 240 |
| 8 | 424 | 620 |
| **9** | **420** | **600** |
| 10 | 400 | 560 |
| 11 | 360 | 440 |
| 12 | 320 | 360 |
| 13 | 340 | 420 |
| 14 | 372 | 480 |
| 15 | 380 | 540 |
| 16 | 280 | 280 |
| 17 | 280 | 260 |
| 18 | 380 | 500 |

Method on the slide: plot the **Production (X) / Energy (Y) graph for the first 9 months** and apply **regression analysis** to obtain slope and intercept:

$$Y = b + mX$$

$$b=\frac{\left(\sum y\right)\left(\sum x^{2}\right)-\left(\sum x\right)\left(\sum xy\right)}{n\left(\sum x^{2}\right)-\left(\sum x\right)^{2}},\qquad m=\frac{n\left(\sum xy\right)-\left(\sum x\right)\left(\sum y\right)}{n\left(\sum x^{2}\right)-\left(\sum x\right)^{2}}$$

Regression worksheet for the nine pre-change observations:

| N | X = Production | Y = Energy | XY | X² | Y² |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 240 | 280 | 67200 | 57600 | 78400 |
| 2 | 320 | 300 | 96000 | 102400 | 90000 |
| 3 | 380 | 340 | 129200 | 144400 | 115600 |
| 4 | 440 | 340 | 149600 | 193600 | 115600 |
| 5 | 460 | 380 | 174800 | 211600 | 144400 |
| 6 | 520 | 380 | 197600 | 270400 | 144400 |
| 7 | 520 | 400 | 208000 | 270400 | 160000 |
| 8 | 600 | 420 | 252000 | 360000 | 176400 |
| 9 | 620 | 424 | 262880 | 384400 | 179776 |
| **Σ** | **4100** | **3264** | **1537280** | **1994800** | **1204576** |

Fitted model (slide's result): **Energy = 0.4 × Production + 182**

Arithmetic check from the column totals with $n=9$: $m=\frac{9(1537280)-(4100)(3264)}{9(1994800)-(4100)^{2}}=\frac{453120}{1143200}\approx 0.396\approx 0.4$ and $b=\frac{(3264)(1994800)-(4100)(1537280)}{1143200}\approx 182$, consistent with the slide's rounded model.

> Notes/traps:
> - In the worksheet, **N** indexes the nine observations **sorted by increasing production** (240 → 620 tonnes); it is a row number, not the calendar month (e.g., N = 1 is month 7).
> - Symbol roles: $X$ = production (tonnes/month), $Y$ = energy (kWh/month); slope $m$ carries **kWh/tonne**, intercept $b$ = 182 kWh is the production-independent term.

### Slide 20 — Worked Example (Part 2): CUSUM Computation and Savings

Applying $E = 0.4 \times P + 182$ to every month, tabulating Actual − Predicted, and accumulating:

| Month | Actual (Monthly Energy Use, KWH) | P (Productions, tonnes) | Predicted ($E = 0.4P+182$) | Actual − Predicted | CUSUM |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 340 | 380 | 334 | 6 | 6 |
| 2 | 340 | 440 | 358 | -18 | -12 |
| 3 | 380 | 460 | 366 | 14 | 2 |
| 4 | 380 | 520 | 390 | -10 | -8 |
| 5 | 300 | 320 | 310 | -10 | -18 |
| 6 | 400 | 520 | 390 | 10 | -8 |
| 7 | 280 | 240 | 278 | 2 | -6 |
| 8 | 424 | 620 | 430 | -6 | -12 |
| 9 | 420 | 600 | 422 | -2 | -14 |
| 10 | 400 | 560 | 406 | -6 | -20 |
| 11 | 360 | 440 | 358 | 2 | -18 |
| 12 | 320 | 360 | 326 | -6 | -24 |
| 13 | 340 | 420 | 350 | -10 | -34 |
| 14 | 372 | 480 | 374 | -2 | -36 |
| 15 | 380 | 540 | 398 | -18 | -54 |
| 16 | 280 | 280 | 294 | -14 | -68 |
| 17 | 280 | 260 | 286 | -6 | -74 |
| 18 | 380 | 500 | 382 | -2 | -76 |

Conclusion (slide's wording): **"A cumulative saving of 76 KWH is achieved after heat recovery system was installed."** [Source: slide 20]

Sample row (month 15): predicted $=0.4\times 540+182=398$ kWh; deviation $=380-398=-18$ kWh; CUSUM $=-36+(-18)=-54$ kWh.

> Sign-convention trap: in this table **actual below predicted gives negative deviations**, and the downward drift of CUSUM to **−76 kWh** is read as a **saving of 76 kWh**. Note also that deviations for **all 18 months** are measured against the **pre-change (months 1–9) model**, which is what lets the post-installation effect accumulate in the CUSUM.
