### Slide 5 — Investment Appraisal

- **Definition:** it is the *analysis done to consider the profitability of an investment over the life of an asset*, alongside considerations of **affordability** and **strategic fit**. [Source: slide 5]
- The **energy manager** has to identify how cost savings arising from energy management could be **redeployed within the organization to the maximum effect**. [Source: slide 5]

### Slide 6 — Financial Analysis Techniques: Simple Payback

- **Simple Payback** — a measure of *how long it will be before the investment makes money, and how long the financing term needs to be*. [Source: slide 6]
- **Simple Payback Period (SPP)** represents, as a **first approximation**, the **time (number of years) required to recover the initial investment (First Cost), considering only the Net Annual Saving**. [Source: slide 6]

Formula implied by the definition and the slide-7 worked example:

$$\mathrm{SPP} = \frac{\text{First Cost}}{\text{Net Annual Saving}}$$

### Slide 7 — Simple Payback: Worked Example

**Example:** a **continuous Deodorizer** costs **Rs. 60 lakhs** to purchase and install, **Rs. 1.5 lakhs/year** on average to operate and maintain, and is expected to **save Rs. 20 lakhs** by reducing steam consumption (as compared to batch deodorizers). [Source: slide 7]

**Answer (as printed on the slide):**

$$\mathrm{SSP} = 60/(20 - 1.5) = 3\ \text{years}\ 3\ \text{months}$$

- Check: $60 / 18.5 \approx 3.24$ years $\approx$ 3 years 3 months. (1 lakh = 100,000.)
- **Trap:** use the **net** annual saving ($20 - 1.5 = 18.5$ lakhs/yr), not the gross saving; $60/20 = 3.0$ years is *not* the slide's method.
- Minor source quirk: the answer line writes "SSP" where slide 6 defines the abbreviation as "SPP". [Source: slides 6–7]

### Slide 8 — Simple Payback: Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages** [Source: slide 8]
- **Simple to implement.**
- Suitable for projects generating substantial cash inflows in **earlier years (within the payback period)**; it *discriminates (not suitable)* against projects bringing substantial cash inflows in **later years (beyond the payback period)** but not in earlier years.

**Limitations** [Source: slide 8]
- Does **not consider the time value of money**.
- **Ignores cash flows beyond the payback period** — this leads to discrimination against projects that generate substantial cash inflows in later years.

### Slide 9 — Time Value of Money

Worked illustration (all figures from the slide): [Source: slide 9]

- Deposited amount in bank = **100/-**; interest = **10% p.a.** → after 1 year worth = **110/-**.
- Thus, **110/- in one year is the future value equivalent to 100/- present value**.
- Conversely, **100/- received one year from now is only worth 90.91/- in today's money**: $90.91 + 10\%\ \text{interest} = 100/-$, i.e. $100/1.10 = 90.91$.
- **90.91 represents the present value of Rs. 100 cash flow occurring one year in the future.**

Symbol note: the discounting relation shown by the numbers is $PV = FV/(1+i)$ with $i = 0.10$. The slide also displays a "**Net Present Value =**" label, but the equation content itself is **not legible/extractable in the source**; only the time-value arithmetic above is available. [Source: slide 9]

**Trap:** don't reverse the direction — 100 grows *to* 110 (compounding), while a future 100 discounts *back* to 90.91 (present value). [Source: slide 9]

### Slide 10 — Return on Investment (ROI)

- **ROI expresses the "annual return" from the project as a percentage of capital cost.** [Source: slide 10]
- The annual return takes into account the cash flows over the project life and the discount rate, by **converting the total present value of ongoing cash flows to an equivalent annual amount over the life of the project**, which can then be compared to the capital cost. [Source: slide 10]
- **ROI does not require similar project life or capital cost for comparison** (unlike some other techniques). [Source: slide 10]
- The slide displays an "**ROI =**" label, but the equation content is **not legible/extractable in the source**. [Source: slide 10]
- Rules from the slide: **ROI must always be higher than the cost of money (interest rate)**; the greater the return on investment, the better the investment. [Source: slide 10]

## Slides 11–20

### Slide 11 — ROI Limitations (continued) and Introduction to NPV

[Source: slide 11]

Continuing from the previous slide, the limitations of the **ROI (Return on Investment)** criterion:

- It does **not** consider the time value of money.
- It does **not** consider the variable nature of annual net cash inflows.

> **Common trap:** these two limitations close out the **ROI** discussion carried over from the previous slide — they are *not* payback limitations. The slide then moves to the next method in the source's numbering: "3) Net Present Value (NPV)".

**3) Net Present Value (NPV)** — The NPV of a project is equal to the sum of the present values of *all* the cash flows associated with it:

$$\text{NPV} = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1+k)^t}$$

Symbol notes (as defined on the slide):

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\text{NPV}$ | Net Present Value |
| $CF_t$ | Cash flow occurring at the end of year $t$, where $t = 0, 1, 2 \ldots n$ |
| $n$ | The project (its life/horizon) |
| $k$ | The discount rate |

### Slide 12 — NPV Worked Example

[Source: slide 12] (content carried by the attached image; slide text itself is only "Continue…")

To illustrate the calculation of net present value, consider a project with the following cash flow stream (amounts in Rs.):

| Investment | Rs. (1,000,000) |
|---|---|
| **Saving in Year** | **Cash flow** |
| 1 | 200,000 |
| 2 | 200,000 |
| 3 | 300,000 |
| 4 | 300,000 |
| 5 | 350,000 |

The cost of capital, $\kappa$, for the firm is **10 per cent**. The net present value of the proposal is:

$$\text{NPV} = -\frac{1{,}000{,}000}{(1.10)^0} + \frac{200{,}000}{(1.10)^1} + \frac{200{,}000}{(1.10)^2} + \frac{300{,}000}{(1.10)^3} + \frac{300{,}000}{(1.10)^4} + \frac{350{,}000}{(1.10)^5} = (5{,}273)$$

- Parentheses $(5{,}273)$ denote a **negative** NPV.
- The net present value represents the net benefit **over and above the compensation for time and risk**.
- Decision rule (source wording): *"Accept the project if the net present value is positive and reject the project if the net present value is negative."*

> **Common trap:** the undiscounted savings total Rs. 1,350,000, which exceeds the Rs. 1,000,000 investment, yet the NPV is still negative at $k = 10\%$. Ignoring the time value of money would give the wrong accept/reject signal here.

### Slide 13 — Advantages of NPV

[Source: slide 13]

The net present value criterion has considerable merits:

- Considers the **time value of money**.
- Considers the **cash flow stream in its project life** (the entire stream, not a truncated payback period).

### Slide 14 — Internal Rate of Return (IRR): Definition

[Source: slide 14]

**4) Internal Rate of Return (IRR):**

- The discount rate at which the current system has the same net present cost.
- It calculates the **rate of return** that the investment is expected to yield.
- The expected rate of return is the interest rate for which **total discounted benefits become just equal to total discounted costs** — i.e., net present benefits (or net annual benefits) equal **zero**, or the **benefit/cost ratio equals one**.
- Mechanics per the slide: discount the invested money with a *common* interest rate, then discount each year's incoming benefits with that same common rate, and add them so that NPV becomes zero — that common rate of interest is the IRR.
- Sign convention (emphasised in the source, in capitals): **CASH FLOW WILL BE −VE FOR EXPENDITURE AND +VE FOR SAVING.**

### Slide 15 — IRR Worked Example: Setup

[Source: slide 15] (content carried by the attached image)

Key framing statements from the image:

- $CF_t$ will be **negative if it is expenditure** and **positive if it is savings**.
- In NPV, the discount rate (cost of capital) is assumed **known** and the NPV is determined; in IRR, NPV is set **equal to zero** and the discount rate that satisfies this condition is determined.

Example cash flows of a project:

| Year | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash flow | (100,000) | 30,000 | 30,000 | 40,000 | 45,000 |

The IRR is the value of "$\kappa$" satisfying:

$$100{,}000 = \frac{30{,}000}{(1+\kappa)^1} + \frac{30{,}000}{(1+\kappa)^2} + \frac{40{,}000}{(1+\kappa)^3} + \frac{45{,}000}{(1+\kappa)^4}$$

The calculation of "$k$" involves **trial and error**: try different values of $k$ until the right-hand side equals 100,000. The first trial is $k = 15\%$.

### Slide 16 — IRR Worked Example: Trial-and-Error Solution

[Source: slide 16] (content carried by the attached image)

At $k = 15\%$:

$$\frac{30{,}000}{(1.15)} + \frac{30{,}000}{(1.15)^2} + \frac{40{,}000}{(1.15)^3} + \frac{45{,}000}{(1.15)^4} = 100{,}802$$

This is slightly **higher** than the target value of 100,000, so $k$ is increased from 15% to 16%. (General rule from the slide: a **higher $k$ lowers** and a **smaller $k$ increases** the right-hand side value.) At $k = 16\%$:

$$\frac{30{,}000}{(1.16)} + \frac{30{,}000}{(1.16)^2} + \frac{40{,}000}{(1.16)^3} + \frac{45{,}000}{(1.16)^4} = 98{,}641$$

Since 98,641 is now **less than** 100,000, the value of $k$ (the IRR) **lies between 15% and 16%**. Per the source: *"For most of the purposes this indication suffices."*

> **Common trap:** the IRR here is *not* exactly 15% or 16% — the slide only brackets it. Do not report a single exact rate from these slides.

### Slide 17 — IRR: Advantages and Limitations

[Source: slide 17]

**Advantages:**

- Considers the time value of money.
- Considers the cash flow stream **in its entirety**.
- Makes sense to businessmen who prefer to think in terms of **rate of return** and find an absolute quantity, like net present value, somewhat difficult to work with.

**Limitations:**

- The IRR figure **cannot distinguish between lending and borrowing**, and hence a high internal rate of return **need not necessarily be a desirable feature**.

### Slide 18 — Cash Flows: Kinds and Types

[Source: slide 18]

**Broadly two kinds:**

1. The initial investment.
2. The savings arising from the investment.

**Other types:**

- **Capital cost** (one-time cost) — design, planning, installations, commissioning.
- **Annual cash flows** — taxes, insurance, equipment leases, energy cost, servicing cost, maintenance, operating labour, etc.
- **Asset depreciation.**
- **Intermittent cash flows** — e.g., relining of boiler once in every 5 years. (Source's parenthetical: relining means repairing the **refractory lining** inside a boiler; this lining is a protective layer, typically made of heat-resistant materials like firebrick or castable refractory.)

### Slide 19 — Financing Options

[Source: slide 19]

A project may be financed:

1. From the **central budget**.
2. From a **specific departmental or section budget**, such as engineering.
3. By obtaining a **bank loan**.
4. By raising money from the **stock market**.
5. By awarding the project to an **Energy Service Company (ESCO)**.

### Slide 20 — Energy Performance Contracting and the Role of ESCOs

[Source: slide 20]

- **ESCOs** — usually companies that provide a **complete energy project service**: from assessment to design to construction or installation, along with engineering and project management services, **and financing**.
- **Contract** — involves the **capitalization of all of the services and goods purchased**, and **repayment out of the energy savings** that result from the project.
- **Performance contracting** — an **end-user** (such as an industry, institution, or utility) seeking to improve its energy efficiency **contracts with an ESCO** for energy efficiency services and financing.

## Slides 21–22

### Slide 21 — What is Performance Contracting? (continued; image slide)

Slide 21's native text is only a "Continue…" marker; its substantive content is the attached slide image, which is legible and transcribed/interpreted below. [Source: slide 21]

- **Definition:** The *core of performance contracting* is an **agreement involving a comprehensive package of services provided by an ESCO**, including:
  1. An energy efficiency opportunity analysis
  2. Project development
  3. Engineering
  4. Financing
  5. Construction/Implementation
  6. Training
  7. Monitoring and verification
- **Monitoring and verification (M&V) is key** to the successful involvement of an ESCO in performance contracting **where energy cost savings are being guaranteed** — guaranteed savings only mean something if they can be measured.
- **ESCOs are not "bankers" in the narrow sense.** Their strength is in putting together a *package of services* that can provide **guaranteed and measurable energy savings** that serve as the basis for **guaranteed cost savings**. But the **energy savings must be measurable**.
- The image text states that "Figure 6.1 shows ESCO Role." The figure itself is **not reproduced on this slide** (it is only referenced); the ESCO role is elaborated on the next slide.

**Exam trap:** Do not describe an ESCO as merely a lender/banker. Per the source, financing is only one element of a bundled service package, and the guarantee rests on *measurable* energy savings. [Source: slide 21]

### Slide 22 — Role of Energy Service Company

- **Role of ESCO:**
  - **Overcoming barriers to investment**
  - **Incentive for investment**
  - **Need for up-front cost is eliminated** (the end-user does not fund the project up front)
  - Performance contracting through an ESCO **transfers the technology and management risks away from the end-user to the ESCO**
  - **Savings are used to payback investment** (the project pays for itself out of realized savings)
- **Performance contract includes:**
  - An energy efficiency opportunity analysis
  - Project development
  - Engineering
  - Financing
  - Construction/Implementation
  - Training
  - Monitoring and verification

[Source: slide 22]

**Notes and traps:**
- The seven-item performance-contract scope on slide 22 matches the package listed on the slide 21 image — learn it as one canonical list (analysis → development → engineering → financing → construction → training → M&V). [Source: slides 21–22]
- Risk transfer is **from end-user to ESCO** (not the reverse), covering both **technology risk** and **management risk**. [Source: slide 22]
- Payback mechanism: **savings pay back the investment**, which is why eliminating the up-front cost is possible. [Source: slide 22]

<details>
<summary>Q2. In what sense are ESCOs not "bankers," and why is monitoring and verification central to their role?</summary>

ESCOs are not "bankers" in the narrow sense: their strength is bundling a package of services that delivers **guaranteed and measurable energy savings**, which form the basis for guaranteed cost savings — financing alone is not the value proposition. Monitoring and verification is key precisely because the energy cost savings are guaranteed: the savings **must be measurable** for the guarantee to be meaningful. [Source: slide 21]
</details>

<details>
<summary>Q3. How does performance contracting through an ESCO address investment barriers for the end-user?</summary>

It (a) overcomes barriers to investment and creates an incentive for investment, (b) eliminates the need for up-front cost, (c) transfers the **technology and management risks** away from the end-user to the ESCO, and (d) uses the resulting **savings to pay back the investment**. [Source: slide 22]
</details>
