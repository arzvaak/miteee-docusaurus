### Slide 2 — Energy Audit: Working Definition and Scope

An **energy audit** is "a depth study to determine how and where the energy is being utilized and converted from one form to another." Per the slide, it includes:

- Systematic and deep study of **production of energy**.
- **Total cost calculation** in production of energy.
- Emphasis on **where and what percentage of energy** is utilized in any equipment.
- **Estimation of how much energy gets wasted**.
- How to **increase efficient use of energy by reducing the cost of production**.

The slide also carries an accompanying image: a dark-blue banner graphic captioned **"Less energy use = More savings"**, showing three icons — a **downward arrow beside a lightning bolt**, a **white house**, and a **money bag marked with a dollar sign** — with an **"© EnergySage"** attribution in the corner. It is illustrative only and contributes no technical data.

[Source: slide 2]

> **Exam note:** This informal definition stresses *quantification* (percentages, waste estimation) — distinguish it from the statutory definition on the next slide.

### Slide 3 — Statutory Definition (Energy Conservation Act 2001)

As per the **Energy Conservation Act 2001**, "Energy Audit" is defined as:

> "a **verification, monitoring and analysis of use of energy** including submission of **technical report containing recommendation for improving energy efficiency with cost benefit analysis**."

[Source: slide 3]

> **Common trap:** The statutory definition has *four* examinable elements — verification, monitoring, analysis, and a technical report with recommendations **plus cost–benefit analysis**. Omitting "cost benefit analysis" loses marks.

### Slide 4 — Need for Energy Audit

The slide justifies "what is the real need of Energy Audit?" with five points:

1. To **minimise the cost of energy production**.
2. To **minimise the operational cost**.
3. To **minimise the cost of maintenance**.
4. **Quality Control**.
5. **Increase the efficiency of the plant** by reducing the overall cost and increasing profit.

The accompanying image is a photograph of an illuminated industrial/refinery complex (storage tanks, distillation columns, pipework) at dusk — illustrative of an industrial energy consumer; it contains no data.

### Slide 8 — Primary and Secondary Energy Sources; the toe Conversion Factor

The slide's flowchart (legible) traces four fuel chains from **PRIMARY ENERGY** to **SECONDARY ENERGY**:

- **Extraction and Treatment** stage: Gas Well, Coal Mine, Crude Oil Well, Uranium Mine.
- **Processing** stage: Treatment (gas), Washing (coal), Refining (crude oil → **LPG, Petrol, Diesel, Petro-chemical**), Enrichment (uranium).
- Processed fuels: **Natural Gas**, **Coal**, refined oil products, enriched uranium.
- Conversion devices: **Steam boiler** and **Power plant** (gas and coal routes), **Power plant** (uranium route).
- Outputs (**SECONDARY ENERGY**): **Steam** and **Electricity** (from gas and coal), **Electricity** (from uranium).

**Unit definition (memorize):** primary energy content of fuels is expressed in **toe (tonne of oil equivalent)**, based on:

$$
1\ \text{toe} = 1 \times 10^{7}\ \text{kcal} = 11630\ \text{kWh} = 41868\ \text{MJ}
$$

[Source: slide 8]

> **Formula note:** The three stated values are mutually consistent: $11630\ \text{kWh} \approx 11.63\ \text{MWh}$, and $11630\ \text{kWh} \times 3.6\ \text{MJ/kWh} \approx 41868\ \text{MJ}$.

### Slide 9 — toe Equivalents and Power-Plant Efficiency

Facts and conversions as given:

- **11.63 MWh** of energy is equivalent to burning **1 ton of crude oil**.
- This number **varies with the type of oil composition** (qualification).
- **1 barrel of oil equivalent (boe)** contains approximately **0.136 toe**.
- **1 ton diesel = 1.01 toe**
- **1 ton petrol = 1.05 toe**
- **39%** thermal-to-electrical conversion efficiency in thermal power plants.

[Source: slide 9]

> **Common traps:**
> - A **boe is much smaller than a toe** (≈ 0.136) — a barrel is a volume unit; don't assume 1 boe ≈ 1 toe.
> - Diesel (1.01 toe/t) and petrol (1.05 toe/t) are **per tonne**, and both exceed 1 toe.
> - The **39%** figure is thermal → electrical; the remaining ~61% is not converted to electricity.

### Slide 10 — Primary → Secondary Energy via Transformation (Diagram)

Slide 10 is image-only; the diagram (legible) shows:

- **Primary Energy** inputs, grouped as:
  - *Crude Oil, Hard Coal, Natural Gas Liquids, Natural Gas, Nuclear Energy, etc.*
  - *Waste*
  - *Wind, Geothermal, Biomass, Hydroelectricity, Wave and Tidal Energy, etc.*
- All flows pass through a central **Transformation** stage.
- **Secondary Energy** outputs:
  - *Petroleum Products, Manufactured Solid Fuels and Gases, etc.*
  - *Electricity and Heat*
  - *Biofuels, etc.*
- All outputs lead **"To Consumption."**

This reinforces slide 7's classification: primary (original fuels) → transformation → secondary (converted carriers) → consumption.

[Source: slide 10]

### Slide 16 — Reserve/Production Ratio (RPR) for India

- **Definition:** Remaining amount of a source of energy **expressed in time**. If the reserves remaining at the end of the year are divided by the production in that year, the result is the length of time the remaining reserves would last **if production continued at that level**. [Source: slide 16]

$$\text{RPR} = \frac{\text{amount of known remaining reserves}}{\text{amount used per year}} \quad [\text{years}]$$

- **India figures (memorise):**
  - India is **second in consuming coal (first: China)** — main use: **Industries**.
  - **RPR for coal (as of 2024): ≈ 120 years**.
  - **RPR for oil: 14 years** (India holds **0.3% of world** reserves; **80% imported**) — main use: **Transport**.
  - **RPR for natural gas: 43.6 years** (**0.6% of world**, **80% import**) — main uses: **Transport (CNG) / Domestic (Piped NG)**. [Source: slide 16]

> **Trap:** RPR divides reserves by *production* (amount used per year), and its unit is **years**. Also note the paired facts: tiny reserve share + heavy import dependence → very low oil RPR.

### Slide 18 — Formation of Oil

Image-only slide; the three-panel diagram *(image-derived; pending attachment confirmation)* states: [Source: slide 18]

1. **300–400 million years ago:** Tiny sea plants and animals died and were **buried on the ocean floor**, covered over time by **layers of silt and sand**.
2. **50–100 million years ago:** Over millions of years the remains were buried **deeper and deeper**; **enormous heat and pressure** turned them into **oil and gas**.
3. **Today:** We **drill down through layers of sand, silt and rock** to reach the rock formations that contain **oil and gas deposits**.

> **Trap:** Two distinct time stamps appear: burial of marine organisms (300–400 million years ago) vs. transformation into oil/gas under heat and pressure (50–100 million years ago). Oil originates from **sea (marine) organisms**, not land plants.

### Slide 22 — Straight line equation

$$Y = mX + b$$

where **X** is the independent variable, **m** is the slope, **b** is the intercept, and **Y** is the dependent variable. The slide also recalls the two-point form $y_2 - y_1 = m(x_2 - x_1)$. [Source: slide 22]

- The attached figure shows a straight line crossing the **y-axis at b** with the label **"slope = m"**; the drawn line falls left-to-right, illustrating a *negative* slope case. [Source: slide 22]
- **Symbol note:** in forecasting, X = year, Y = consumption; m = annual trend, b = the value where the fitted line meets the Y-axis. [Source: slide 22]

## Slides 31–39

## Energy Scenario — Part IV: Intensity, Indian Outlook, Pricing & Security

This section completes the *Energy Scenario* module (L01), covering national-level energy metrics, India's long-term outlook, tariff mechanisms, energy security, and the conservation-vs-efficiency distinction.

### Slide 31 — Energy Intensity

At the **national level**, energy intensity is defined as [Source: slide 31]:

$$\text{Energy Intensity} = \frac{\text{Total final consumption (toe)}}{\text{Gross domestic product (US\$)}}$$

**Symbol/unit notes:** numerator is final energy consumption in *toe* (tonnes of oil equivalent); denominator is GDP in US dollars.

Key interpretations given on the slide:
- A **low energy intensity** indicates the country has the **right mix** of industries *sector-wise*.
- A country relying on **trade to acquire (import) carbon-intensive goods** will — when all other factors are equal — have **lower energy intensity than countries manufacturing the same goods for export**.
- Energy use generally rises as the economy grows, but **continuing improvement in energy efficiency** and a shift to **less energy-intensive activities** are projected to keep the **rate of energy consumption growth lower than the rate of GDP growth**.

> **Exam trap:** Low intensity is not proof of superior efficiency alone — import dependence on carbon-intensive goods structurally depresses the ratio (slide 31 explicitly qualifies this with "when all other factors are equal").

### Slide 32 — Long Term Energy Scenario for India

All points as stated [Source: slide 32]:
- **Coal imports** are expected to **increase**.
- The **gap between electricity supply and demand continues to increase**, impacting industrial output and economic growth.
- About **90 %** of India's total **oil demand** would have to be met by **imports**.
- **Natural gas demand** in India has been on the **increase**.
- With India already under **peak demand and energy shortage**, increasing economic growth is expected to put heavy pressure on the power sector.
- For sustaining the current economic growth rate, **capacity will have to be doubled every 10 years**.
- Targets toward commercial viability of utilities by reducing **Aggregate Technical & Commercial (AT&C) losses to 15 %**.
- Improvement in **quality, supply and reliability of supply**; improved **revenue collection and customer satisfaction**.

> **Memory hooks:** 90 % oil imported · capacity doubling every decade · AT&C target 15 % — these three figures are classic one-mark exam items.

### Slide 33 — Energy Pricing

[Source: slide 33]
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** — indicates **economic productivity and standard of living**; glossed on the slide as "[Same price for same goods across the world]".
- In India, **power tariffs for industrial and commercial establishments are highest in the world** — due to **cross subsidies**.
- **State Electricity Regulatory Commissions (SERCs)** decide the tariff for billing.
- Billing basis differs by consumer class: **High Tension (HT) consumers are billed on kVA and kWh; Low Tension (LT) consumers are billed on kWh only**.
- **Farmers** receive almost **free power for agriculture** — leading to **exploitation of ground water** and **loss of revenue**.

> **Exam trap:** Don't conflate the billing quantities — the kVA (demand + energy) charge applies to HT consumers only; LT is kWh-only. Also remember *who sets tariffs* (SERCs), not the utilities themselves.

### Slide 34 — Availability Based Tariffs (ABT): Concept

[Source: slide 34]
- **Availability** = the **readiness of the generating station to deliver energy**.
- Rationale: the **cost of storing electricity is more than its production cost**, so generation is essentially **just-in-time production** meeting **unpredictable demand on a minute-to-minute basis**.
- **ABT** = a **pricing mechanism applicable in India for unscheduled electric power transactions**.
- It applies **incentives and dis-incentives to grid participants against deviations in their commitment**.
- Effect: ABT brings about **enhanced grid discipline** and promotes **competition and efficiency**.

### Slide 35 — Availability Based Tariffs (ABT): Applicability and Obligations

[Source: slide 35]
- ABT applies to the **supply of electricity by generators owned and controlled by the central government**.
- It requires **both generators and beneficiaries to commit to a day-ahead schedule**.
- It ensures **prompt payment of dues**; **non-payment of prescribed charges is liable for appropriate action**.

> **Common trap:** ABT governs *unscheduled* transactions and binds *both sides* (generator **and** beneficiary); quoting only one party or calling it a scheduled-transaction tariff loses marks.

### Slide 36 — Energy Security

[Source: slide 36]

**Definition (quote exactly):** Energy security is *"The continuous availability of energy in varied forms in sufficient quantities at reasonable prices."*

- For any country, energy security means **reducing dependency on imported energy sources** for its **economic growth**.
- Chain of logic on the slide: **energy efficiency initiatives → energy imports reduced → conserving limited resources → improved energy security**.

> **Definition checklist (4 elements):** continuity, varied forms, sufficient quantity, reasonable price — omitting any element weakens a full-mark definition answer.

### Slide 37 — Strategies for Better National Energy Security

Strategies listed [Source: slide 37]:
1. **Building** energy supplies.
2. **Diversification** of energy supply sources — the **energy mix**; includes development of **renewable energy sources**.
3. Increased capacity of **fuel switching**.
4. **Controlling and managing demand**.
5. **Energy efficiency** — implementing **minimum energy performance standards**.
6. **Sustainable development** — long-term benefits.
7. Efficient **generation** of energy resources.
8. **Privatization** of the energy sector — including **reducing cross subsidies on oil products and electricity tariffs**.

> **Grouping tip for recall:** Supply-side measures (building supplies, diversification/renewables, fuel switching, efficient generation, privatization) vs. demand-side measures (demand control, efficiency standards) — exam answers often ask for two examples of each type of grouping implicit here.

### Slide 38 — Benefits from Energy Efficiency Programs

Energy efficiency benefits at three levels [Source: slide 38]:

| Level | Benefits listed |
|---|---|
| **Industry** | Reduced energy bills · Increased competitiveness · Increased productivity · Improved quality · Increased profits |
| **Nation** | Reduced energy imports · Avoided costs usable for poverty reduction · Conservation of limited resources · Improved energy security |
| **World** | Reduced GHG and other emissions · Maintains a suitable environment |

> **Exam trap:** "Improved energy security" appears under **Nation** (and connects back to slide 36), while emission reduction belongs to the **World** column — attributing benefits to the wrong level is a common error.

### Slide 39 — Energy Conservation vs. Energy Efficiency

The slide distinguishes the two terms [Source: slide 39]:
- **Energy Conservation:** the **need for energy is reduced, measured in physical terms**.
- **Energy Efficiency:** the **energy consumed in a specific process is reduced without affecting output quality**.

> **Classic distinction:** Conservation reduces *how much energy service you need*; efficiency delivers the *same output with less input*, with the explicit condition that **output quality is unaffected**. If output drops, it is conservation, not efficiency improvement.
