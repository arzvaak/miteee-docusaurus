# Energy Classification and Global Reserves

> [!important] ◆ MIDSEM SYLLABUS
> This chapter is inside the immediate midsem boundary.


Energy is the capacity to do work. To manage, trade, and utilize it effectively, we classify energy based on its origin, stage of processing, market availability, and exhaustibility. This classification is fundamental to energy economics, policy, and the transition to renewables.

## Primary vs. Secondary Energy

**Primary energy sources** are energy forms found in nature, requiring no conversion before use. They are the raw inputs to the global energy system: coal, crude oil, natural gas, nuclear fuel (uranium), wind, solar radiation, hydropower, and geothermal heat.

**Secondary energy sources** are energy carriers produced by converting primary sources into more convenient forms. The conversion always involves some energy loss. Examples include electricity (generated from any primary source), refined petroleum products (gasoline, diesel from crude oil), hydrogen (from electrolysis or reforming), and coke (from coal).

The key distinction: a primary source (coal) is burned to produce a secondary source (electricity). The coal is the origin; the electricity is the carrier.

## Commercial vs. Non-Commercial Energy

**Commercial energy** is traded in the market for a definite price: grid electricity, coal, LPG, petrol, diesel. It forms the backbone of industrial, agricultural, and transport sectors.

**Non-commercial energy** is not bought or sold in the formal market: firewood, cattle dung cakes, agricultural waste burned directly. In India, a significant portion of the rural population has historically relied on non-commercial energy for cooking. Government initiatives like Ujjwala Yojana (LPG connections) and solar home systems aim to transition this demand toward cleaner sources.

## Conventional vs. Non-Conventional (Renewable) Energy

**Conventional energy sources** are established, large-scale, depletable sources: coal, crude oil, natural gas, nuclear energy, and large hydropower. They are finite, carbon-intensive, well-established technologically, and dominate the current global energy mix.

**Non-conventional (renewable) energy sources** are generally inexhaustible alternatives: solar, wind, small hydropower, biomass gasification, tidal, geothermal, and wave energy. They are environmentally benign, often decentralized, suitable for rural electrification, and their costs are declining rapidly.

A useful rule of thumb: conventional = fossil fuels + nuclear + large hydro (established, depletable); non-conventional = renewables (solar, wind, small hydro, biomass, tidal, geothermal). Large hydro is often classified as conventional due to its established infrastructure and environmental impact, while small/micro hydro is non-conventional.

## Global Primary Energy Reserves

Proven reserves are quantities of energy sources that geological and engineering data demonstrate with reasonable certainty to be recoverable from known reservoirs under existing economic and operating conditions.

### Coal
- **Global proven reserve (end of 2020):** 1,156 billion tonnes
- **Top holders:** USA (25.4%), Russia (15.9%), China (11.6%), India (8.6%)

### Oil
- **Global proven reserve (end of 2016):** 1.65 trillion barrels (1 barrel approximately equals 160 litres)
- **Top holder:** Saudi Arabia (~23%)
- **Reserve-to-Production (R/P) ratio:** ~47 years. At the 2016 consumption rate, known reserves would last roughly 47 years. This is a dynamic metric that changes with new discoveries and consumption rates.

### Natural Gas
- **Global proven reserve (end of 2020):** 7,257 trillion cubic feet
- **Top holder:** Russian Federation (~27%)

## Global Energy Consumption and Distribution

Global primary energy consumption at the end of 2020 was equivalent to roughly 100.37 million barrels of oil per day. In India, natural gas's share in primary energy rose to 6.7% in 2020, while coal's share in power generation declined marginally from 74% to 72%.

A stark disparity exists between developed and developing nations:

| Metric | Developed Nations | Developing Nations | World Average |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Population share | ~20% | ~80% | — |
| Energy consumption share | ~60% | ~40% | — |
| Per capita consumption | 4–5 times world average | 1/9 of world average | 2.2 tonnes of coal equivalent |

An average American uses roughly 32 times more commercial energy than an average Indian.

## Worked Example: Reserve-to-Production Ratio

**Given:** Global proven oil reserve at end of 2016 = 1.65 × 10¹² barrels, R/P ratio = 46.6 years.

**Implied annual consumption:**

$P = R / (R/P) = (1.65 × 10^{12}) / 46.6 ≈ 35.4 × 10^9 "barrels/year"$

**Verification:**

$R/P = (1.65 × 10^{12}) / (35.4 × 10^9) ≈ 46.6 " years" ≈ 47 " years"$

At the 2016 consumption rate, the world had roughly 47 years of oil left from proven reserves.
