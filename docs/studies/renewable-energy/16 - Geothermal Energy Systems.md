# Geothermal Energy Systems

> [!note] ◇ AFTER MIDSEM
> This chapter is outside the immediate midsem boundary and is retained for the complete course.


## Earth's Internal Heat

Geothermal energy is the primary heat from Earth's interior. The Earth's structure comprises the crust (outermost solid shell), mantle (extending to ~2900 km depth), and core (innermost, with a solid inner core of radius ~1170 km).

The **geothermal gradient** averages about 0.03°C/m (30°C per km depth). Localized hot spots exist near magma or tectonic activity.

**Key reserve figures:** Total geothermal reserves within Earth: $4 times 10^24$ MJ. Renewable portion in the upper 3 km of crust: $4 times 10^15$ MJ. Average heat flux through crust: 0.06 W/m².

## Classification of Geothermal Resources

| Type | Description | Typical Temperature |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hydrothermal | Both heat and fluid (water/steam); most common | > 150°C |
| Geo-pressured | Pressurized hot water at ~3500 m with dissolved methane | 150–200°C |
| Hot Dry Rock (HDR) | Hot but dry rock; requires artificial fracturing | > 150°C |
| Magma | Directly harnesses molten rock heat; experimental | > 600°C |

Hydrothermal resources are subdivided: **vapour-dominated (dry steam)** fields produce saturated steam up to ~350°C; **liquid-dominated (wet steam)** fields contain pressurized hot water above 100°C.

## Dry Steam Power Plant

The simplest type, used for vapour-dominated resources.

**Working:**
1. Steam extracted directly from reservoir via production well
2. Passes through centrifugal separator to remove particulates
3. Cleaned dry steam fed to steam turbine
4. Turbine drives electrical generator
5. Exhaust steam enters condenser; non-condensable gases (CO₂, H₂S, NH₃) removed by steam ejector or vacuum pump
6. Condensate reinjected to maintain reservoir pressure

**Typical operating conditions:** ~165°C, ~7 atm, plant efficiency ~14%.

## Flash Steam Power Plant

Used for liquid-dominated resources with reservoir temperature above 180°C.

**Working:**
1. Hot water (brine) extracted at high pressure (e.g., 40 bar)
2. Pressure reduced at wellhead (throttling); water flashes to steam
3. Mixture enters flash chamber/separator; steam rises, brine settles
4. Separated steam drives turbine-generator
5. Hot brine and condensate reinjected

**Limitations:** Requires large mass flow rates; ground subsidence risk; mineral scaling from brine.

## Binary Cycle Power Plant

Enables power generation from lower-temperature resources (100–150°C).

**Working:**
1. Hot geothermal water pumped through heat exchanger
2. Transfers heat to secondary working fluid (isobutane, isopentane) with lower boiling point
3. Secondary fluid vaporizes and drives turbine
4. Spent vapour condensed and pumped back (closed loop)
5. Geothermal water reinjected

**Key characteristics:** Geothermal fluid never contacts turbine (minimal corrosion/scaling); enables lower-temperature resources. First installed in USSR (1967, 680 kW). Notable: 11 MW in California, 100 MW at Raft River, USA.

## Comparison of Power Plant Types

| Feature | Dry Steam | Flash Steam | Binary Cycle |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Resource type | Vapour-dominated | Liquid-dominated (high T) | Liquid-dominated (low T) |
| Working fluid | Geothermal steam | Geothermal steam (flashed) | Secondary organic fluid |
| Typical resource T | > 240°C | > 180°C | 100–150°C |
| Key component | Steam separator | Flash chamber | Heat exchanger |
| Emissions | Direct NCG release | Direct NCG release | Closed loop; minimal |
| Corrosion/scaling | Moderate | High | Low (fluid isolated) |

## Environmental Considerations

- **Air emissions:** NCGs including CO₂, CH₄, NH₃, H₂S. Per-kWh emissions much lower than fossil fuels. H₂S is toxic.
- **Water contamination:** Effluent brine can be saline with dissolved minerals (sodium, potassium, lithium, boron, arsenic).
- **Land subsidence:** Large-scale fluid extraction can cause surface sinking.
- **Induced seismicity:** Re-injection or hydraulic fracturing can trigger minor earthquakes.
- **Noise pollution:** From drilling and plant operations.

**Primary mitigation:** Re-injection of spent geothermal fluids maintains reservoir pressure, reduces brine disposal, and minimizes subsidence.

## Geothermal Energy in India

**Potential:** 10,600 MW estimated across ~350 hot spring sites in 11 states.

**Seven geothermal provinces:**
1. Puga Valley, Ladakh (Himalayas)
2. Sohana (Rajasthan, Haryana)
3. West Coast (Maharashtra-Goa)
4. Cambay (Gujarat)
5. SONATA Belt (Son, Narmada, Tapi)
6. Godavari Belt
7. Mahanadi Belt

**Status:** No large-scale operational power plants. Puga Valley (5 MW pilot) under development. Current uses include fisheries and crop drying.

## Advantages and Disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| :--- | :--- |
| Renewable, sustainable | Geographically limited to tectonically active regions |
| High capacity factor (24/7) | Low conversion efficiency (10–12%) |
| Low GHG emissions | High initial drilling/exploration cost |
| Small land footprint per MW | Resource depletion risk if not managed |
| Negligible fuel cost | Corrosive/abrasive fluids reduce equipment life |
| Capital cost 40–60% less than thermal/nuclear | Induced seismicity and subsidence risk |

## Applications Beyond Electricity

Space heating/cooling; industrial process heating (paper, plastic manufacturing); agriculture (crop drying, greenhouses, mushroom culture); aquaculture; timber seasoning; salt production; desalination; sewage treatment.
